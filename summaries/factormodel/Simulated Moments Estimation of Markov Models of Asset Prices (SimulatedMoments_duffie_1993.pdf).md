# Simulated Moments Estimation of Markov Models of Asset Prices

Darrell Duffie and Kenneth J. Singleton. *Econometrica* 61(4), July 1993, pp. 929–952.

## Why this matters

A dynamic asset-pricing model can be easy to simulate yet hard to estimate. Equilibrium prices may be numerical functions of latent states, preference shocks can invalidate an observable Euler-equation formulation, and observed aggregates may combine several model periods. In these settings an analytical map from structural parameters to observable population moments may not exist. Simulated moments replace that map with moments of a synthetic history generated by the model.

The paper's central contribution is to make this estimator rigorous for Markov time series. Two obstacles distinguish the problem from ordinary GMM or independent-observation simulation estimators. First, the simulator is usually initialized at an arbitrary state, not a draw from the model's unknown invariant distribution. Second, a parameter change alters every transition in the simulated history. Continuity of a one-period observation function does not by itself control the accumulated effect of changing that history.

Duffie and Singleton give weak- and strong-consistency conditions, alternative routes through ergodicity and contraction, and an asymptotic variance that explicitly includes simulation error. The practical payoff is an estimation method for models whose population moments are computationally inaccessible, with a transparent cost for approximating them by simulation. The article provides theory and an illustrative economy, not a fitted empirical application or Monte Carlo performance table.

## The structural model and the object being matched

The general state recursion is

$$
Y_{t+1}=H(Y_t,\varepsilon_{t+1},\beta_0),
$$

where $Y_t\in\mathbb R^N$, the innovations are independent and identically distributed, and $\beta_0$ is a $Q$-dimensional parameter in a compact set $\Theta$. The transition can be explicit or obtained from a numerical equilibrium solution. Let

$$
Z_t=(Y_t,Y_{t-1},\ldots,Y_{t-l+1})
$$

contain a finite state history. An $M$-vector $f(Z_t,\beta_0)$ supplies the observed moment variables, with $M\geq Q$. The notation permits components such as levels, products, growth rates, and lagged cross-products; selecting them is part of the application, not a universally prescribed vector in the paper.

Construct an independent innovation sequence $\widetilde\varepsilon_t$ with the same law as the structural shocks. For each candidate parameter, start from a specified state and generate

$$
Y_{s+1}^{\beta}=H(Y_s^{\beta},\widetilde\varepsilon_{s+1},\beta),
\qquad f_s^\beta=f(Z_s^\beta,\beta).
$$

The artificial innovations are independent of the historical innovations. Across parameter evaluations, the formal construction uses the same underlying simulated innovation sequence. This common-random-number coupling makes pathwise comparisons meaningful. Redrawing shocks at every optimizer evaluation changes the numerical objective into a noisy moving target and is not the smooth construction used in the differentiability assumptions.

For $T$ historical observations and $S=S(T)$ simulated observations, define

$$
G_T(\beta)=\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^T f_t^*
-\frac1S\sum_{s=1}^S f_s^\beta,
\qquad f_t^*=f(Z_t,\beta_0).
$$

The simulated moments estimator minimizes

$$
\widehat\beta_T\in\arg\min_{\beta\in\Theta}
G_T(\beta)'W_TG_T(\beta).
$$

$W_T$ is positive semidefinite with rank at least $Q$. It can depend on the historical and simulated data. The limiting criterion must uniquely identify $\beta_0$: matching the selected stationary moments cannot leave a second structural parameter with the same criterion value. Having at least as many moments as parameters is necessary for the stated local rank condition but is not sufficient for either local or global identification.

A historical moment variable is treated as observed in the baseline construction. This does not require every state component to be observed: the model can map latent states into observed prices and quantities. It does require the chosen historical moment to be constructible. If its construction itself depends on the trial parameter, Section 6 supplies an extension rather than silently treating it as fixed data.

## The illustrative production economy

Section 2 uses a representative-consumer growth economy to expose the estimation issues. Output is $z_tk_t^\phi$, with $0<\phi<1$, capital $k_t$, and productivity $z_t$. A competitive firm rents capital at rate $r_t^k$ and distributes its profit as dividends. Its first-order condition gives $r_t^k=\phi z_tk_t^{\phi-1}$ and hence

$$
d_t=(1-\phi)z_tk_t^\phi.
$$

The source's equation (2.2) writes the dividend as an “argmax”; economically it is the maximized profit value, while the argument is the capital demand. This distinction matters if the displayed equation is translated into software.

The consumer's budget is

$$
c_t+k_{t+1}+p_ts_{t+1}
=(d_t+p_t)s_t+(r_t^k+\mu)k_t,
$$

where $p_t$ is the share price, $s_t$ share holdings, and $1-\mu$ the depreciation rate. Preferences are described as discounted power utility with a multiplicative taste shock $u_t$. Productivity and taste shocks follow a joint Markov recursion $X_t=h(X_{t-1},\varepsilon_t,\rho)$, where $X_t=(z_t,u_t)'$. The structural parameter includes $\phi$, risk aversion, shock-law parameters, $\mu$, and the subjective discount factor $\delta$.

There is a conspicuous printing inconsistency in equation (2.4), p. 931: the displayed expression has $(c_t-1)^{1-\alpha}$ and states $\alpha<0$, while the text calls $\alpha$ relative risk aversion and later explicitly sets $\alpha=1$ for logarithmic utility. A reproducing researcher should not implement that line literally as standard CRRA. The conventional interpretation consistent with the later example is $u_t c_t^{1-\alpha}/(1-\alpha)$, up to an irrelevant additive normalization, with the logarithmic limit at $\alpha=1$. This is an explicit reconstruction, not a transcription of the printed expression.

Solving the equilibrium yields a transition for the augmented state $Y_t=(X_t',k_t)'$ and price and consumption functions. Estimation then compares the joint moments of model-implied quantities and returns with their historical counterparts. Taste shocks motivate this approach because they can make a simple observable Euler equation unavailable. Temporal aggregation can be incorporated by applying the same aggregation to simulated high-frequency quantities as to observed data, provided the underlying model and observation mapping are specified.

A tractable special case on p. 937 has no taste shocks, complete capital depreciation $\mu=0$, logarithmic utility, and

$$
\log z_{t+1}=\zeta_z+\rho\log z_t+\varepsilon_{t+1}.
$$

The stated solution is

$$
k_{t+1}=\delta\phi z_tk_t^\phi,
\qquad
p_t=\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}(1-\phi)z_tk_t^\phi.
$$

Thus the price–dividend ratio is $\delta/(1-\delta)$. This provides a useful analytical unit test for an equilibrium simulator. The paper gives no calibrated values of $\phi,\delta,\rho$, shock variance, sample size, or observed data for an estimation experiment. It would be misleading to convert this illustrative special case into a reported empirical result.

The one-step transition of the augmented state can be singular because tomorrow's capital is determined by today's state. That does not prevent states from being reachable over several steps. The example motivates the paper's multi-step accessibility condition instead of requiring a full-support one-step transition density for every coordinate.

## Initialization, ergodicity, and uniformity

For a transition law $P^t_x$ and invariant law $\pi$, geometric ergodicity means that, for some $r<1$, $r^{-t}\|P_x^t-\pi\|_{TV}\to0$ for every initial state, under the source's definition. The important economic consequence is loss of dependence on an arbitrary simulation starting point. The relevant moment averages can converge to invariant expectations without having been initialized exactly in the invariant law.

The paper reviews a sufficient route based on accessibility, aperiodicity, and a drift bound. Condition B requires that every positive-measure region be reached with probability bounded away from zero from a compact set after some finite number of steps. Aperiodicity excludes deterministic alternation between disconnected timing classes. For sufficiently large states the transition must contract in an $L^q$ sense:

$$
\|H(y,\varepsilon,\beta)\|_q\leq r\|y\|,
\qquad \|y\|>K,\quad r<1.
$$

Continuity into $L^q$ and the other conditions of Lemma 1 accompany this inequality. A drift calculation alone is not the entire theorem. Also, moment bounds on the state do not automatically establish moment bounds on an arbitrarily fast-growing observation function. The application must check the actual $f$ used in estimation.

Pointwise ergodicity is insufficient for optimization over $\Theta$. The weak-law route requires continuity of the invariant moment map and a parameter Lipschitz bound

$$
\|f_t^\beta-f_t^\theta\|\leq K_t\|\beta-\theta\|,
\qquad \frac1T\sum_{t=1}^TK_t=O_p(1).
$$

This bound concerns the total parameter effect, including the simulated state history. It is stronger than checking that $f(z,\beta)$ is smooth while holding $z$ fixed. Together with pointwise laws of large numbers and compactness, it gives uniform convergence in probability of simulated moment averages.

The proof idea is a finite parameter cover. At cover centers the ordinary law of large numbers applies. Within each neighborhood the Lipschitz bound controls the sample moment movement, and continuity controls the population movement. First bound the probability that the average Lipschitz coefficient exceeds a large constant; then choose a sufficiently fine cover; finally increase the sample length. This order of choices explains why tightness, rather than convergence of the coefficient to a deterministic number, is sufficient.

The appendix on p. 949 states that boundedness in probability yields a deterministic bounded sequence approximating the random coefficient in probability. That implication is not valid in general. The tightness-and-cover argument just described supplies the needed uniform weak-law reasoning without that assertion. Likewise, the prose before Theorem 1 says that the criterion converges almost surely, whereas the theorem and its displayed proof establish convergence in probability under the weak-law assumptions. This note preserves the theorem's weaker, justified mode of convergence.

Under geometric ergodicity, uniform parameter continuity, suitable $2+\eta$ moments, convergence of the weight matrix, and unique identification, Theorem 1 gives $\widehat\beta_T\to_p\beta_0$. Simulation length must tend to infinity. An arbitrary but fixed finite simulated history generally leaves a random approximation error that does not vanish merely because the historical sample grows.

## Strong consistency and contraction conditions

The alternative route controls propagation of perturbations path by path. Around each parameter $\theta$, the asymptotic unit-circle condition bounds state differences by a random Lipschitz coefficient $p_\theta(\varepsilon_t)$ with

$$
\|H(y,\varepsilon_t,\beta)-H(x,\varepsilon_t,\beta)\|
\leq p_\theta(\varepsilon_t)\|y-x\|,
$$

$$
\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^T\log p_\theta(\varepsilon_t)<0
\quad\text{almost surely}.
$$

The coefficient need not be smaller than one at every date. Its product must decline geometrically on typical paths. Consequently, histories sharing shocks but starting from different states become close. A stationary causal version can be constructed by initializing further and further in the past and taking a limit. The proof requires the corresponding innovation/transition integrability controlling the forcing term $H(0,\varepsilon,\beta)$; an application should check that term, not only the coefficient multiplying state differences.

The observation function must be locally Lipschitz in state, and its parameter sensitivity must obey a linear-growth bound. The theorem also needs integrability of a local parameter modulus for the stationary simulated process. The source explicitly says this last assumption is not known to follow from the asymptotic unit-circle condition alone. Under these conditions, stationary and arbitrarily initialized moment averages are asymptotically equivalent uniformly in parameters, and Hansen's uniform strong law yields almost-sure consistency.

A stronger $L^2$ unit-circle condition is

$$
E[p_\theta(\varepsilon_t)^2]<1.
$$

Together with the stated smoothness and moment controls it makes the required local modulus integrable and provides a more readily checked sufficient route. It is stronger than a negative average log coefficient. The paper's claims about geometric ergodicity should still be read with the accessibility and distributional conditions of its Markov setup: contraction by itself does not guarantee convergence in total variation. For example, a deterministic contracting trajectory can approach its fixed point without ever assigning mass to it. The pathwise-coupling argument and the total-variation ergodicity argument should therefore not be conflated.

Section 4.5 explains why the two consistency routes are useful rather than redundant. A conditionally heteroskedastic transition can have sublinear innovation scale in the tails and a stable drift, yet have a large local derivative near a small positive variance floor. It can then satisfy a drift-based ergodicity argument without satisfying the uniform contraction condition. The authors explicitly do not prove the needed parameter-uniform Lipschitz property for their illustrative heteroskedastic process. Their example demonstrates a potential gap between assumptions, not a complete consistency theorem for every such specification.

## Asymptotic variance and the price of simulation

Let $\mu(\beta)=E[f_\infty^\beta]$ and define the $M\times Q$ total derivative $D=\partial\mu(\beta_0)/\partial\beta'$. It includes the change in the stationary state distribution induced by the parameter. The local rank requirement is that $D$ have full column rank. The paper also requires an interior solution, pathwise differentiability, and a uniform-law condition for the total derivative process.

Let the long-run covariance of the moment observations at the truth be

$$
\Sigma=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}
\operatorname{Cov}(f_t^*,f_{t-j}^*).
$$

With independent simulated and historical shocks, the same correctly specified law gives the same long-run covariance for both histories. If $T/S(T)\to\tau$, Theorem 4 gives

$$
\sqrt T G_T(\beta_0)\Rightarrow N(0,(1+\tau)\Sigma).
$$

The multiplier is $1+T/S$, not $1+S/T$. The historical average contributes $\Sigma$ on the $\sqrt T$ scale; the simulated average contributes $\tau\Sigma$. Their covariance is zero by construction. Serial correlation is already inside $\Sigma$ and cannot be replaced by an independent-observation variance without further assumptions.

For an efficient weight $W=\Sigma^{-1}$,

$$
\sqrt T(\widehat\beta_T-\beta_0)
\Rightarrow N\left(0,(1+\tau)(D'\Sigma^{-1}D)^{-1}\right).
$$

The standard error of the estimator itself divides this covariance by $T$ before taking square roots. Equal historical and simulated lengths double asymptotic variance, multiplying standard errors by $\sqrt2$. A simulation ten times as long adds 10% to variance, or about 4.9% to standard errors. These are arithmetic implications of the theorem, not reported experimental findings. As $S/T\to\infty$, simulation becomes asymptotically negligible relative to sampling error and the estimator approaches the moment estimator with analytically known expectations.

For clarity, the correct influence map under the convention $D=\partial\mu/\partial\beta'$ is

$$
\sqrt T(\widehat\beta_T-\beta_0)
=(D'WD)^{-1}D'W\sqrt TG_T(\beta_0)+o_p(1).
$$

The sentence preceding Theorem 4 on p. 944 omits the factor $D'W$ and has incompatible dimensions when $M>Q$. In addition, the derivative of $G_T$ tends to $-D$ under this convention. The representation here follows directly from the first-order condition; the paper's final covariance formula (5.5) is consistent with it.

Estimate $\Sigma$ using a consistent long-run covariance method, such as the spectral estimators referenced by the authors. A longer simulated history can potentially improve estimation of this covariance, but using an estimated parameter in that simulation requires a two-step argument. The paper raises this issue rather than establishing all implementation details for every such procedure. No HAC bandwidth or kernel is fixed in the source.

Numerically inspect the singular values or an orthogonalization of a simulated derivative matrix, as suggested on p. 946. Nearly collinear moment responses indicate weak local information about some parameter combinations. A stable optimizer output does not resolve this problem. The authors recommend sensitivity to the moment selection, especially when equilibrium prices are obtained by a black-box numerical solution.

## Extensions: parameter-dependent observations and partially analytical moments

Section 6 permits historical moments $g(Z_t,\beta)$ to depend directly on the trial parameter. Then

$$
G_T(\beta)=E_Tg_t^\beta-E_Sf_s^\beta,
$$

and the identifying Jacobian is the difference between the historical and simulated derivatives. For example, constructing a marginal rate of substitution from observed consumption requires a risk-aversion parameter. The simulated and historical transformations must use the same trial value, rather than estimating one side and freezing it accidentally.

Let $\Sigma_g$ and $\Sigma_f$ be the corresponding long-run covariance matrices at the truth. The covariance of $\sqrt TG_T$ is now

$$
\Omega=\Sigma_g+\tau\Sigma_f.
$$

These matrices need not coincide, so the simple scalar inflation rule need not hold. With efficient weighting the parameter covariance is $(J'\Omega^{-1}J)^{-1}$, where $J=E(\partial g/\partial\beta'-\partial f/\partial\beta')$. Both histories generally enter a feasible covariance estimate.

When some expectations are known analytically, replace their simulated coordinates by those exact functions. This removes unnecessary simulation noise, while other coordinates remain simulated. The full covariance matrix must reflect these changes, including cross-covariances; blindly multiplying the original covariance by $1+\tau$ would be incorrect.

Mean-zero measurement noise can be included in the historical moment observation without adding artificial measurement noise to the simulator. This works when the moment-level mean equality remains valid. It does not say that arbitrary nonlinear moments of mismeasured raw variables are unbiased. Likewise, the law of iterated expectations can replace a difficult model-implied conditional expectation, such as an option price, by an easier simulated discounted payoff with the same unconditional mean. That is a moment-matching device, not a pathwise claim that a realized payoff equals its price.

## Reconstruction protocol and remaining inputs

An implementation should document the equilibrium equations, state variables, shock law, and observation map before selecting an optimizer. It should solve the model at each parameter, simulate with a fixed shock stream, preserve the same sampling and aggregation conventions as the data, calculate the selected historical and simulated moments, and minimize their weighted discrepancy. A finite-history moment needs enough initial lags; dropping initial observations must be handled consistently when defining $S$.

The source does not choose a burn-in, seed, simulation length, numerical solver, equilibrium grid, finite-difference step, tolerance, moment vector, or empirical sample. Initial-state irrelevance is asymptotic, not evidence that a short simulation needs no burn-in assessment. Comparing starts and increasing simulation length are useful implementation checks, but they do not replace the stated ergodicity and uniformity requirements.

Approximate equilibrium solutions and discretized continuous-time models create a second error distinct from Monte Carlo noise. Footnote 2, p. 932, is explicit: with a fixed approximation, the estimator generally targets the approximate model. Consistency for the original model needs approximation error to disappear; preserving the same first-order limit typically requires moment approximation error to be negligible on the $T^{-1/2}$ scale, an implementation requirement derived from the estimating equation.

Finally, structural trends must be removed in a model-consistent way. Deterministic trend parameters can be estimated jointly through additional moment equations. Stochastic trends require a transformation making the whole model, including forcing variables, stationary in the relevant sense. Applying the stationary Markov theorem to raw growing capital or output without such a transformation changes the problem and loses the justification. The paper's durable lesson is that simulatability, identification, stability, and statistical precision are four separate requirements; a successful synthetic history establishes only the first.
