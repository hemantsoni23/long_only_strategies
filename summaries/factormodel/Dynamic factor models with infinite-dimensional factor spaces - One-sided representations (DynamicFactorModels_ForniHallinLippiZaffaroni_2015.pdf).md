# Dynamic factor models with infinite-dimensional factor spaces: One-sided representations

Mario Forni, Marc Hallin, Marco Lippi and Paolo Zaffaroni. *Journal of Econometrics* 185 (2015), 359–371. DOI: 10.1016/j.jeconom.2013.10.017. This note concerns the 2015 representation-theory article, distinct from the 2017 companion on asymptotic estimation and forecasting.

Source: [local PDF](../../Finance/DynamicFactorModels_ForniHallinLippiZaffaroni_2015.pdf), 13 pages. Page references below use journal pagination. The entire source, including its proof appendix, was inspected. The article contains no empirical tables or independently specified simulation experiment; its discussion of performance refers to a companion working paper.

## Why this matters

A small number of shocks need not imply a small number of contemporaneous factors. Different securities or macroeconomic series can respond to the same innovation with different persistence. A model with one primitive shock and hundreds of distinct autoregressive responses can therefore have an infinite-dimensional contemporaneous common-component space. Fitting a fixed number of ordinary principal components imposes a restriction on those responses, rather than merely exploiting the small number of shocks.

The paper solves a specific obstacle to using the more general model: dynamic principal components ordinarily produce two-sided filters, while forecasting and real-time shock extraction require filters using current and past observations. Under rational transfer functions and generic rank conditions, small blocks of common components possess finite autoregressive representations. Applying these block filters to observations turns the original dynamic factor problem into a static factor problem whose factors are the primitive innovations. The resulting transformation is one-sided even when no fixed-dimensional static representation of the original common components exists.

This is principally a representation result. It establishes the structure from population spectra and covariances and explains its sample analogue. It does not supply a finite-sample accuracy guarantee, automatic structural identification, or a complete empirical forecasting recipe. Those distinctions are necessary when deciding what the theorem permits an implementation to claim.

## Model and the meaning of “common”

Let $i=1,2,\ldots$ index a growing cross-section and $t\in\mathbb Z$ denote time. Observations are covariance-stationary, centered processes with spectral densities. The generalized dynamic factor model is

$$
x_{it}=\chi_{it}+\xi_{it},\qquad
\chi_{it}=\sum_{f=1}^{q}b_{if}(L)u_{ft},\qquad E(u_tu_t')=I_q.
$$

The $q$ shocks are orthonormal white noise. Initially the filters are square summable and need not have the eventual causal rational form. Common shocks are orthogonal to every idiosyncratic component at every lead and lag. The idiosyncratic terms may have serial dependence and cross-sectional correlation; they are not assumed independent across securities.

The identifying distinction is spectral pervasiveness. For the first $n$ series, let $\Sigma_n^x(\theta)$ be the spectral density and order its eigenvalues decreasingly. Assumption A.2 requires that the $q$th eigenvalue diverge as $n$ grows for almost every frequency, while the $(q+1)$st eigenvalue is bounded uniformly in $n$ and essentially uniformly in frequency. Theorem A, recalled from earlier work, gives a unique decomposition into a rank-$q$ common spectrum and an idiosyncratic spectrum whose largest eigenvalue remains bounded.

Thus “common” is not a name assigned to a regressor. It is an asymptotic cross-sectional property. A disturbance allowed in the idiosyncratic component may affect several variables, but its contribution cannot create another unbounded spectral eigenvalue. Bounded spectral eigenvalues also explain why sufficiently diversified weighted averages of the idiosyncratic terms vanish in mean square. No finite panel by itself proves this asymptotic property.

Uniqueness of $\chi$ and $\xi$ does not identify the labels of $u_t$. For any orthogonal $Q$, the product $b(L)u_t$ is unchanged by replacing it with $b(L)Q\,Q'u_t$. Before imposing fundamentalness, even frequency-dependent all-pass transformations can preserve the common spectrum. An estimated innovation must not be called a monetary, productivity, or demand shock without additional identifying restrictions.

## Why static dimension and dynamic rank differ

A conventional static representation takes the form

$$
x_{it}=\lambda_i'F_t+\xi_{it},\qquad F_t=N(L)u_t,
$$

where the number $r$ of contemporaneous factors is finite and $r\ge q$. In applications $F_t$ often follows a finite VAR driven by $q$ innovations. This is convenient because ordinary PCA estimates the span of $F_t$ directly. But its existence requires that the space generated by all $\chi_{it}$ at a fixed date have finite dimension.

The counterexample in equation (1.5), p. 360, is

$$
\chi_{it}=\frac{a_i}{1-\alpha_iL}u_t.
$$

With infinitely many distinct persistence coefficients $\alpha_i$, the response sequences $a_i(1,\alpha_i,\alpha_i^2,\ldots)$ do not lie in a fixed finite-dimensional space. Nevertheless there is only one primitive shock. If the persistence coefficients take only finitely many values, a finite static representation can return; the obstruction is heterogeneous dynamics of unbounded diversity, not simply the presence of an autoregressive filter.

A second example shows why dynamic and static notions of pervasiveness can disagree even when a finite algebraic factor representation exists:

$$
x_{1t}=u_t+a u_{t-1}+\xi_{1t},\qquad
x_{it}=u_t+\xi_{it}\quad(i>1).
$$

The dynamic common component of the first series includes the lagged shock. In a static eigenvalue classification, however, $u_{t-1}$ affects only one series at date $t$, so it is not a second pervasive contemporaneous factor. Static extraction can absorb $a u_{t-1}$ into the first series' residual and lose its impulse response. The dynamic approach recovers the response $1+aL$. The introduction briefly prints $1-aL$ while discussing this example; the model and the explicit construction on p. 363 require the plus sign.

For the first two common components, the causal transformation is transparent:

$$
\begin{pmatrix}1&-aL\\0&1\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}\chi_{1t}\\\chi_{2t}\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}u_t.
$$

It removes the lag using another series rather than asking a contemporaneous PCA to retain a nonpervasive lag direction.

## Rationality and recoverable innovations

The additional structure is a rational transfer function for each common-component response:

$$
b_{if}(L)=\frac{c_{if}(L)}{d_{if}(L)},\qquad
c_{if}(L)=\sum_{j=0}^{s_1}c_{if,j}L^j,\qquad
d_{if}(L)=1+\sum_{j=1}^{s_2}d_{if,j}L^j.
$$

The denominator roots lie outside the unit circle. Common degrees $s_1,s_2$ simplify the argument and provide a common order bound. Rationality excludes an arbitrary infinite filter but allows heterogeneous ARMA responses. It leaves the idiosyncratic dynamics largely unspecified, making this a semiparametric rather than fully specified Gaussian state-space model.

Let $\mathcal H_t^y$ be the closed linear space generated by all current and past coordinates of $y$. A representation $y_t=b(L)v_t$ is fundamental if the filter is one-sided and $v_t\in\mathcal H_t^y$. Fundamentalness means the innovations can be recovered from the observable history in the linear mean-square sense. It does not assert that each individual series can recover them alone.

For an infinite-dimensional process, rational spectra and finite rank are insufficient for a causal representation. The example $y_{it}=u_{t+i-1}$ exposes arbitrarily distant future innovations as the cross-section expands. There is no finite observed subvector whose past contains the whole panel's information. This example prevents treating finite dynamic rank as an automatic causal identification theorem.

Lemmas 1 and 2, pp. 363–364, establish the missing bridge. For a finite-rank process with rational spectra, existence of a one-sided rational representation is equivalent to existence of a finite subvector whose present and past span the full process's present and past. Under this condition there is a fundamental rational representation. Any two fundamental representations differ only by a constant orthogonal rotation, and their impact matrix has rank $q$.

The proof first uses rational spectral factorization on the informative finite subvector. Other coordinates are projections on its innovation history, and rationality carries over. Conversely, nonfundamental zeros can be removed through all-pass transformations; because a finite submatrix has finitely many zeros, finitely many transformations suffice. This yields a finite informative subvector. The result identifies a history space, not economic shock labels.

## The small-block autoregressive theorem

Take a block of exactly $q+1$ common components driven by $q$ shocks. Its spectrum is singular: it has more coordinates than innovations. Proposition 1, p. 365, says that, for generic coefficients in the specified rational parameterization,

$$
A_k(L)\chi_t^{(k)}=R_k u_t,\qquad
A_k(0)=I_{q+1},\qquad
\deg A_k\le S=qs_1+q^2s_2,
$$

where $R_k$ is $(q+1)\times q$ and has rank $q$. Within the bounded-order innovation representations covered by the proposition, the AR coefficients are unique; $R_k$ and the orthonormal shocks remain identifiable only up to rotation.

The choice $q+1$ is substantive. A square $q$-variable system need not have a finite autoregressive inverse. Adding one coordinate generically makes shock recovery possible with a finite polynomial filter. With more than $q+1$ coordinates, finite autoregressions can still exist, but redundant lag regressors can destroy uniqueness of a minimum-order coefficient matrix. The block construction uses just enough redundancy without introducing that ambiguity.

“Generic” refers to the chosen parameter space, not to all conceivable representations of a process. In finite dimension the excluded coefficient set is nowhere dense. For the countable panel, the union of excluded block configurations is meagre in the product topology. This is a topological statement. It does not supply a probability that an empirical block is well conditioned, a numerical lower bound on singular values, or robustness to being close to an exceptional set.

A useful two-variable example is

$$
y_{1t}=a_1v_t+b_1v_{t-1},\qquad
y_{2t}=a_2v_t+b_2v_{t-1},\qquad
\Delta=a_1b_2-a_2b_1.
$$

When $\Delta\ne0$,

$$
v_t=\frac{b_2y_{1t}-b_1y_{2t}}{\Delta},\qquad
y_t=C y_{t-1}+a v_t,
$$

with

$$
C=\frac{1}{\Delta}
\begin{pmatrix}b_1b_2&-b_1^2\\b_2^2&-b_1b_2\end{pmatrix},
\qquad a=(a_1,a_2)'.
$$

Each scalar MA can be noninvertible while the pair is jointly fundamental. Conversely, as $\Delta$ approaches zero the recovery coefficients become large. The same example explains both the theoretical gain from cross-sectional information and the finite-sample conditioning problem.

The appendix proves the order bound through polynomial relations among lagged observations. A linear dependence in a lag stack corresponds to a polynomial row vector annihilating the transfer matrix. Determinants and resultants show that, outside exceptional coefficient sets, no such relation exists below degree $S$. Clearing denominators turns the rational system into a finite MA. A square map from a sufficiently long stack of shocks to the corresponding observation stack can then be inverted, yielding the finite AR. Independence of the relevant lag stack gives uniqueness. These are algebraic arguments; they require neither a likelihood nor a Gaussian assumption.

## Constructing the filter from population covariances

Partition the panel into disjoint blocks of $q+1$ variables, assuming the number used is a multiple of $q+1$. Assumption A.3 requires every relevant block to share the full innovation-history space and to possess the unique bounded-order AR. It is stronger than merely saying that the entire panel has rank $q$.

From the common cross-spectrum, recover covariances using the convention

$$
\Gamma_{jk}^\chi(h)=E[\chi_t^{(j)}\chi_{t-h}^{(k)'}]
=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{ih\theta}\Sigma_{jk}^\chi(\theta)\,d\theta.
$$

For block $k$, define the lag stack $Y_{k,t-1}=(\chi_{t-1}^{(k)'},\ldots,\chi_{t-S}^{(k)'})'$, the cross-covariance $B_k=E[\chi_t^{(k)}Y_{k,t-1}']$, and the lag covariance $C_{kk}=E[Y_{k,t-1}Y_{k,t-1}']$. Then

$$
[A_{k,1}\ \cdots\ A_{k,S}]=B_kC_{kk}^{-1},\qquad
A_k(L)=I-\sum_{s=1}^SA_{k,s}L^s.
$$

This explicit covariance definition avoids ambiguity about Toeplitz lag signs. The innovation cross-covariance can be constructed most safely as

$$
\Gamma_{jk}^{\Psi}
=\sum_{a=0}^S\sum_{b=0}^S D_{j,a}\Gamma_{jk}^\chi(b-a)D_{k,b}',
\quad D_{k,0}=I,\quad D_{k,a}=-A_{k,a}\ (a>0).
$$

Its population rank is $q$. Factorizing the assembled matrix as $SS'$ recovers the stacked impact matrix up to an orthogonal rotation. Equation (4.16) in the PDF has a dimensionally problematic shortened cross-block covariance expression; the filter-sandwich formula above is the direct covariance identity and makes all transposes explicit. Likewise a loading rotation must be $RQ$, not $QR$, for an $n\times q$ loading matrix.

Assemble $A(L)=\operatorname{diag}(A_1(L),A_2(L),\ldots)$ and apply it to observations:

$$
Z_t=A(L)x_t=Ru_t+\Phi_t,\qquad \Phi_t=A(L)\xi_t.
$$

The factor part is now contemporaneous in the shocks. Filtering requires only current and past observations. The large problem has become a sequence of $(q+1)$-dimensional covariance solves followed by static factor extraction.

## Why normalization and renewed pervasiveness are necessary

Filtering weak idiosyncratic noise does not automatically leave it weak uniformly over the cross-section. Blocks can approach singularity, producing arbitrarily large coefficients in $A_k$. The two-variable determinant example demonstrates this directly. An assumption that each determinant is nonzero is weaker than a common positive lower bound.

The 2015 article takes the normalization route. Define $w_i=\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(Z_{it})}$ when the variance is positive, with $w_i=1$ otherwise, and $V=\operatorname{diag}(w_i^{-1})$. Set

$$
z_t=VZ_t=ru_t+\phi_t,\qquad r=VR,\qquad\phi_t=V\Phi_t.
$$

Assumption A.4 additionally requires the smallest eigenvalue of the original idiosyncratic spectral density to be bounded below by a positive constant $b_\xi$, uniformly in panel size and almost every frequency. Together with the existing upper bound $B_\xi$, Proposition 2 implies

$$
\lambda_{\max}(\operatorname{Var}(\phi_{nt}))
\le\frac{B_\xi}{b_\xi}(q+1).
$$

The proof bounds each block filter's energy using its own variance normalization, integrates over frequencies, and sums over only $q+1$ coordinates per block. The lower spectral bound prevents normalization from concealing directions with almost no original idiosyncratic energy. It is sufficient, not asserted necessary.

Normalization still does not guarantee strong transformed common factors. If impact coefficients shrink too quickly relative to filtered noise, the contemporaneous shocks need not remain pervasive after transformation. Assumption A.5 therefore separately requires the $q$th eigenvalue of $rr'$ to diverge. This is not deduced from divergence of the original common spectral eigenvalues. A correct implementation should monitor both the block conditioning and the transformed factor signal, rather than citing the original spectral gap as justification for everything downstream.

## Sample implementation and what remains unspecified

The source's proposed estimation sequence is: estimate the observed spectral density; retain its leading $q$ eigencomponents at each frequency to estimate the common spectrum; inverse-transform to common autocovariances; construct block AR filters; filter and normalize the observed panel; then extract $q$ ordinary principal components from the transformed data. The last PCA uses current transformed values, hence current and lagged original values.

A reproducible program would additionally record centering and scaling, frequency grid, spectral kernel and bandwidth, selected $q$, lag-order search and criterion, block ordering, treatment of incomplete final blocks, numerical conditioning thresholds, sample-start handling, and shock rotation. These choices are not fully supplied here. They belong to the companion estimation analysis or an explicitly documented implementation. In particular, two-sided spectral estimation of covariance parameters is not itself a two-sided real-time signal filter; however, a historical backtest must re-estimate those parameters using only the training sample available at each forecast origin.

For impulse responses, the conceptual reconstruction is $b(L)=A(L)^{-1}R$, with the normalization reversed as necessary. To forecast, future innovations have zero linear prediction and observed past innovations propagate through the response coefficients. Idiosyncratic predictability is a separate modeling issue: the paper permits serially dependent residuals, so forecasting only the common component is not automatically the optimal forecast of the complete observation.

The parameter comparison on p. 368 is informative but not a universal efficiency ranking. Block ARs and impacts require $[S(q+1)+q]n$ coefficients under the displayed counting convention. A static representation with $r$ loadings and a $p$-lag factor VAR requires $nr+pr^2+rq$. The dynamic structure can be more faithful yet still estimate unnecessary unrestricted block coefficients. For heterogeneous scalar AR responses it fits bivariate VARs even when a simpler diagonal structure generated the data.

## Scope, limitations and practical interpretation

There are no measured forecast gains, return estimates, confidence intervals, transaction costs, portfolio rules or tuning experiments in this article itself. Its qualitative performance discussion points to a companion working paper. The 2017 article is the appropriate separate source for rates, simulation settings and forecasting evidence. Transferring those numbers into the 2015 theorem would obscure the distinction between existence and estimator performance.

The most consequential restrictions are covariance stationarity, rational common responses, a fixed finite number of pervasive primitive shocks, sufficiently informative small blocks, and adequate transformed signal strength. Structural breaks, fractional filters, weak shocks, or near-collinear blocks can invalidate or destabilize the route from theory to computation. Genericity does not cure these problems in finite samples.

The method's useful conceptual lesson is narrower and stronger than a claim that dynamic PCA always wins: low dynamic rank should be modeled independently from low static rank. A large information set can restore shock recoverability that is absent in individual series, and a sequence of small causal inverse filters can exploit that information without fitting an unrestricted high-dimensional VAR. The payoff is preservation of heterogeneous impulse responses and end-of-sample usability. The cost is spectral estimation, block conditioning, additional regularity assumptions, and a structural-identification problem that remains external to the statistical decomposition.
