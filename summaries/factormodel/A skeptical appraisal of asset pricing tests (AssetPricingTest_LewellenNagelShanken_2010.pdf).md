# A skeptical appraisal of asset pricing tests

Jonathan Lewellen, Stefan Nagel, and Jay Shanken. Library version: accepted manuscript, received December 2007, revised June 2008, accepted October 2008; source filename identifies the 2010 publication. Source: [AssetPricingTest_LewellenNagelShanken_2010.pdf](../../Finance/AssetPricingTest_LewellenNagelShanken_2010.pdf), 43 PDF pages. Page references below use the manuscript's printed numbering; the title page is PDF page 1. The complete manuscript, appendices, simulations, and final two-page results table were inspected.

## Why this matters

A model can explain the average returns of the 25 size–book-to-market portfolios extraordinarily well while having little economically distinctive content. The paper identifies why: these portfolios have a low-dimensional covariance structure, several conventional tests leave economically important prices unrestricted, and cross-sectional fit is noisy. A factor weakly correlated with the factors that organize the test assets can inherit their apparent pricing success. Consequently, impressive in-sample fit does not by itself distinguish a consumption mechanism, an investment mechanism, or an arbitrary statistical proxy.

The contribution is both algebraic and operational. The authors prove population results explaining spurious discrimination, simulate the sampling distribution of cross-sectional fit, connect GLS pricing errors to attainable portfolio performance, and reexamine five influential models under a common design. Their recommendations concern the construction and interpretation of an asset-pricing test. They are not an out-of-sample return-forecasting strategy, and no trading-cost-adjusted investment performance is claimed.

The useful target for a reproducing researcher is therefore a diagnostic system: ask whether a proposed model survives changes in the test-asset span, restrictions on prices that follow from the model, and confidence intervals for economic pricing errors. Rejecting a null is only part of this task. A wide range of economically incompatible models can fail to be rejected by the same weak experiment.

## Population mechanism: a change of coordinates can look like a new explanation

Let $R$ be an $N$-vector of excess returns generated by $K$ true factors $F$:

$$
R=BF+e,\qquad E[e]=0,\qquad \operatorname{Cov}(e,F)=0,\qquad E[R]=B E[F].
$$

Here $B$ is an $N\times K$ loading matrix. A researcher proposes a $J$-vector of factors $P$ and estimates its loadings

$$
C=\operatorname{Cov}(R,P)\operatorname{Var}(P)^{-1}.
$$

The conventional cross-sectional regression fits

$$
\mu_R=z\mathbf1+C\lambda+\alpha.
$$

The intercept $z$, factor prices $\lambda$, and pricing errors $\alpha$ must be distinguished from time-series regression intercepts. Because returns are excess returns, a model that prices the riskless asset ordinarily requires $z=0$. A traded factor also has an observable expected excess return that restricts its own price. Allowing both objects to be unrestricted can make the test substantially less demanding than the economic theory.

Observation 1 considers $J=K$, nonsingular $\operatorname{Cov}(F,P)$, and $\operatorname{Cov}(e,P)=0$. Then

$$
C=BQ,\qquad Q=\operatorname{Cov}(F,P)\operatorname{Var}(P)^{-1}.
$$

Since $Q$ is invertible, choosing $\lambda=Q^{-1}\mu_F$ gives $C\lambda=B\mu_F=\mu_R$. The proposed model prices perfectly. Correlations between $P$ and $F$ may be arbitrarily small as long as the relevant covariance matrix retains rank. The risk prices can become correspondingly large. Thus a good cross-sectional fit need not imply that the proposed factors capture a substantial fraction of the variation in returns or resemble the true economic shocks.

This is a population identification problem, not merely a small-sample bias. It also affects moment-based or stochastic-discount-factor formulations that impose equivalent unrestricted linear pricing relations. An invertible relabeling of the relevant span cannot be distinguished through the same cross-sectional restrictions.

Observation 2 makes the issue tangible. Under a strict factor structure with diagonal idiosyncratic covariance, returns on any suitable $K$ assets can price the other assets if those $K$ assets span the common exposures and are excluded from the test assets. Their idiosyncratic noise need not destroy the apparent result. In the one-factor illustration, the unrestricted price associated with the selected asset is its expected excess return divided by its squared correlation with the true factor. That price generally differs from the asset's own expected return. Putting the candidate factor into the pricing exercise, with its price restricted appropriately, exposes what the unrestricted exercise concealed.

Observation 3 quantifies chance fit. After cross-sectional demeaning, normalize the true loading directions to be orthogonal with unit second moments. Let $Q$ be a $K\times J$ matrix of independent isotropic normal draws, with $J\leq K$. For a model whose loadings are $BQ$, cross-sectional fit is

$$
R^2=\frac{\mu_F'Q(Q'Q)^{-1}Q'\mu_F}{\mu_F'\mu_F},\qquad E[R^2]=\frac{J}{K}.
$$

The proof uses rotational symmetry: the expected projection onto a random $J$-dimensional subspace of a $K$-dimensional space is $(J/K)I$. This expectation belongs to the stated random-direction experiment. It is not a universal formula for every distribution of candidate economic factors. It does show why a few arbitrary factor directions can appear persuasive when the assets' relevant cross-sectional structure has very few dimensions.

The 25 size–book-to-market portfolios are especially vulnerable. Fama–French factors explain more than 92% of their time-series variation and more than 80% of their average-return variation in the paper's population calibration. Market betas are close to one, so an unrestricted intercept absorbs much of the market direction. What remains is close to a two-dimensional size–value problem.

## Random-model experiments and their reproducible ingredients

Section 2 and Figures 1–3 use quarterly returns, 1963–2004, with $T=168$. The sample means and covariances of the 25 Fama–French portfolios are treated as population moments in the first experiments. Each distribution in Figure 1 uses 5,000 random model draws.

In the first construction, each candidate factor is $P=w'F+\nu$, where $F$ contains the three Fama–French factors, $w$ has independent standard-normal entries, and $\nu$ is independent of returns. Its return covariance is therefore the covariance with $w'F$. A one-factor model has median OLS $R^2=0.15$, and 23% of draws exceed 0.50. With two factors the median is 0.79 and 89% exceed 0.50. Three factors always recover the Fama–French fit of 0.81 because they span the same population directions almost surely.

The second construction draws weights over all 25 portfolio returns, then demeans and rescales them to one dollar long and one dollar short. Its candidate factors are random zero-investment portfolios. For one through five factors the median $R^2$ values are 0.15, 0.67, 0.78, 0.82, and 0.84. Seventy-one percent of two-factor models and 92% of three-factor models exceed 0.50. Ten percent of the two-factor models and 30% of the three-factor models outperform the Fama–French cross-sectional fit.

The third construction retains random portfolios with approximately zero expected returns. Their correct risk prices should then be approximately zero, whereas the regression is allowed to estimate arbitrary prices. Median fits with one, three, and five factors are 0.03, 0.52, and 0.64; the corresponding 95th percentiles are 0.53, 0.67, and 0.72. The numerical tolerance used to define “approximately zero” is not reported. That tolerance is a missing replication input, not a license to impose a convenient threshold and claim an exact reproduction.

These are intentionally artificial models. Their function is to establish a benchmark distribution for how easy it is to pass a particular empirical exercise. A proposed theory should do more than perform favorably against an uninformative zero-fit benchmark when arbitrary portfolios often produce much larger fits.

Figure 2 separates population fit from sampling variation. For each target population $R^2$ on a grid from zero to one, the authors construct factor covariance vectors $g_j$ with equal shares of cross-sectional explanatory power and mutually orthogonal cross-sectional directions. A portfolio with weights $w_j=V^{-1}g_j$, where $V=\operatorname{Var}(R)$, has the required covariance vector. Ten factor specifications are constructed at each target. For each specification, 4,000 samples of complete quarterly observations are drawn with replacement, preserving the contemporaneous dependence of returns and factors. Both first-pass betas and the cross-sectional regression are reestimated in every bootstrap sample. Thus each distribution uses 40,000 simulated estimates, not a bootstrap of the second pass alone.

A noisy-factor experiment adds independent noise with variance three times the variance of the underlying factor portfolio. This caps the factor's attainable time-series explanatory power at 25%. A replicator should preserve this variance ratio. The paper does not supply a random seed or a complete distributional specification for every auxiliary noise draw, so bitwise reproduction is not possible from the text alone.

Even when true fit is zero, the 95th percentile of sample adjusted $R^2$ is 0.44 for one noisy factor, 0.62 for three, and 0.69 for five. For five factors, rejecting true fit of 0.30 or less at the 5% level requires observed fit above approximately 0.73. Power is only 17% when true fit is 0.70 and 26% when it is 0.80. High observed fit is therefore weak evidence that the underlying model explains average returns accurately.

Figure 3 adds 30 industry portfolios to the 25 size–value portfolios in one joint cross section. With three factors, median fits for the three random-model constructions fall from 0.81, 0.78, and 0.52 to 0.35, 0.21, and 0.11. The design matters: running a separate industry test is not equivalent to requiring one set of prices to fit all 55 assets. New assets need to introduce variation in expected returns or exposures that challenges the existing relation; both do not have to change independently.

## GLS fit, quadratic errors, and portfolio opportunity sets

Write $X=[\mathbf1,B]$ for the model's cross-sectional regressors and $\mu=E[R]$. OLS and GLS solve different projection problems:

$$
\hat\lambda_{\rm OLS}=(X'X)^{-1}X'\mu,\qquad
\hat\lambda_{\rm GLS}=(X'V^{-1}X)^{-1}X'V^{-1}\mu.
$$

Here the coefficient vectors include the intercept. Let $\alpha$ and $\alpha^*$ denote their respective residuals. If $\Sigma$ is the covariance of first-pass residuals, replacing $V$ by $\Sigma$ in GLS gives the same coefficients and residuals under the factor decomposition. Although OLS and GLS $R^2$ can be very different, their properly constructed pricing-error quadratic forms coincide:

$$
q=\alpha'(M_X\Sigma M_X)^+\alpha
  ={\alpha^*}'V^{-1}\alpha^*
  ={\alpha^*}'\Sigma^{-1}\alpha^*,
\qquad M_X=I-X(X'X)^{-1}X'.
$$

The superscript $+$ denotes a generalized inverse. The singularity is structural: estimated pricing errors satisfy orthogonality restrictions and cannot have a full-rank covariance matrix. Ignoring this distinction would produce an incorrect test statistic.

GLS fit can be expressed as

$$
R^2_{\rm GLS}=1-\frac{q}{Q},\qquad
Q=(\mu-\mu_{\rm GMV}\mathbf1)'V^{-1}(\mu-\mu_{\rm GMV}\mathbf1),
$$

$$
\mu_{\rm GMV}=\frac{\mathbf1'V^{-1}\mu}{\mathbf1'V^{-1}\mathbf1}.
$$

This assumes $Q>0$. The denominator depends on the test assets, not on the particular model. The quadratic error measures a remaining portfolio opportunity, while GLS $R^2$ scales that error by the overall cross-sectional opportunity set. A change of test assets can therefore change the denominator as well as the model's error.

Factor-mimicking portfolio weights are proportional to $V^{-1}\operatorname{Cov}(R,F)$. Their span turns a model of nontraded factors into an equivalent set of return directions for this algebra. GLS makes pricing errors orthogonal to these portfolios in the relevant metric. Where unit-budget normalization is feasible, their prices are their means relative to the fitted zero-beta return. A portfolio with zero total weight cannot simply be rescaled to unit total weight; budget conventions and any intercept restriction must be maintained explicitly.

The economic interpretation is an unexplained squared generalized Sharpe ratio, evaluated at the fitted zero-beta rate when the intercept is free. It is not automatically the squared Sharpe ratio relative to the observed riskless rate. Imposing a zero excess-return intercept is what ties that interpretation to the actual riskless asset.

The paper's calibrated Fama–French model has OLS fit 0.809 but GLS fit only 0.195 on the 25 portfolios. GLS is a much higher practical hurdle here. Nevertheless it is not a universal cure: under Observation 1's exact conditions both OLS and GLS fit perfectly. The reason to report GLS is its economic weighting and interpretation, not an assertion that it eliminates all weak identification.

## Statistical uncertainty and model restrictions

The paper advocates intervals for true fit and economic error rather than treating point estimates or rejection indicators as sufficient. Figure 5 inverts simulated sampling distributions: a one-factor sample fit of 0.50 can imply a 90% interval for true fit extending roughly from 0.25 to 1.00. The interval is asymmetric and can reach the parameter boundary. A symmetric normal interval around the observed $R^2$ misses this behavior.

For conventional two-pass inference, the pricing-error statistic can be written schematically as

$$
T^2=\hat\alpha'\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat\alpha)^+\hat\alpha
 =\frac{T\hat q}{1+\hat\gamma'\hat\Sigma_F^{-1}\hat\gamma}.
$$

The denominator is the errors-in-variables correction associated with estimated betas. With an intercept and $K$ factors, the central chi-squared reference has $N-K-1$ degrees of freedom under the null and the conventional regression moment conditions. The appendix develops a noncentral approximation for alternatives. A precise qualification matters: the rigorous finite noncentral limiting distribution is a local-alternative result, with pricing errors shrinking at rate $T^{-1/2}$. Under a fixed nonzero error, the statistic diverges; it does not converge to one fixed noncentral chi-squared distribution.

For traded or factor-mimicking portfolio returns $R_P$, consider the time-series system $R=\alpha+B R_P+e$. Let $s_P^2$ be the sample squared maximum Sharpe ratio of factor returns, $c=(1+s_P^2)/T$, and let $\hat\Sigma$ use residual degrees of freedom $T-K-1$. The GRS statistic is

$$
F=\frac{T-N-K}{N(T-K-1)}c^{-1}\hat\alpha'\hat\Sigma^{-1}\hat\alpha.
$$

Under conditionally independent Gaussian residuals and the maintained regression structure, its null reference is $F_{N,T-N-K}$. The noncentrality is linked to $\theta_z^2=\alpha'\Sigma^{-1}\alpha$, the additional squared Sharpe ratio available beyond the factor portfolios. Normality and independence are substantive conditions, not automatically established by using quarterly observations.

For the CAPM on the 25 portfolios, average absolute alpha is 0.96% per quarter and the GRS statistic is 3.49. A 90% confidence interval for $\theta_z^2$ is 0.21–0.61, corresponding to a remaining quarterly Sharpe ratio of approximately 0.46–0.78, versus 0.18 for the market. This expresses the economic size of the failure much more directly than a near-zero p-value.

The connection to Hansen–Jagannathan distance uses the **squared** distance $D$. With an optimally chosen linear proxy, an intercept, and fixed gross riskless return $1+r_f$, the relevant result is $D=\theta_z^2/(1+r_f)^2$. Taking a square root converts this to an $L^2$ distance. Confusing the squared and unsquared conventions changes units and invalidates a numerical comparison.

## Common empirical reappraisal

Table 1 compares the 25 portfolios with the combined 55-portfolio cross section using quarterly percentage returns, generally 1963–2004. Yogo's supplied factors end in 2001. The five main models are Lettau–Ludvigson's consumption-growth interactions with the consumption–wealth ratio, Lustig–Van Nieuwerburgh's consumption-growth interactions with housing collateral, Santos–Veronesi's labor-income-share interaction with the market, Li–Vassalou–Xing's household/corporate/noncorporate investment-growth factors, and Yogo's nondurable-consumption growth, durable-consumption growth, and market return. The CAPM, consumption CAPM, and Fama–French model serve as benchmarks.

The factors are obtained from the original researchers' data. The manuscript does not reproduce each original study's entire real-time data-release calendar, construction code, or vintage policy. Thus a full replication needs those original factor files and definitions. This paper's experiment is a common pricing reappraisal conditional on those inputs, not a self-contained reconstruction of every underlying macroeconomic database.

Across the five main models, average GLS fit falls from approximately 0.08 to 0.02. Their unweighted cross-sectional fits often differ much more than their economically weighted pricing errors.

Conventional test outcomes can suggest less failure than these economic errors imply. For example, Lettau–Ludvigson's $T^2$ changes from 33.9 with p-value 0.08 to 193.8 with p-value rounded to 0.00. Lustig–Van Nieuwerburgh changes from 20.8 (0.57) to 157.1 (0.04), Santos–Veronesi from 26.0 (0.63) to 160.8 (0.07), Li–Vassalou–Xing from 25.2 (0.29) to 141.2 (0.11), and Yogo from 24.9 (0.69) to 159.3 (0.06). The Fama–French model is rejected in both universes, despite looking much stronger in some point-estimate comparisons.

The free intercepts are economically substantial: approximately 1.7%–3.6% per quarter across reported specifications. The main models imply annualized zero-beta excess returns around 7.8%–14.3%. Treating these as innocuous nuisance estimates conceals a large deviation from the models' pricing of the riskless asset.

For Santos–Veronesi, the supplied return factors have sample means about 1.53% per quarter for the market and −0.01% for its interaction. Imposing their observable prices makes OLS and GLS fit negative. The corresponding test statistics rise to 89.2 and 233.7 for 25 and 55 assets, both with effectively zero reported p-values. Also imposing zero intercept raises them to 96.9 and 240.1. Including traded factors as additional left-hand-side assets forces the relevant self-pricing restriction under GLS; adding them to OLS does not mechanically guarantee it.

Confidence intervals remain wide. Average 95% OLS-fit interval width exceeds 0.70. Li–Vassalou–Xing's interval moves from approximately [0.75, 1] on the 25 portfolios to [0.20, 1] on all 55. Some GLS intervals marked with an asterisk are empty inversions because the observed fit lies below the simulated lower tail at every grid value; these are not evidence that population fit is known to equal zero. The simulations condition on estimated nuisance parameters and are approximate finite-sample diagnostics, not uniformly exact confidence procedures.

## Restrictions that remain meaningful under rescaling

A practical way to audit the population examples is to change only the measurement units of a proposed factor. If a scalar factor is replaced by $aP$ with $a\ne0$, its time-series loading becomes $C/a$, while its unrestricted cross-sectional price becomes $a\lambda$. Predicted expected returns are unchanged. This explains why large or small estimated prices have no meaning without a declared factor normalization. A factor can have weak covariance with returns and still obtain a high unrestricted fit because the estimated price compensates for the loading's scale.

There is an additional distinction between units and economic restrictions. If $P$ is a traded excess return, its expected return transforms to $aE[P]$ under the same rescaling. The equality between its price and expected return is invariant and meaningful. Choosing an arbitrary unrestricted price is not made economically valid merely by changing factor units. The proposed factor's own payoff supplies a restriction that the remaining test assets may fail to impose.

The one-factor noisy-proxy example makes the difference explicit. Let $P=F+\nu$, with independent noise variance $\sigma_\nu^2$. Its loading on a true one-factor return system is attenuated by $q=\sigma_F^2/(\sigma_F^2+\sigma_\nu^2)$. The unrestricted price $\mu_F/q$ offsets that attenuation exactly, preserving perfect population pricing of the original assets under the stated orthogonality assumptions. As noise grows, the first-pass relation becomes less informative and the fitted price grows. Restricting a genuinely traded proxy's price to its own mean prevents this arbitrary compensation. This algebra is a specialization of Observation 1, not a separate estimated result.

For the expanded-universe experiment, keep the factors and their price restrictions fixed while adding industry portfolios. Reestimating different economic prices for each asset subgroup would surrender the joint restriction being tested. Likewise, a separate high fit on industries and on size–value portfolios can coexist with failure of one common price vector on both. A replication should therefore store the portfolio ordering and fit all 55 rows at once when constructing the combined-sample $q$ and $R^2$ statistics.

These checks explain why the authors place several recommendations together. A broader universe interrogates the economic relation outside the original low-dimensional span; GLS measures remaining opportunities in a return-covariance metric; self-pricing restrictions prevent an arbitrary factor from borrowing unrestricted prices; and confidence intervals quantify how much discrimination remains after sampling error. None of these checks logically substitutes for the others.

## Reimplementation sequence and limits

A faithful implementation begins by aligning complete quarterly return and factor observations, preserving the published end date for each model. Estimate first-pass loadings and residual covariances; fit both OLS and GLS cross sections with explicitly recorded intercept and factor-price restrictions. Report the raw pricing errors, adjusted OLS fit, GLS fit, $q$, and appropriate uncertainty measures. Repeat the entire exercise jointly on the expanded asset set. For return factors, run a separate restricted specification using their means, and document whether they appear among the test assets.

For the random-model experiments, hold the empirical population moments fixed, construct factors before sampling, and distinguish random factor directions from bootstrap sampling variation. Reestimate betas inside every resample. For interval inversion, evaluate candidate population fits rather than using the observed fit as the sole bootstrap population. The paper's empirical interval construction fixes historical loading structure, alters the component of expected returns explained by the model, and adds isotropic cross-sectional residual variation while preserving historical dispersion. This nuisance-parameter calibration must be disclosed because another calibration can produce different intervals.

The most important numerical fragility is covariance inversion with 55 assets and 168 quarters. The paper studies this finite-sample setting through simulation; it does not prescribe a shrinkage estimator or claim that arbitrary regularization preserves its exact statistics. Any regularized implementation is an extension and needs its own inference. Likewise, missing random seeds, rejection tolerances, complete factor vintages, and auxiliary simulation settings prevent literal reconstruction of every table entry from the manuscript alone.

The broader implication is narrow but powerful: evidence for a mechanism requires restrictions that competing mechanisms cannot cheaply mimic. Diverse test assets, correctly priced traded factors, economically meaningful intercepts, and uncertainty about remaining portfolio opportunities create such restrictions. The results do not establish that every macroeconomic model is false, that returns are irrational, or that a rejected model is useless for risk management. They establish that a familiar successful pricing regression may contain far less information than its fit suggests.
