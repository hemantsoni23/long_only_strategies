# Timing versus Sizing Skill in the Investment Process

Ronald J. M. van Loon. *The Journal of Portfolio Management*, Winter 2018, pp. 25–32. Source: [SharpeDecomposition_VanLoon_2018.pdf](../../Finance/SharpeDecomposition_VanLoon_2018.pdf), 8 pages.

## Why this matters

A strategy can win infrequently and still make money when its winning positions are large enough; it can also win often and lose money through occasional large losses. Van Loon decomposes performance using a hit ratio, a conditional win/loss ratio, and the number of investment opportunities. The resulting framework is useful for discussing an investment process in terms a portfolio manager can inspect: how often positions succeed, how much is made relative to what is lost, and how frequently independent opportunities arise.

The strongest result is exact for a two-outcome return model. The extension to general returns is an approximation and requires information about payoff dispersion that hit ratio and average win/loss ratio do not contain. This distinction matters because the paper's headline coefficient, approximately 1.6, is not implied by the central limit theorem. It reflects a particular relationship between conditional payoff magnitudes and total volatility. Applying it universally can produce a materially wrong information ratio even in the paper's elementary binomial model.

The paper develops a practical decomposition rather than a method for identifying causal forecasting skill. Observed hit rates and payoff ratios can be generated by trading rules, exposure scaling, option-like payoff shapes, transaction costs, or luck. Calling the two terms “timing” and “sizing” is an interpretation that should be checked against the actual decision process.

## Definitions and the exact two-outcome model

Use $X$ for one opportunity's active log return. In the elementary model,

$$
X=\begin{cases}a,&\text{with probability }p,\\-b,&\text{with probability }1-p,\end{cases}
\qquad a>0,\ b>0.
$$

The hit ratio is $p$, the average win/loss ratio is $x=a/b$, and the one-opportunity information ratio is $IR_1=E[X]/\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}$. This is a mean-to-standard-deviation ratio of active log returns. It is not automatically the same as a conventional arithmetic active-return information ratio.

The source writes the two increments using logarithms of an up and down return. A precise implementation must interpret those objects as gross return factors, with $u>1$ and $0<d<1$, so that $a=\log u$ and $b=-\log d$. A logarithm of a negative simple return is undefined. All expectations and volatility estimates must use the same return convention.

Direct calculation gives

$$
\mu=pa-(1-p)b=b\{p(1+x)-1\},
$$

$$
\sigma^2=p(1-p)(a+b)^2.
$$

The variance follows by subtracting the square of the mean from $pa^2+(1-p)b^2$. Consequently,

$$
IR_1=\frac{p-1/(1+x)}{\sqrt{p(1-p)}}.
$$

This identity separates a break-even threshold from a hit-rate normalization. Positive expected active return requires

$$
p>\frac{1}{1+x}.
$$

A hit ratio of 40% and win/loss ratio of 1.5 is exactly break-even, not strictly profitable. A win/loss ratio above one lowers the hit rate needed to break even; it does not guarantee profitability. The condition concerns expected return and assumes the win/loss quantities include the economically relevant costs.

Near $p=1/2$, $\sqrt{p(1-p)}\approx1/2$, giving

$$
IR_1\approx2\left(p-\frac{1}{1+x}\right).
$$

Writing $p=1/2+\varepsilon$, the denominator is $\tfrac12\sqrt{1-4\varepsilon^2}$. Its inverse is $2(1+2\varepsilon^2+O(\varepsilon^4))$. Thus the denominator approximation is locally second order in the hit-rate departure from one half. It becomes poor as hit rates approach zero or one, where the binomial variance vanishes and estimation is also most fragile.

## Opportunity count and the coefficient problem

For $N$ independent, identically distributed additive log-return opportunities,

$$
E\left[\sum_{j=1}^N X_j\right]=N\mu,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}\left(\sum_{j=1}^N X_j\right)=N\sigma^2,
$$

so

$$
IR_N=\sqrt N\,IR_1.
$$

This is an exact moment identity; it does not require asymptotic normality. The central limit theorem describes the limiting shape of a standardized sum, but it does not change its mean or variance or insert an additional factor into their ratio.

The paper's broader approximation is

$$
IR\approx1.6\left(HR-\frac{1}{1+WL}\right)\sqrt N,
$$

with $1.6$ approximately $\sqrt{8/\pi}$. The source motivates a conversion between a binomial formulation and normally distributed returns. That conversion cannot be justified merely by saying that a sum of binomial increments becomes normal. For the fixed two-outcome model, the original mean and variance still produce the exact coefficient near 2.

A numerical counterexample makes the distinction concrete. With $p=0.55$, $x=1$, and $N=12$, the exact binomial information ratio is 0.348155. The paper's 1.6 approximation gives 0.277128, approximately 20.4% lower. Increasing $N$ does not remove that proportional discrepancy because both formulas scale with $\sqrt N$. This is a change in the underlying payoff model, not a finite-sample failure of the central limit theorem.

## General returns: the missing conditional variances

For a general distribution with no point mass at zero, define

$$
p=P(X>0),\quad a=E[X\mid X>0],\quad
b=-E[X\mid X<0],
$$

and $v_+=\operatorname{Var}(X\mid X>0)$, $v_-=\operatorname{Var}(X\mid X<0)$. The mean remains $pa-(1-p)b$, but the law of total variance gives

$$
\sigma^2=pv_++(1-p)v_-+p(1-p)(a+b)^2.
$$

The binomial model sets both conditional variances to zero. Hit ratio and win/loss ratio do not determine those variances, so they cannot identify the information ratio for arbitrary returns.

Define a shape factor

$$
\kappa=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{p(1-p)}(a+b)}\ge1.
$$

Then the exact general identity is

$$
IR_1=\frac{p-1/(1+x)}{\sqrt{p(1-p)}\,\kappa}.
$$

This is a useful diagnostic extension of the source's approximation. For fixed $p$, $a$, and $b$, adding dispersion within winning or losing outcomes increases $\kappa$ and reduces the magnitude of the information ratio. The hit rate and conditional mean payoffs can remain unchanged while total risk changes substantially.

For a nearly centered normal distribution, $p\approx1/2$ and $a\approx b\approx\sigma\sqrt{2/\pi}$. Consequently $\kappa\approx\sqrt{\pi/2}$, and the local coefficient becomes $2/\kappa=\sqrt{8/\pi}$. This supplies the extra shape assumption under which the coefficient 1.6 is reasonable. A normality assumption on individual payoff magnitudes near symmetry does real work; a central limit theorem for their sum does not substitute for it.

If observations equal zero, the analyst must define the hit-rate denominator and treat the zero mass explicitly. Removing zero returns changes the opportunity count. Splitting them between wins and losses changes the conditional averages. Neither is innocuous when a strategy is frequently inactive.

## Tail adjustment and its limits

The paper replaces 1.6 with smaller coefficients for Student-$t$ return distributions. Exhibit 3 reports coefficients around 1.55 for excess kurtosis 1, 1.52 for 2, 1.50 for 3, 1.47 for 5–6, 1.45 for 8–10, and 1.41 in its limiting high-kurtosis case. For a Student-$t$ variable with degrees of freedom $\nu>4$, excess kurtosis is $6/(\nu-4)$.

The local symmetric-distribution interpretation reconstructs this table. For a centered $t_\nu$ variable, the coefficient is twice its mean absolute deviation divided by its standard deviation:

$$
c_\nu=\frac{4\sqrt{\nu-2}\,\Gamma((\nu+1)/2)}{(\nu-1)\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma(\nu/2)},\qquad \nu>2.
$$

This converges to $\sqrt{8/\pi}$ as $\nu\to\infty$. At $\nu=4$, it equals $\sqrt2$, matching the table's limiting value as finite excess kurtosis grows without bound. The table therefore spans a particular Student-$t$ family with $\nu\ge4$, not every fat-tailed distribution. For example, $\nu=3$ still has finite variance but the coefficient is $4/\pi\approx1.273$, below the table's range; its fourth moment is infinite.

Accordingly, the paper's statement that fat tails reduce the coefficient by up to roughly 12% applies to the illustrated family/range. It is not a universal bound. Skewness, mixtures, asymmetric conditional variances, changing exposures, and tail dependence can all change the mapping. Estimating excess kurtosis from a short financial sample is itself noisy, so a coefficient chosen from a single kurtosis estimate should not be treated as precisely known.

## Marginal improvements and a derivative correction

Let $K=c\sqrt N$ and use the approximation $IR=K[p-1/(1+x)]$. Its partial derivatives are

$$
\frac{\partial IR}{\partial p}=K,\qquad
\frac{\partial IR}{\partial x}=\frac{K}{(1+x)^2}.
$$

The ratio of derivatives with respect to equal **absolute** changes is therefore $(1+x)^2$, which is 4 at $x=1$. The source's second endnote prints a factor $\tfrac12(1+x)^2$ in discussing sensitivity. That factor is not the ratio of these partial derivatives.

There is a coherent interpretation for the factor one half: compare equal proportional changes in $p$ and $x$. The local ratio becomes

$$
\frac{p\,\partial IR/\partial p}{x\,\partial IR/\partial x}
=\frac{p(1+x)^2}{x},
$$

which equals 2 at $p=1/2$, $x=1$. Thus the paper's practical comparison between a 10% relative improvement in hit ratio and a 10% relative improvement in win/loss ratio is approximately valid locally, while the derivative labeling needs correction. Hit ratio moving from 50% to 55% is a five-percentage-point change and a 10% relative change; win/loss ratio moving from 1 to 1.1 is a 0.1 absolute change.

For $N=12$ and $c=1.6$, those finite changes give information-ratio increments of 0.277128 and 0.131966, respectively. The ratio is approximately 2.10 rather than exactly 2 because the win/loss transformation is nonlinear. More generally, the derivatives hold other quantities fixed. An actual change in position sizing can alter hit rate, conditional variances, tail shape, and the effective number of opportunities simultaneously.

## Costs and gain-to-pain conversion

If each opportunity incurs a fixed log-return cost $d>0$, and $d<a$ so winning observations remain positive, the net conditional magnitudes are $a-d$ and $b+d$. Hence

$$
WL_{\mathrm{net}}=\frac{a-d}{b+d},\qquad
p_{\mathrm{break-even,net}}=\frac{b+d}{a+b}.
$$

The source's third endnote suggests a simple subtraction involving twice a logarithmic cost from the win/loss ratio. That is not a general identity for a ratio of conditional means. The correct transformation is fractional and depends on both gross payoff magnitudes. If the cost exceeds some gross winners, observations cross zero and the hit ratio also changes; subtracting costs from a fixed pair of conditional means is then insufficient.

The gain-to-pain ratio, defined as total positive return divided by the absolute total negative return, has a simpler exact relation. With the same sample, same return units, and no ambiguity about zero observations,

$$
GtP=\frac{p\,a}{(1-p)b},\qquad
WL=GtP\frac{1-p}{p}.
$$

This conversion is useful when a data provider reports gain-to-pain but not conditional win/loss ratio. It does not recover conditional second moments and therefore does not solve the general information-ratio identification problem.

## Empirical illustrations and actual inputs

Exhibit 4 reports sixteen investment styles using January 1972–December 2012 results sourced from Kolanovic and Wei (2013). The opportunities are treated as monthly, so $N=12$ for annualization. The table supplies annualized excess returns, tracking errors, excess kurtosis, reported information ratios, the chosen coefficient, hit ratios, win/loss ratios, and predicted information ratios.

For FX carry, excess return is 5.7% per year, tracking error 7.9%, excess kurtosis 2.8, reported $IR=0.72$, coefficient 1.50, hit ratio 0.63, and win/loss ratio 1.02. The approximation gives 0.70. FX momentum has 2.4% return, 9.0% tracking error, reported $IR=0.26$, hit ratio 0.59, and win/loss ratio 0.85; the predicted ratio is 0.27. FX value has 3.7% return, 8.8% tracking error, reported $IR=0.42$, hit ratio 0.55, and win/loss ratio 1.14; its prediction is 0.43.

The agreement is not exact across rows. Equity carry has reported information ratio 0.61 versus predicted 0.70, and bond value has 0.56 versus 0.62. Traditional bonds have reported 1.26 versus predicted 1.25, using hit ratio 0.67, win/loss ratio 1.33, and coefficient 1.49. These examples support the approximation's descriptive usefulness in the supplied data; they do not establish a distribution-free formula.

Exhibit 5 examines equity-market timing rules over January 1976–December 2016, drawing on Feldman, Jung, and Klein (2015). Reported annualized information ratios and predictions are 0.96 and 0.93 for a leading-economic-indicator rule, 0.81 and 0.80 for a yield-curve rule, 0.81 and 0.80 for the Fed model, and 0.67 and 0.74 for a CAPE rule. Their hit ratios are respectively 0.75, 0.67, 0.69, and 0.73, while win/loss ratios are 0.77, 0.95, 0.86, and 0.71. High hit rates can compensate for average losses larger than average gains.

The endnotes provide partial trading specifications. The leading-indicator rule uses three consecutive increases to choose equities and three consecutive decreases to choose Treasury bills. The yield-curve rule compares the ten-year Treasury yield with the three-month bill yield. The Fed-model rule compares earnings yield minus ten-year yield with a historical tenth-percentile threshold. CAPE affects equity allocation relative to a long-run valuation reference. Exact data vintages, signal publication lags, initial states, percentile-window implementation, and the full CAPE-to-weight mapping are not supplied here. These omissions prevent an independent reconstruction of the timing histories solely from this article.

Exhibit 6 simulates switching between the S&P 500 and a Bloomberg U.S. Treasury index over January 2000–December 2016. It reports 10,000 iterations for each hit-rate setting and explores hit rates from 0.40 to 0.60 and win/loss ratios from 1.0 to 1.3. At a hit ratio of 0.50, simulated information ratios for those four win/loss settings are 0.00, 0.13, 0.24, and 0.34, versus formula values 0.00, 0.13, 0.25, and 0.36. At 0.55, the pairs are 0.27/0.28, 0.40/0.41, 0.51/0.53, and 0.61/0.64. At 0.60 they are 0.54/0.55, 0.66/0.69, 0.78/0.80, and 0.88/0.91.

The direction of the approximation error in this table is informative: predicted values tend to exceed simulated values toward higher hit rates and win/loss ratios. But the article does not provide enough detail about the randomization and conditioning used to impose both target statistics to reproduce every simulation cell exactly. A simulation that fixes those statistics mechanically can also differ from a feasible trading rule that generates them endogenously.

## What a reproducible application needs

A practitioner should begin with a dated series of net active returns and an explicit definition of an opportunity. Compute positive and negative counts, conditional means, conditional variances, the unconditional mean and standard deviation, and the exact sample information ratio using one consistent frequency. Then evaluate the formula as a diagnostic approximation, reporting the observed shape factor rather than assuming it away.

Independent opportunities are a substantive requirement for square-root scaling. If $X_t$ is stationary with variance $\sigma^2$ and lag-$h$ covariance $\gamma_h$, then

$$
\operatorname{Var}\left(\sum_{t=1}^N X_t\right)
=N\sigma^2+2\sum_{h=1}^{N-1}(N-h)\gamma_h.
$$

Positive serial dependence reduces the information ratio relative to the independent-opportunity calculation for a fixed mean; negative dependence can increase it. Counting overlapping monthly trades or correlated assets as independent bets exaggerates breadth. Heterogeneous strategies also cannot generally be summarized by unweighted averages of their hit ratios and win/loss ratios: conditional payoff magnitudes, opportunity counts, risk allocations, and covariances determine the aggregate.

The empirical tables use realized hit ratios, payoff ratios, and information ratios from the same histories. Their agreement is partly an internal description of those distributions, not an out-of-sample forecast test. The article does not supply confidence intervals, a multiple-testing adjustment, transaction-level data, or an identification design separating genuine timing information from systematic payoff asymmetry. A short-option strategy can have a high hit rate without superior forecasts; a trend-following strategy can have low hit rate and large winners because of its exit rules.

The useful managerial question is therefore not simply whether to improve timing or sizing. It is which controllable decision changes the full net payoff distribution, by how much, with what estimation error, and at what implementation cost. The paper's decomposition helps organize that discussion. The exact two-outcome identity and the general conditional-variance identity supply the checks needed to keep the discussion quantitatively honest.
