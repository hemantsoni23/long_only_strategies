# The Variance Drain and Jensen’s Inequality

**Robert A. Becker.** CAEPR Working Paper 2012-004, Indiana University, March 19, 2012. Source: [VarianceDrain_Becker_2012.pdf](../../Finance/VarianceDrain_Becker_2012.pdf). The full 14-page file was read, including references. Printed pp. 1–13 follow a cover page, so PDF page numbers exceed printed numbers by one. This note treats the local working paper as the source version.

## Why this matters

The rule that compound return is approximately arithmetic return minus half the variance is useful but often treated as though it were an identity. Becker shows how to connect it to an exact refinement of Jensen's inequality. The resulting coefficient depends on the entire observed return sequence. This explains both the appeal and the limitations of a mean–variance description of realized compound growth.

The innovative component is the application of Hölder's defect formula to the arithmetic–geometric mean gap, followed by a finite-sample financial interpretation that requires no distributional model. The paper does not claim to originate the half-variance approximation; it discusses earlier derivations, including Messmore and Young–Trent. It supplies a Taylor-remainder proof and distinguishes an exact log-gap relation from approximate differences in simple returns. There is no portfolio-selection algorithm or predictive return model to estimate.

For an implementation, the main payoff is an exact consistency check and a curvature bound. Mean and variance alone cannot determine compound growth, because the supposedly missing higher moments reappear through the sequence-dependent coefficient. The theorem gives a disciplined way to identify that dependence instead of hiding it inside an approximation sign.

## Objects and assumptions of the theorem

Let $x_1,\ldots,x_N$ lie in a compact interval $[a,b]$, and let nonnegative weights $p_k$ sum to one. Zero-weight points can be discarded; statements about equality then refer to the positive-weight support. Define

$$
x_A=\sum_{k=1}^N p_kx_k,
\qquad
V=\sum_{k=1}^N p_k(x_k-x_A)^2.
$$

The notation can describe a finite random variable $X$ with probabilities $p_k$, but the proof only needs a collection of weighted numbers. It applies just as well to a realized return history whose observations are dependent or generated by an unknown process.

Assume $f$ is twice continuously differentiable and convex on $[a,b]$, with finite bounds

$$
0\le m\le f''(x)\le M<\infty.
$$

Jensen's inequality says $\sum_kp_kf(x_k)\ge f(x_A)$. The difference

$$
\Delta=\sum_kp_kf(x_k)-f(x_A)
$$

is the Jensen gap, or defect. Theorem 1, printed p. 4, states that there is a coefficient $\mu\in[m,M]$ such that

$$
\Delta=\frac{\mu}{2}V.
$$

If $V=0$, the gap is zero and any permitted coefficient represents it; $2\Delta/V$ must not be evaluated. If $V>0$, the coefficient defined by that ratio is unique for the given $f$, values, and weights, even though intermediate Taylor points need not be unique. Strict convexity implies a positive gap whenever the supported observations differ.

The compact-support and smoothness assumptions make the curvature bounds finite. They are automatically available for a finite positive return sequence bounded away from zero after applying the theorem to negative logarithms. They should not be silently extended to an arbitrary population distribution with mass at ruin or with divergent log moments.

## Proof, reconstructed step by step

For each observation, expand around $x_A$. Taylor's theorem provides an intermediate point $c_k$ between $x_A$ and $x_k$ satisfying

$$
f(x_k)=f(x_A)+f'(x_A)(x_k-x_A)
       +\frac12f''(c_k)(x_k-x_A)^2.
$$

Multiply by $p_k$ and sum. The first-order term vanishes because $\sum_kp_k(x_k-x_A)=0$, leaving

$$
\Delta=\frac12\sum_kp_k(x_k-x_A)^2f''(c_k).
$$

Every squared deviation is nonnegative. Bounding each curvature therefore yields

$$
\frac m2V\le\Delta\le\frac M2V.
$$

For $V>0$, division immediately yields $\mu=2\Delta/V\in[m,M]$. Equivalently, $\mu$ is a weighted average of the intermediate curvatures, with weights proportional to $p_k(x_k-x_A)^2$. The paper uses the intermediate value theorem to express the same conclusion. This is an exact second-order remainder argument, not a truncation that discards higher-order terms.

The proof explains why “proportional to variance” needs qualification. The multiplier can change as the distribution changes. A statement that the gap rises linearly with variance across all distributions would incorrectly treat this multiplier as fixed. Becker explicitly notes that its value absorbs effects associated with higher central moments.

Remark 2 gives the pairwise representation

$$
\Delta=\frac\mu4\sum_j\sum_kp_jp_k(x_j-x_k)^2,
$$

using the exact identity that the double sum is $2V$. An affine function has zero gap. For $f(x)=x^2$, $f''=2$ everywhere, so $\mu=2$ and the gap is exactly $V$. Only in this quadratic example is the coefficient independent of the sample. These checks provide straightforward tests for a generic implementation of the defect formula.

## Applying the theorem to gross returns

For positive $x_k$, choose $f(x)=-\log x$. Then $f''(x)=x^{-2}$ and

$$
x_G=\prod_kx_k^{p_k},
\qquad
\Delta=\log x_A-\log x_G.
$$

Proposition 5, printed p. 7, therefore gives

$$
\log\frac{x_A}{x_G}=\frac\mu2V,
\qquad
x_G=x_A\exp\left(-\frac\mu2V\right).
$$

The arithmetic–geometric mean inequality follows because $\mu>0$ and $V\ge0$. Equality occurs exactly when all positive-weight observations coincide. Exponentiation turns an additive Jensen defect into a multiplicative discount on the arithmetic mean.

One source correction is necessary. Immediately after Proposition 5, the PDF prints a bound involving $x_N^{-1}$ and $x_1^{-1}$. Since the relevant derivative is **the second derivative** of $-\log x$, the correct bound for sorted positive observations is

$$
x_N^{-2}\le\mu\le x_1^{-2}.
$$

The source's inverse-first-power interval is inconsistent with its own Theorem 1. All bounds in this note use the squared inverse. Consequently, one obtains directly computable limits

$$
x_Ae^{-V/(2x_1^2)}
\le x_G\le
x_Ae^{-V/(2x_N^2)}.
$$

These bounds may be loose, especially when the minimum gross return is close to zero. Their value is that they follow without normality or a fitted return distribution. Knowing only mean and variance but not a positive lower support bound does not supply the same control near ruin.

## Financial specialization and exact implementation

For equal-length observations $r_t>-1$, set $x_t=1+r_t$ and $p_t=1/T$. Write

$$
R_A=\frac1T\sum_t r_t,
\qquad
R_G=\exp\left(\frac1T\sum_t\log(1+r_t)\right)-1,
$$

$$
\sigma^2=\frac1T\sum_t(r_t-R_A)^2.
$$

Adding one changes the mean but leaves variance unchanged. Equations (27)–(28), printed p. 9, become

$$
1+R_G=(1+R_A)e^{-\mu\sigma^2/2},
$$

$$
\log(1+R_A)-\log(1+R_G)=\frac\mu2\sigma^2.
$$

When variance is positive, calculate

$$
\mu=\frac{2[\log(1+R_A)-T^{-1}\sum_t\log(1+r_t)]}{\sigma^2}.
$$

This is an exact descriptive coefficient. It is not an out-of-sample estimator of future geometric return, because calculating it already requires the log-return information that determines the realized geometric mean. A hypothetical prediction using a fixed $\mu$ would need an additional stability assumption absent from the theorem.

For reproducibility, convert percentage observations to decimals, retain the common return interval, and use the divisor $T$ for descriptive variance. Validate positive gross returns before taking logarithms. Compute mean logs with `log1p` and recover geometric return with `expm1`. At exactly zero variance, report zero defect and leave the coefficient unidentified rather than divide by zero. At very small variance, cancellation can make a ratio of two small quantities numerically unstable; higher precision or a local curvature check is useful.

A transparent derived example uses returns 5%, 15%, and −5%. Their arithmetic mean is 5%, geometric mean is 4.681575%, and descriptive variance is $0.00666667$. The implied coefficient is approximately $0.911168$. Its curvature bounds are $1/1.15^2\approx0.75614$ and $1/0.95^2\approx1.10803$. It falls within the required interval and exactly reconciles the two log means. This example is calculated here to demonstrate the theorem; it is not one of Becker's reported empirical datasets.

## Which approximation is being made?

Becker first replaces the logarithms of the gross mean returns by their net returns:

$$
\log(1+R_A)\approx R_A,
\qquad
\log(1+R_G)\approx R_G.
$$

That gives $R_A-R_G\approx\mu\sigma^2/2$. Replacing the sample-specific coefficient by one produces the usual rule $R_A-R_G\approx\sigma^2/2$. These are separate assumptions. A small absolute approximation error can result from small variance even if $\mu$ is not especially close to one.

A second source correction occurs at the end of Section 4, printed p. 10: the displayed formula inserts a **minus** sign before half the variance while its left side is $R_A-R_G$. The intended sign is positive, as follows from the introduction, AM–GM, and Equation (28). Carrying that minus sign into an implementation would reverse the conclusion.

The assertion that a sufficiently small $R_A$, together with $R_A\ge R_G$, automatically makes the log approximation at $R_G$ at least as good is also too broad. It works under suitable nonnegative, small-return restrictions, but the inequality alone gives no lower bound keeping $R_G$ away from −1. As a derived counterexample, returns of +100% and −99% have arithmetic mean 0.5% but geometric mean $\sqrt{0.02}-1\approx-85.8579\%$. The first log approximation is good; the second is very poor. The exact theorem remains valid for these positive gross returns.

There is a useful way to avoid conflating the two approximation steps. Rearranging the exact exponential relation gives

$$
R_A-R_G=(1+R_A)\left(1-e^{-\mu\sigma^2/2}\right).
$$

For small $\mu\sigma^2$, a derived first-order expansion is

$$
R_A-R_G\approx(1+R_A)\frac\mu2\sigma^2.
$$

If all observations cluster around $R_A$, the effective curvature is close to $(1+R_A)^{-2}$, yielding the local rule $R_A-R_G\approx\sigma^2/[2(1+R_A)]$. The familiar half-variance result additionally requires $R_A$ near zero. These expansions clarify the mechanism but do not replace the exact calculation when all realized returns are known.

## Units, sampling frequency, and the role of time

The paper emphasizes that return means and squared returns have different units when returns are described as rates per unit time. In a practical discrete implementation, use dimensionless decimal holding-period returns at one specified frequency. Then the theorem is an identity among numbers. Changing to percentages rescales variance quadratically and changes the numerical coefficient; the factor one-half cannot be applied to percentage numbers as if they were decimals.

Likewise, changing from annual to monthly observations changes the sample, the mean, the variance, and the coefficient. Annual geometric return from monthly data is $(1+R_{G,m})^{12}-1$, while a conventional annualized arithmetic mean is $12R_{A,m}$. An annual variance estimate obtained by multiplying monthly variance by 12 requires its own assumptions if it is meant to describe a stochastic annual return. Becker's finite-sample theorem does not authorize mixing these annualization conventions or assuming independence.

The coefficient's dependence on support also makes the observation horizon relevant. A longer realized sample may reveal a more extreme negative return and widen the curvature interval. Large $T$ alone does not force the coefficient to one or eliminate approximation error. No central limit theorem is used in the proof.

## Empirical illustrations and replication limits

The financial comparison draws on the **Morningstar 2011 Classic Yearbook**, covering US asset-class annual returns during **1926–2010**. The paper discusses large-cap stocks, small-cap stocks, long-term government and corporate bonds, intermediate governments, and Treasury bills. It does not supply the annual vectors, a machine-readable table, code, uncertainty estimates, or a resampling design. These are illustrations using published descriptive statistics, not an investment backtest with formation dates, rebalancing, costs, and validation splits.

Becker reports coefficients of roughly 0.85 for large-cap stocks and the long-term corporate/government series, 0.75 for small caps, 0.58 for intermediate governments, and 2 for T-bills. The last value is discussed alongside a small absolute variance: half-variance is approximately five basis points, whereas the rounded arithmetic–geometric gap is approximately ten basis points. A ratio can be sensitive to rounding when both numerator and denominator are small. The local PDF does not provide enough underlying precision to independently reproduce or diagnose every coefficient.

The introduction's footnote reports that the usual half-variance approximation misses the small-cap gap by about 71 basis points, while other cited errors are around five to eight basis points. The conclusion reports errors around two basis points for long bonds, 20 basis points for large caps, and 60 basis points for small caps when comparing the adjusted approximation with the exact log relation; intermediate bonds and bills are effectively zero at available precision. These statements concern related but different comparisons. They should not be merged into one universal accuracy number.

To replicate the empirical section, obtain the exact cited yearbook series and its unrounded moments, verify total-return and frequency conventions, calculate descriptive variance with the stated divisor, and separately report the exact log defect, its inferred coefficient, the simple-return gap, and errors of both the coefficient-adjusted and ordinary half-variance approximations. The current source cannot support a claim that this reconstruction has already been completed from raw data.

## Interpretation and limits

The strongest result is deterministic: any finite positive gross-return sequence admits the exact exponential relationship, and curvature bounds restrict its coefficient. The weak point of the common heuristic is equally precise: suppressing this coefficient and linearizing logarithms discards information about the shape and scale of returns. Higher moments disappear from the displayed formula only because their influence is carried by a quantity computed from the whole sample.

This distinction prevents several misuses. The result does not prove that two portfolios with equal arithmetic means and variances have equal compound returns. It does not rank uncertain future investments without assumptions about future distributions. It does not ensure that reducing volatility while changing mean, skewness, costs, or leverage improves wealth growth. And it does not make a near-ruin observation harmless merely because its contribution to variance is finite. Its appropriate use is as an exact accounting relation, a proof of the direction of the realized mean gap, and a controlled explanation of when a convenient approximation may or may not be accurate.
