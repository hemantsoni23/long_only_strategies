# Robust Structure Without Predictability: The “Compass Rose” Pattern of the Stock Market

**Timothy Falcon Crack and Olivier Ledoit (1996).** *The Journal of Finance* 51(2), 751–762. Source: [original PDF](../../Finance/AssetReturn_Regularity_CrackLedoit_1996.pdf). All twelve article pages and nine figures were inspected visually because the local scan has essentially no usable text layer. This is a short methodological article; the note stops at its substantive content rather than expanding its exploratory suggestions into results the authors did not establish.

## Why this matters

A plot of today's return against tomorrow's return can contain striking geometric structure even when that structure offers no return forecast. Crack and Ledoit show that discrete price changes produce rays radiating from the origin in return scatterplots, resembling a compass rose. A sufficiently coarse **effective** price increment relative to ordinary price changes is central. The official minimum exchange tick alone is not a sufficient diagnostic.

The practical goal is to distinguish a market's observation grid from its economic dynamics. A researcher testing nonlinear dependence, estimating conditional volatility, or visually screening candidate signals can otherwise mistake arithmetic restrictions on observed prices for a predictable process. The article demonstrates the geometry in individual stocks, a cross-section, lagged and compounded returns, squared returns, and simulated prices. It does not estimate a profitable strategy or prove that every feature of the underlying return process is unpredictable.

Its innovative component is the transparent explanation connecting return ratios to small-integer price increments, together with counterexamples to the idea that a formal exchange tick necessarily determines whether the pattern appears. The paper's remarks about ARCH estimators and chaos tests are research hypotheses motivated by the geometry. They are not measured bias estimates or formal statistical power results.

## Price arithmetic and the ray mechanism

Let $P_t$ be the stock price at day $t$, and define the simple, not logarithmic, return

$$
R_t=\frac{P_t-P_{t-1}}{P_{t-1}}.
$$

Let $h$ denote the effective price increment and write $P_t-P_{t-1}=h n_t$, where $n_t$ is an integer. If daily price changes are small relative to price levels, the two adjacent return denominators are approximately equal. Equation (1), p. 754, then gives

$$
\frac{R_{t+1}}{R_t}
\approx\frac{P_{t+1}-P_t}{P_t-P_{t-1}}
=\frac{n_{t+1}}{n_t}.
$$

The exact identity, useful for understanding the approximation, is

$$
\frac{R_{t+1}}{R_t}
=\frac{n_{t+1}}{n_t}\frac{P_{t-1}}{P_t}
=\frac{n_{t+1}/n_t}{1+R_t},\qquad n_t\ne0.
$$

The last equality is a direct algebraic expansion, not an additional equation estimated by the authors. It shows how denominator differences perturb each ideal ray. A zero price change gives an axis observation; it must be plotted directly rather than passed through this ratio formula.

When most daily changes are only a few effective ticks, ratios such as $1$, $2$, $1/2$, and their negatives occur frequently. Points $(R_t,R_{t+1})$ concentrate near lines with those slopes. The rays need not be evenly spaced in angle: rational slopes generated by small integers are not a uniform angular grid. The compass analogy describes an appearance, not an exact equally spaced construction.

If the stock price stayed within a narrow range, the increments would instead generate recognizable grid points at approximately $(h n_t/P,h n_{t+1}/P)$. Variation in the price level changes the scale $h/P$ and moves observations along those directions. Over a long sample, this spreads grid points radially into visible rays. The source calls this a smudging effect. Thus changes in price level help make the ray structure visible even though small adjacent-period price changes are needed to keep individual ray directions sharply defined.

The paper lists three conditions for a clear pattern: price changes are small relative to the price; typical changes contain only a small number of effective ticks; and the price level varies substantially over the sample. These are qualitative conditions for a visual diagnostic. The article acknowledges the subjectivity of deciding whether a pattern is clearly visible, so they should not be converted into a numerical necessary-and-sufficient theorem without further definitions. No threshold for “few ticks,” angular concentration, or adequate sample length is calibrated.

## Baseline illustration and display choices

The baseline security is Weyerhaeuser. Figure 1 reproduces the appearance of a conventional return scatterplot over January 2, 1986–December 30, 1988. It has 758 observations, of which six fall outside the displayed bounds of approximately −0.10 to 0.10 on each axis. The comparatively large plotting symbols and wide axis range obscure fine structure.

Figure 2 uses December 6, 1963–December 31, 1993, small point markers, and axes from −0.03 to 0.03. It contains 7,566 points, with 1,212 outside the plotted window. The compass rose becomes visually apparent. This is a change in sample, plot scale, and marker size simultaneously; the article does not isolate each display choice in a controlled factorial experiment. A reproduction should preserve all three changes before attributing visibility to any single one.

These plots also warn against treating a displayed window as the estimation sample. Points beyond the axes are omitted from the picture, not necessarily from the underlying return series. Clipping axes at three percent removes many observations visually; it is not a stated return-winsorization rule. The paper gives counts outside each figure, which are useful checks on the raw sample and plotting code.

The argument for lack of a forecasting implication is structural. Integer price increments restrict where a point can lie, but do not supply the conditional probability of an upward rather than downward next price change. A cloud can exhibit rays without a profitable conditional mean forecast. The authors explicitly leave open the possibility that other, subtler features in the data might possess predictive content. The title should therefore not be read as a proof that stock returns are independent or that the figure contains no economic information of any kind.

## Cross-sectional evidence and the effective tick

Figure 3 replaces a long time series with a cross-section of 2,123 NYSE stocks. Its horizontal coordinate is each stock's return on October 19, 1993, and its vertical coordinate the return on October 20. Of these points, 469 lie outside the roughly ±3% display window. The ray pattern survives. Because each stock contributes only one observation, the pattern is not dependent on a long autoregressive history within the same stock. Differences in price levels across stocks provide the radial scaling that changes in price levels provide in the time-series example.

Figure 4 is Berkshire Hathaway over October 14, 1976–December 31, 1993: 4,229 points, 405 outside the ±3% window. The stock's price was around \$20,000 in late 1994, so an official one-eighth-dollar tick was tiny relative to its price and usual price moves. Nevertheless, the pattern is visible. Prices effectively clustered around much larger increments, approximately \$25, so normal changes involved only a few effective units. The authors mention comparable appearances for other high-priced stocks, including CBS and Capital Cities/ABC, without presenting an exhaustive security-by-security table.

The example does not show that all high-priced securities share an identical increment, or that clustering is constant through time. It shows why official exchange rules cannot substitute for inspecting the increments actually used by traders. An exchange could permit fine increments while participants voluntarily transact on a coarse grid. The relevant discreteness could persist even without a binding official rule.

Figure 5 gives the converse caution using the London afternoon gold fixing in US dollars, January 3, 1978–November 17, 1994. There are 4,015 observations, of which 339 lie outside the displayed window. Despite discrete prices and clustering at quarters and dimes, the data do not show an equally clear compass rose. Typical gold changes contain many ticks relative to the quoted nickel increment. Many possible integer ratios crowd together, making the individual rays hard to distinguish.

The article does not identify whether differences between gold and equities arise primarily from asset characteristics or from market structure, such as the fixing mechanism versus a continuous auction. It explicitly treats that economic explanation as unfinished research. Nor does it estimate how much gold-return variance or serial-covariance bias remains. The proposed absence-of-pattern diagnostic suggests such effects might be small in that case, but the authors say further verification is needed.

Small portfolios and broad indices provide another boundary. Aggregating returns across securities usually makes the attainable return grid finer relative to typical aggregate changes, and the pattern becomes difficult to discern. The paper notes its absence from stock indices and from portfolios containing more than a very small number of stocks. Its intuitive equal-weight scaling discussion is not a theorem for arbitrary prices, weights, rebalancing rules, and correlated increments. Those details would matter for an exact portfolio-grid derivation.

## Changing lag, compounding horizon, and transformation

Two operations that might casually be called “using weekly returns” have different effects and must be separated.

In Figure 6 the returns remain **daily** returns but are plotted five trading days apart: $(R_t,R_{t+5})$. The Weyerhaeuser sample remains December 6, 1963–December 31, 1993, with 7,562 pairs and 1,233 beyond the displayed bounds. The pattern becomes fuzzier because price levels can change more between the two return denominators. The paper says some structure remains even at a one-year lag, though it does not plot that result. This is not evidence of one-year economic forecasting power.

In Figure 7, each coordinate is a **compounded five-day return**. The horizontal interval covers days $t$ through $t+4$ and the vertical interval days $t+5$ through $t+9$. The two coordinates within a pair refer to adjacent, nonoverlapping five-day blocks, while successive plotted pairs roll through the data and therefore overlap one another. The figure contains 7,558 pairs, with 1,518 outside axes of approximately ±5%.

To implement this faithfully, use

$$
R_t^{(5)}=\prod_{j=0}^{4}(1+R_{t+j})-1,
$$

and plot $(R_t^{(5)},R_{t+5}^{(5)})$. This notation makes the caption's construction explicit. The source reports a visible weekly pattern, an indistinct monthly pattern, and no quarterly pattern. Longer cumulative price changes involve more ticks, generating more possible slopes; that is a different explanation from simply widening the gap between two daily returns.

Figure 8 plots squared daily returns, $(R_t^2,R_{t+1}^2)$, using 7,566 Weyerhaeuser pairs, with 1,626 outside the plotted window. All signs are folded into the nonnegative quadrant. The directions are approximately squared integer ratios, $(n_{t+1}/n_t)^2$, and small asymmetries in the original rays produce closely spaced pairs of rays after squaring. The axes extend to roughly $7\times10^{-4}$, so their units are squared decimal returns, not percent returns.

This construction motivates the authors' concern about regressions of squared returns on their lags, such as those used in ARCH estimation. But they do not fit an ARCH model, compare estimators, quantify a bias, or show that observed volatility clustering is an artifact. A reproducible extension would require choosing a latent volatility process, a quantization rule, an estimator, and a simulation design. Those are inputs for future work, not findings supplied by this paper.

## Simulation: a visible pattern without a designed signal

Figure 9 uses simulated daily stock prices and plots the three-dimensional triples $(R_t,R_{t+1},R_{t+2})$. The caption supplies the following inputs:

- 10,000 daily returns drawn from a normal distribution with mean 0.0005 and standard deviation 0.012.
- Starting stock price of \$5.
- Prices truncated to the nearest one eighth of a dollar.
- A two-for-one split when the price doubles from its starting value.
- 9,998 plotted return triples, of which 36 lie outside the displayed box.

The resulting points show a three-dimensional starburst. Independent Gaussian innovations combined with a discrete price-recording mechanism are sufficient to produce this kind of visible low-dimensional geometry. This is a constructive counterexample to inferring an economic forecasting mechanism from the pattern alone.

The caption leaves several implementation details unresolved. “Truncated to the nearest” is not an unambiguous computational specification: truncation toward a grid boundary and rounding to the closest grid value differ. The random seed, generator, exact recursion between latent and recorded prices, and adjustment of returns on split dates are not given. It is also necessary to specify how the doubling threshold is reset after a split. A faithful reconstruction should disclose these choices and compare the visual output, rather than claim bit-for-bit reproduction of Figure 9.

One transparent implementation would maintain a latent price updated by the simulated return, transform it onto an explicit tick grid, and compute returns from the recorded prices after accounting for splits. That is a reconstruction choice, not code supplied by the authors. Feeding rounded prices back into the next latent-price update is a different process and should not be silently substituted. Similarly, a simulated −50% split return caused by omitting corporate-action adjustment would be an implementation artifact rather than the intended discreteness effect.

The paper connects this result to tests looking for low-dimensional organization in histories of returns, mentioning the BDS test. It suggests discreteness could induce spurious findings interpreted as chaos. This is deliberately a caution: a rejection by a dependence test does not uniquely identify deterministic chaos, and the article provides neither rejection frequencies nor a theorem about a particular test's size under quantization. Its demonstrated result is the starburst geometry, not a calibrated testing correction.

## Replication scope, limitations, and implications

A minimal reproduction should begin with unrounded source prices where available, an explicit corporate-action convention, and a separate record of the effective price increments. It should compute simple returns, construct the exact pairings for each figure, and match sample endpoints, point counts, marker sizes, and axis limits. Zero returns should remain as axis points. Dropping them would remove an important part of the discrete structure. Log returns may have a similar small-return appearance, but they do not satisfy the article's exact simple-return algebra and should be treated as a sensitivity analysis.

The paper gives the displayed instruments, dates, figure counts, and core simulation parameters. It does not provide a formal universe filter for the cross-section, a full data-cleaning protocol, transaction-cost assumptions, a code archive, or an objective statistic for deciding whether a compass rose is present. It also supplies no standard errors or hypothesis test for comparing the strength of the pattern across instruments. Its evidence is geometric and illustrative rather than an estimated causal model of trading behavior.

For a quantitative researcher, the useful diagnostic is to compare ordinary price changes with effective increments, not merely to read the exchange's tick rule. If a typical move spans very few increments, return scatterplots and nonlinear transformations can inherit strong lattice structure. If moves span many increments, individual rays become crowded or invisible even though observations remain discrete. This explains why a coarse-looking visual pattern can be robust across samples and transformations without constituting an exploitable prediction.

The broader implication is methodological restraint. A visually ordered state space, a predictive conditional mean, a correctly specified volatility model, and an implementable trading strategy are different claims. Crack and Ledoit establish the first through simple arithmetic and examples, and show why it cannot by itself establish the others.
