## Why this matters

The chapter gives several foundations for allocating the value of a cooperative game. It begins with the unique transferable-utility value generated by average marginal contributions, explains alternative axiomatizations, and then develops the nontransferable-utility Shapley correspondence. It is useful for understanding exactly what an allocation rule promises. A Shapley allocation is an attribution under a specified coalition-value function; it is not automatically a competitive outcome, a core allocation, or a causal treatment effect.

## Transferable utility: objects and normalization

Let $N$ be a finite player set with $n$ members. A transferable-utility game assigns a real worth $v(S)$ to each coalition $S\subseteq N$, with $v(\varnothing)=0$. A value assigns a payoff vector $\phi(v)\in\mathbb R^N$. Efficiency requires $\sum_i\phi_i(v)=v(N)$. Additivity requires $\phi(v+w)=\phi(v)+\phi(w)$.

For nonempty $T$, the unanimity game $U_T$ is worth one when $T\subseteq S$ and zero otherwise. Under the chapter's normalization, the game $aU_T$ allocates $a/|T|$ to each member of $T$ and zero to everyone else. This treats the members needed to unlock the unanimity payoff symmetrically, including when $a$ is negative.

The first theorem states that the value satisfying these requirements is uniquely

$
\phi_i(v)=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\pi}\left[v(P_i^\pi\cup\{i\})-v(P_i^\pi)\right],
$

where the sum ranges over all orderings of the players and $P_i^\pi$ is the set preceding player $i$. Each ordering imagines players entering a coalition one at a time; the allocation is the player's average incremental worth. The random ordering is a symmetry device, not an empirical assertion about actual bargaining arrival times.

Grouping permutations by predecessor set gives an equivalent implementation formula:

$
\phi_i(v)=\sum_{S\subseteq N\setminus\{i\}}
\frac{|S|!(n-|S|-1)!}{n!}\big[v(S\cup\{i\})-v(S)\big].
$

The coefficient counts the orderings that put exactly $S$ before $i$. For each ordering, marginal contributions telescope to $v(N)$, proving efficiency. They are linear in $v$, proving additivity. In a unanimity game, a member earns the full payoff exactly when it is the last member of $T$ to arrive, an event with probability $1/|T|$; outsiders never unlock it.

## Why uniqueness holds and how to reconstruct the value

There are $2^n-1$ nonempty coalitions and the same dimension of the space of normalized games. The chapter proves that the unanimity games are linearly independent, hence form a basis. Additivity and the unanimity normalization determine the value on every basis expansion, so there can be only one such rule. This is an exact finite-dimensional argument, not an approximation based on many simulated permutations.

For implementation, the basis coefficients can be calculated by Möbius inversion:

$
\delta(T)=\sum_{U\subseteq T}(-1)^{|T|-|U|}v(U),\qquad
v(S)=\sum_{T\subseteq S}\delta(T),\qquad
\phi_i(v)=\sum_{T\ni i}\frac{\delta(T)}{|T|}.
$

These explicit inversion expressions are an algebraic reconstruction of the chapter's basis argument. They are useful for testing an implementation against the permutation formula. They still require a specification or oracle for coalition worth. The chapter provides no empirical coalition-estimation procedure, error model, or prescribed Monte Carlo stopping rule. Exhaustive enumeration becomes demanding as the number of players grows.

## Examples and economic distinctions

In a three-player majority game, any two players produce one unit and each receives $1/3$. The core is empty: satisfying every winning pair would require total payoffs exceeding the grand coalition's unit worth. Thus a well-defined Shapley allocation does not imply coalition stability.

In the one-seller, two-buyer example, a coalition produces one unit only if it includes the seller and at least one buyer. The seller receives $2/3$, each buyer $1/6$. The seller is pivotal when arriving after at least one buyer, which occurs in four of six orders. Each buyer is pivotal only in the ordering with seller first and that buyer second. The core instead allocates the full unit to the seller. The chapter therefore separates the marginal-contribution bargaining principle from the no-blocking principle.

For weighted majority voting with weights $(2,1,1,1)$ and quota three, the large player has Shapley power $1/2$ and each small player $1/6$. Voting power is a probability of being pivotal, not the fraction of nominal votes. In the next example, weights $(3,3,1,1,1)$ under majority rule give the large players $3/10$ each and the small players $2/15$ each. If the three small players merge, the resulting three equal blocks each receive $1/3$, below the small players' previous combined $2/5$. Aggregation can therefore reduce total Shapley power; no general merger advantage follows from the rule.

The glove example makes the difference from a competitive scarcity allocation vivid. With one million left gloves and one million plus one right gloves, pairs are productive and unmatched gloves are not. The core gives all worth to scarce left gloves. The reported Shapley aggregate allocation is approximately 500,428 to left-glove holders and 499,572 to right-glove holders. Those are the chapter's large-market example numbers, not results of a simulation reproduced for this note. Marginal-contribution averaging can value plentiful participants even when competitive scarcity pricing does not.

## Alternative foundations

The potential formulation assigns a scalar $P$ to every subgame so that a player's payoff is the difference between the potential with and without that player. With $P(\varnothing)=0$, the defining identity is

$
\sum_{i\in N}\left[P(N)-P(N\setminus\{i\})\right]=v(N).
$

For a fixed game, this implies the recursive computation

$
P(N)=\frac{v(N)+\sum_i P(N\setminus\{i\})}{|N|},\qquad
\phi_i(v)=P(N)-P(N\setminus\{i\}).
$

The denominator in the recursion is the size of the subgame currently evaluated, not always the size of the original grand coalition. This provides another exact finite-game route to the same allocation.

Young's characterization replaces additivity with strong monotonicity: if a player's marginal contribution is at least as high in every coalition, its value cannot be lower. Together with efficiency and symmetry this characterizes the Shapley value. The chapter states this theorem without supplying a complete proof. Neyman's result shows that a characterization need not assume additivity across the entire space of all games. It is enough to work on the additive group generated by the game's restrictions $v_S(T)=v(S\cap T)$ with suitable axioms. These results address the domain and normative force of the characterization; they are not alternative empirical models.

## Nontransferable utility: the correspondence

When utility cannot be freely transferred between players, scalar coalition worth is replaced by a feasible-utility set $V(S)\subseteq\mathbb R^S$. The chapter works with nonempty, convex, comprehensive proper sets and additional regularity on the grand-coalition frontier, including smoothness and a strictly Pareto frontier. Its weak-domination condition links proper-coalition opportunities to the grand coalition. These restrictions matter: they are not satisfied automatically by any arbitrary collection of estimated utility outcomes.

For strictly positive utility weights $\lambda_i$, define the auxiliary transferable-utility game

$
v_\lambda(S)=\sup_{z\in V(S)}\sum_{i\in S}\lambda_i z_i.
$

At weights for which the support values are finite, a Shapley payoff $x$ lies on the grand-coalition boundary and satisfies

$
\lambda_i x_i=\phi_i(v_\lambda)\quad\text{for every }i.
$

The weights convert utilities into a common supporting scale. Different supporting weights may produce different solutions, so the output is a correspondence, potentially containing multiple payoff vectors. It should not be described as the unique payoff vector of every nontransferable-utility game.

The domain used for the characterization consists of games already known to have at least one Shapley value. Consequently the axiomatic result does not prove universal existence for all feasible-set games. It is a uniqueness result for a rule on that domain.

## What the nontransferable-utility axioms establish

The rule must be nonempty, Pareto efficient, correctly normalized on unanimity games, invariant to replacing feasible sets by their closures, and covariant under positive coordinatewise changes in utility scale. Additivity is conditional: sums of selected outcomes that lie on the frontier of the sum game must be selected there. It is not unrestricted equality between the correspondence of a sum and the sum of two correspondences.

An independence-of-irrelevant-alternatives requirement concerns shrinking the grand-coalition set while keeping proper-coalition sets fixed: previously selected outcomes that remain feasible must survive. With this condition the chapter's fourth theorem characterizes the Shapley correspondence uniquely. Without it, the fifth theorem says every correspondence satisfying the other axioms is contained in the Shapley correspondence. Thus the latter is maximal under those axioms, not necessarily the only rule or the smallest possible set of selections.

The chapter supplies characterizations and intuition rather than an executable general solver for the weight equations, numerical convergence guarantees, or an applied dataset. To use it in a quantitative allocation problem, one must first define what each coalition can achieve, establish the relevant regularity, and decide whether transferable utility is defensible. A risk-attribution or forecast-explanation application is an interpretation of that chosen game, not a financial experiment reported in this source. The exact marginal-contribution formulas then provide an auditable allocation, while the core examples explain why attribution, bargaining stability, and economic causation remain separate questions.
