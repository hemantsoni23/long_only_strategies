# The Hedge Fund Game: Incentives, Excess Returns, and Piggy-Backing

**Dean P. Foster and H. Peyton Young.** Oxford Department of Economics Discussion Paper 378. The outer cover says January 2008; the internal title page says November 2007, **revised 2 March 2008**. This note follows that local working-paper version, not an assumed later publication.

**Source:** [HedgeFunds_FosterPeytonYoung_2007.pdf](../../Finance/HedgeFunds_FosterPeytonYoung_2007.pdf), 25 PDF pages including two covers, abstract, complete proof, numerical illustration, contract extensions, and references. Printed p. 11 is PDF p. 12.

## Why this matters

A long series of modest positive benchmark-relative returns can be generated by bearing a rare, severe loss rather than by possessing investment skill. When fees reward realized success without charging the manager symmetrically for failure, a manager can be well paid before that rare loss arrives. Foster and Young formalize this identification and incentive problem: a contract that rewards a genuinely skilled manager may also reward a manager who merely imitates the skilled manager's successful return path with high probability.

The distinctive contribution is a path-mimicking argument that applies to general nonnegative fee schedules, rather than only to a particular convex “two and twenty” contract. It separates **alignment**, making the manager care about investor outcomes, from **separation**, distinguishing skilled from unskilled managers. Deferring fees, requiring a personal investment, and imposing penalties can affect alignment without automatically solving separation.

The printed theorem requires mathematical qualification. Its deterministic-benchmark, fair-lottery mechanism is straightforward, but the stochastic-benchmark proof incorrectly treats an event defined from the benchmark as independent of its realized path. It also changes the effective payoff units of a digital claim without saying so. The note presents the authors' claim, derives the valid normalized special case, and identifies the unsupported extension explicitly. These are source-specific proof issues, not evidence against the broader economic concern about hidden tail exposure.

## The performance and compensation model

There are $T$ discrete periods. $Y_t>0$ is the gross return of an exogenous tradable benchmark. $X_t\ge0$ is the manager's multiplicative contribution relative to that benchmark, so the gross fund return in period $t$ is $X_tY_t$. The gross terminal wealth per initial dollar, before any fee withdrawals, is

$$
R_T=\prod_{t=1}^T X_tY_t.
$$

The paper calls $\prod_tX_t-1$ the total excess return. This is a multiplicative relative-wealth quantity, not the sum of additive annual regression alphas. A benchmark-relative factor $X_t=1+\alpha$ corresponds to simple fund return $(1+\alpha)Y_t-1$.

A contract specifies a fee $\phi_t(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)$ per dollar present at the beginning of period $t$, constrained by

$$
0\le\phi_t(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)\le x_ty_t.
$$

Net investor wealth after $t$ periods is

$$
r_t(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)=\prod_{s=1}^t[x_sy_s-\phi_s(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)],\qquad r_0=1.
$$

Total fees per initial dollar are therefore

$$
\phi(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)=\sum_{t=1}^T\phi_t(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)r_{t-1}(\mathbf x,\mathbf y).
$$

This accounting matters: a percentage fee on the remaining fund is not the same as a fixed fee on initial assets. The model assumes no premature withdrawals in this representation. The authors do not require fees to be increasing in performance; nonnegativity is the essential condition for their lower bound. High-water marks and deferred schedules can be represented through dependence on the return path, provided the payment rule and timing are specified consistently.

The formal example on printed p. 9 is

$$
\phi_t=0.02x_ty_t+0.20[x_t-1]^+y_t.
$$

Here the management charge is 2% of end-period gross assets and the incentive fee is benchmark-relative. This differs slightly from the opening numerical illustration, which subtracts a 2% charge measured against beginning-period capital. A reproduction should choose the relevant convention rather than silently mixing the two examples.

A manager has no ability if $E[X_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]=1$ for every relevant prior history. A constant-ability manager satisfies $E[X_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]=1+\alpha$. The theorem actually compares the imitator with the stronger special case of a manager delivering a prescribed path $x_t\ge1$ with certainty. It does not solve for the optimal strategy of a general manager with stochastic skill, wealth effects, or competition for inflows.

The authors' performance measure is the manager's **take**:

$$
\tau=\frac{E[\phi(\mathbf X,\mathbf Y)]}{E[R_T]}.
$$

It is a ratio of expectations, not an expected fee-to-return ratio. The latter can fail to exist when terminal fund wealth is zero. The numerator sums fees at their payment dates without an explicit reinvestment or discounting convention, whereas the denominator is terminal gross wealth. Accordingly, take should be interpreted as the paper's defined comparison statistic, not a universal present-value fee measure.

## The opening illustration and its limitations

Section 2 starts with a fund of **\$100 million**, a **4%** safe annual return, and a binary liability that pays **\$1 million** if a specified event occurs. The event probability is **10%**. The paper assumes each liability can be sold for **\$100,000**, its undiscounted probability-weighted payoff. If proceeds can themselves collateralize additional liabilities, the number $c$ measured in millions satisfies

$$
c=100+0.10c,\qquad c=111.\overline1.
$$

The collateral grows to approximately **\$115.56 million**. If the event does not happen, that is the pre-fee fund value. If it does, paying the liabilities leaves approximately **\$4.44 million**, before management charges. Conditional on avoiding the event, the authors calculate a net annual gain of about **11.2%**: roughly 15.5% gross, less 2% management fees, less 20% of performance above the 4% benchmark. With repeated conditional 90% survival, the probability of five years without a crash is $0.9^5\approx59.0\%$. They report expected five-year managerial fees above **\$19 million**.

This illustration communicates the asymmetry: successful paths compound, fees can be collected before a terminal disaster, and the most damaging liability may remain unrealized throughout the observed track record. It is not calibrated evidence that a particular fund uses such positions. The article's references to short out-of-the-money puts concern related payoff shapes, not a demonstrated prevalence estimate.

The illustration is not an arbitrage-free option-pricing calculation. A cash payoff due in one year generally has a discounted risk-neutral price, not its undiscounted physical expected value. Even if the relevant pricing probability were 10%, a 4% discount rate would change the price. Physical and pricing probabilities can also differ because the loss state carries systematic risk. The theorem below is clearest in discounted benchmark units with the fair-lottery pricing assumption explicitly imposed. It should not be implemented by treating any observed option premium as an objective event probability.

## A valid deterministic-benchmark reconstruction

Normalize wealth by a deterministic positive benchmark, so its gross growth is one in the chosen units. Fix a target factor $x_t\ge1$ in each period and assume the manager can access a fully collateralized, fair binary payoff with event probability

$$
q_t=1-\frac1{x_t}.
$$

“Fair” here means that the price in normalized units equals $q_t$ for a payoff of one normalized unit. This is an additional market/pricing assumption, not a consequence of continuity of an arbitrary asset's return density.

Suppose current fund wealth is $w$. Selling $c$ such liabilities raises $cq_t$ and requires collateral $c$. The budget equation is

$$
w+cq_t=c,\qquad c=x_tw.
$$

At settlement, normalized gross fund wealth is $x_tw$ if the liability is not triggered, and zero otherwise. Thus the return factor is

$$
X_t=\begin{cases}x_t,&\text{with conditional probability }1/x_t,\\0,&\text{with conditional probability }1-1/x_t,\end{cases}
\qquad E[X_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]=1.
$$

After failure the fund is absorbed at zero. If the conditional survival probability at each still-active date is $1/x_t$, the chain rule gives

$$
P(\text{all periods survive})=\prod_{t=1}^T\frac1{x_t}=\frac1A,
\qquad A=\prod_{t=1}^Tx_t.
$$

Independence across dates is sufficient but not necessary; these conditional probabilities are enough. Pre-fee discounted terminal wealth is $A$ on survival and zero on failure, with expectation one. Conditional on complete survival, the manager produces exactly the target path, so any return-path contract pays the same fees as to the skilled comparator. Fees received on paths that eventually fail are nonnegative. Therefore

$$
E[\phi_{unskilled}]\ge\frac{\phi(\mathbf x)}{A}.
$$

The skilled comparator has gross normalized terminal wealth $A$ and fee total $\phi(\mathbf x)$, hence take $\phi(\mathbf x)/A$. The imitator's expected gross terminal wealth is one, so its take is at least as large. This establishes the economic mechanism in the normalized deterministic-benchmark environment.

The result concerns compensation **relative to gross expected wealth**. It does not say that the imitator always earns a higher expected fee in currency than the skilled manager, or that its investor outcome is superior. For a positive reward, the imitator's lower bound is only a fraction $1/A$ of the skilled manager's fee. Its worse investor performance makes the take ratio comparable.

## Why the printed stochastic-benchmark proof does not establish its claim

Theorem 1, printed pp. 11–14, also allows a stochastic benchmark with a continuous conditional density and finite positive expected terminal wealth. The proof chooses a threshold $s_t$ so that

$$
P(Y_t\le s_t\mid Y_1,\ldots,Y_{t-1})=1/x_t,
$$

then defines the digital exercise event as $Z_t=\mathbf1\{Y_t>s_t\}$. The next step claims that target returns occur with probability $1/x_t$ **for every realized value of $Y_t$**, and ultimately with probability $1/A$ irrespective of the complete benchmark path. That does not follow. Conditional on the realized $Y_t$, $Z_t$ is already known. It is not a fresh independent lottery.

A concrete one-period counterexample uses an everywhere continuous density. Let $Y=1+B$, where $B$ has density $6b(1-b)$ on $[0,1]$ and zero outside, and choose $x=2$. Symmetry gives the threshold $s=1.5$. Even granting the proof's desired payoff factor $X=2\mathbf1\{Y\le1.5\}$,

$$
E[X]=1,\qquad E[Y]=\frac32,
$$

but

$$
E[XY]=2\int_0^{1/2}(1+b)6b(1-b)\,db=\frac{21}{16}<\frac32.
$$

Thus no ability as defined by $E[X]=1$ does not imply unchanged expected gross wealth when $X$ and $Y$ are dependent. Equation (9)'s factorization fails. Likewise, fees conditional on survival can depend on a selected distribution of benchmark returns, so the expectation in equation (8) cannot generally be factored into unconditional expected skilled fees times $1/A$.

There is a separate settlement issue. The printed digital pays one cash unit on exercise. If collateral $c$ is invested in a benchmark with gross return $Y$, terminal wealth is $cY-cZ$, not $cY(1-Z)$. On exercise it equals $c(Y-1)$, which is neither necessarily zero nor necessarily nonnegative. To obtain the asserted multiplicative payoff, the claim would need to pay benchmark units, with a different pricing specification. The proof also treats positivity of each $Y_t$ as following from its displayed expected-product condition; positive expected product alone is not sufficient, so positivity should be stated separately.

One sufficient repair would provide an independent fair lottery, claims settling in benchmark units, and a pricing convention consistent with those units, with survival independent of the full benchmark path relevant to the contract. Those are stronger assumptions than the paper states and are not generated by choosing a threshold of the benchmark itself. The source's stochastic theorem should therefore be retained as an asserted result with a proof gap, rather than promoted to a general no-arbitrage theorem about all derivative markets.

## Quantifying the track-record problem

For a constant target relative factor $1+\alpha$, the valid survival calculation is

$$
p_{survive}(\alpha,T)=(1+\alpha)^{-T}.
$$

The paper's Table 1 reports the following rounded probabilities:

| Annual target relative excess | Five years | Ten years | Twenty years |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1% | 95% | 91% | 82% |
| 3% | 86% | 74% | 55% |
| 5% | 78% | 61% | 38% |
| 10% | 62% | 39% | 15% |
| 20% | 40% | 16% | 3% |

At a 3% target over ten years, an unskilled manager can receive at least about **74% of the skilled manager's expected monetary reward** in the normalized setting. A modest-looking alpha is especially difficult to distinguish from a rare-loss strategy using a finite successful history. Raising the target makes a clean history less likely; it does not create skill.

Increasing reporting frequency does not remove this mathematical possibility. If daily target growth is $(1+\alpha)^{1/365}$, multiplying the corresponding daily survival probabilities over $365T$ days again gives $(1+\alpha)^{-T}$. This assumes the requisite fair contracts can be reset at the finer frequency without costs or market constraints. Daily holdings disclosure is different from daily return reporting: it can reveal the exposure itself, an information channel absent from the theorem.

A reproducible numerical check needs only a target sequence and uniform random draws. At each date, continue the target path when the draw is at most $1/x_t$; otherwise set wealth to zero and stop. Apply the contract to actual net wealth at every date, retain fees collected before failure, and compare Monte Carlo survival to $1/A$. The pre-fee terminal mean should converge to one in discounted units. An implementation producing a mean above one has mixed payoff or premium conventions. The paper does not supply a Monte Carlo experiment, seed, confidence interval, or proprietary dataset; these checks are a reconstruction of its probability model.

## Deferred fees: changing timing does not create identification

Section 4 first considers a nonnegative terminal reward $g(R_T)$, with no reward for benchmark-relative underperformance. In the deterministic normalized case, choosing target terminal wealth $A$ gives expected fee at least $g(A)/A$. The manager can seek the best such target. This is a lower-bound construction, not proof that the resulting target is an equilibrium strategy once risk preferences, entry costs, and fundraising are modeled.

The numerical example assumes a ten-year horizon, 20% of final gross excess wealth as the fee, a benchmark that doubles, and a 3% annual relative target. Successful terminal fee is

$$
0.20\times2F[(1.03)^{10}-1]\approx0.138F,
$$

where $F$ is initial assets. Survival probability is approximately 74%, and failure gives zero terminal incentive fee. The source describes benchmark doubling in expected-value terms; the displayed deterministic successful payout is exact if benchmark doubling is deterministic, or an appropriate conditional expectation under an independently repaired stochastic model. It is not a fixed successful payout for an unrestricted stochastic benchmark.

Deferral removes early irreversible fee collection, but a nonnegative reward still has option-like upside without equivalent downside. It can improve alignment while leaving successful-path imitation attractive. The paper does not quantify how legal clawbacks, financing costs, or limits on eligible derivative exposures change this calculation.

## Manager co-investment and the relevant wealth comparison

Let the manager own fraction $\theta\in(0,1)$ of initial fund capital and normalize the safe benchmark away. For a skilled manager delivering $A=\prod_tx_t$, the source's terminal-wealth comparison is

$$
W_S=\theta A+(1-\theta)\phi(\mathbf x).
$$

The corresponding piggy-back expectation, under the simplified terminal accounting, is

$$
E[W_U]=\theta+(1-\theta)\frac{\phi(\mathbf x)}{A}=\frac{W_S}{A}.
$$

This identity shows that requiring a stake does not by itself destroy the imitation ratio. It does not imply the stake is behaviorally irrelevant. The manager may dislike the risk of losing personal wealth, may have concentrated outside exposures, or may face a binding wealth constraint. Conversely, hidden offsetting positions outside the fund could undo apparent co-investment. The source raises these possibilities but does not estimate them or solve a risk-averse optimal-control problem.

The exact equality uses the source's simplified wealth representation; under a general interim fee schedule with additional fees on crash paths, the nonnegative-fee argument naturally provides a lower bound rather than an equality based solely on the fully successful path. A practical contract analysis must specify how fees are accumulated and whether the manager pays fees on the manager's own stake.

## Penalties and the participation constraint

The final extension permits negative compensation, backed by an escrow deposit of $\delta$ per initial dollar of fund capital. The largest possible penalty is $\delta$. A skilled manager that could earn the same target factor $A$ on its own money must be compensated for tying that capital up in escrow. Its participation condition is

$$
\delta+\phi(\mathbf x)>\delta A
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\phi(\mathbf x)>\delta(A-1).
$$

For an unskilled risk-neutral manager, the successful path occurs with probability $1/A$. Even imposing the maximum penalty on every failed path, expected incremental payoff relative to retaining the escrow is

$$
\frac{\phi(\mathbf x)}{A}-\delta\left(1-\frac1A\right).
$$

It is positive precisely when $\phi(\mathbf x)>\delta(A-1)$, the same condition. If some failures incur a smaller penalty, imitation is even more attractive. Hence, within this normalized model, a contract that compensates the skilled manager for the escrow's opportunity cost also leaves a profitable imitation opportunity for a risk-neutral unskilled manager.

Equation (14) calls this incremental expression “end-wealth,” although it omits the initial escrow principal. Adding $\delta$ gives total expected wealth; comparing that total with the outside option $\delta$ recovers equation (15). The participation inequality survives this labeling correction.

The result relies on enforceable escrow, negligible strategy setup costs, the skilled manager's ability to apply the same scalable skill to personal capital, a specified target path, and risk neutrality for the unskilled participant. It is not a universal impossibility result for every contract with audits, exposure restrictions, reputation, criminal liability, or other information. Those mechanisms alter the feasible strategy or payoff set.

## What can and cannot be concluded

The paper gives a sharp warning about inference from successful return histories: rare losses may remain unseen for economically long periods, and payoff-based rewards can attract managers whose expected investment contribution is ordinary. The analysis is particularly relevant when a return looks smooth because it sells insurance on an infrequent state and when fees are irrecoverable after payment.

It does not establish an empirical fraction of deceptive managers, a test that labels a specific fund fraudulent, or an optimal investor withdrawal rule. The option examples illustrate distributions, not evidence of intent. Nor does the paper solve a market equilibrium in which investors anticipate imitation, competition changes fees, and genuinely skilled managers choose disclosure. Its predicted deterioration in investor confidence is an economic conjecture built on the incentive mechanism.

For implementation, distinguish three objects: the distribution of gross fund payoffs, the path and timing of fee transfers, and the information available about underlying positions. A return-only diagnostic cannot identify all tail exposures, but position and liability information can change the problem. The reliable mathematical core is the deterministic-benchmark fair-lottery construction and its compensation comparison. The stochastic generalization needs a corrected joint probability and pricing model before it can support quantitative contract design.
