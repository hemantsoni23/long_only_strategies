# Are We All Less Risky and More Skillful than Our Fellow Drivers?

Ola Svenson, *Acta Psychologica* 47 (1981), 143-148. Source: [Are We All Less Risky and More SKillful than our Fellow Drivers.pdf](<../../Finance/Are We All Less Risky and More SKillful than our Fellow Drivers.pdf>), six pages. All numerical claims concern this experiment unless labeled as a recalculation.

## Why this matters

The familiar claim that people see themselves as better than average can reflect a poorly defined comparison population. Svenson asks whether favorable self-ranking persists when the comparison is explicitly the other licensed drivers participating in the same experiment. It does. The contribution is a simple reference-group refinement and a separation of perceived skill from perceived safety, rather than a direct measurement of actual competence.

For quantitative investing, the relevance is to eliciting beliefs about relative skill and risk. An investor can believe both that they earn more and that they take less risk than peers. This study illustrates how such beliefs can be collectively inconsistent. It does not measure portfolio behavior, trading skill, or the effect of overconfidence on returns, and it should not be cited as direct financial evidence.

## Design sufficient to reconstruct the elicitation

There are 161 licensed student drivers in two convenience samples. Eighty-one US participants responded to an advertisement in the University of Oregon student newspaper; their median age was 22. Forty-one assessed driving skill and forty assessed safety. Eighty Swedish psychology students at Stockholm University had median age 33; forty-five assessed skill and thirty-five safety. The sample-size split matters because percentages correspond to small numbers of people, not a large representative survey.

The US question appeared among several judgment tasks. Swedish participants had no additional task in the session. Both received written questions asking for their percentile position among the other experimental participants, expressly excluding drivers in their city or country as the comparison. The instructions acknowledged that participants did not know everyone else's driving and requested the most accurate estimate possible. Answers were one of ten consecutive ten-percentile intervals; higher ranks meant safer or more skillful. The skill wording was adapted from the safety question.

Thus the two attributes were assessed by different subsets, rather than every person rating both. Country differences are confounded with age, recruitment, task context, and possibly other unreported composition differences. The source does not state a randomization procedure for assigning the two questions, incentives for accuracy, session sizes, sex composition, prior driving experience, or actual crash records for these participants.

The text rounds the above-median proportions to 88% and 77% for US and Swedish safety, and 93% and 69% for skill. These agree with summing the upper five bins, up to rounding. US skill respondents placing themselves in the top twenty percent total 46.3%, compared with approximately 15.5% in Sweden.

The safety medians are consistent with the table: the US median falls in 81-90 and the Swedish median in 71-80. The text's stated **skill** median intervals appear one bin too low. From the table, US cumulative percentage is about 41.4% through 61-70 and 53.6% through 71-80, so its median is in 71-80, rather than the printed 61-70. Swedish cumulative percentage is about 48.7% through 51-60 and 59.8% through 61-70, so its median is in 61-70 rather than the printed 51-60. This is a recalculation from the published distribution, not a new dataset. It strengthens rather than removes the qualitative favorable-ranking pattern.

The article supplies descriptive distributions without confidence intervals, a formal cross-country test, or a calibrated individual-error statistic. The small denominators are important: one US safety response is 2.5 percentage points. Apparent precision beyond these counts is unwarranted.

## What is established and what is interpretation

Within a group with distinct actual ranks, about half its members can be above the median. Much larger proportions claiming upper-half rank indicate collective overplacement relative to a common ranking. However, the study does not observe true driving skill or safety and cannot identify which particular respondent is mistaken. Individual subjective information, different internal definitions of skill, and uncertainty about others are not separately measured.

The source suggests cognitive availability of favorable experiences and limited knowledge of others as possible mechanisms. It also discusses outside studies of accident-involved drivers and other self-serving judgments. Those are cited background findings, not extra observations in this 161-person experiment. The study does not experimentally manipulate memory, feedback, or knowledge of peers to discriminate among explanations.

The discussion links favorable self-images to weaker responses to safety campaigns and potentially greater risk taking. These are plausible behavioral implications, not causal effects measured here. No actual driving decisions, accidents, subsequent information acquisition, or longitudinal learning outcomes are recorded. Likewise, the stronger US pattern is not an identified cultural effect because recruitment and age differ sharply.

## Reproduction limits and research use

Reproduction requires preserving the named reference group, the exact ten-bin rank response, the separate skill/safety subsets, and the unequal task settings. A follow-up measuring actual performance or experimentally varying feedback would answer a different question. It would be useful to distinguish overplacement against peers from overestimation of absolute ability, which this design does not elicit.

The durable finding is that a favorable comparative self-image survives a more concrete peer comparison. The study is compact, descriptive evidence rather than a general law that all people are overconfident or that overconfidence necessarily worsens financial outcomes. Its most actionable methodological lesson is to define the peer population and distinguish the performance dimension before interpreting a relative-skill survey.
