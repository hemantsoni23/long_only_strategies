# Efficient Markets Hypothesis (Adaptive Markets) — Andrew W. Lo (2007) — Detailed Quantitative Research Notes

**Author:** Andrew W. Lo (MIT Sloan) | **Year:** 2007 | **Venue:** entry "Efficient Markets Hypothesis" in L. Blume and S. Durlauf (eds.), *The New Palgrave: A Dictionary of Economics*, 2nd ed., Palgrave Macmillan (pre-publication version, 22 pp. + bibliography)  
**Source file:** `Finance/HedgeFunds_Lo_2007b.pdf` — the filename is misleading: the content is the EMH/AMH dictionary entry, not a hedge-fund paper. Cross-references: asset price anomalies; bounded rationality; financial market anomalies; information economics; rational expectations.

---

## What the entry does

A survey of market-efficiency theory and evidence that ends with Lo's **Adaptive Markets Hypothesis (AMH)**. Its argument, in four steps: (1) the EMH has sharp but *joint* empirical implications, so decades of tests have not produced consensus; (2) anomalies and behavioural biases are real but their economic value is undecidable from paper backtests; (3) Grossman–Stiglitz makes perfect efficiency impossible, so efficiency should be measured *relatively*; (4) an evolutionary framework reconciles EMH with behavioural findings — efficiency is a property of the market's ecology and varies over time.

## Origins

- **Samuelson (1965)**, "Proof that Properly Anticipated Prices Fluctuate Randomly": from spatial and temporal commodity pricing; properly anticipated price changes are unforecastable. That lineage leads to dynamic allocation, log-optimal policies, warrant/option pricing, Black–Scholes and Merton (1973).
- **Fama (1963, 1965a,b, 1970)**: from measuring the statistical properties of prices and adjudicating technical vs fundamental analysis; first to use the term "efficient markets" (1965b); operationalises "prices fully reflect all available information" by structuring information sets. That lineage leads to event studies, tests of linear factor models, and the anomaly catalogue.
- Logic: competition for information makes prices martingales in a frictionless market ("the more efficient the market, the more random the price changes"). The neoclassical extension (LeRoy 1973, Rubinstein 1976, Lucas 1978) with risk-averse agents: *marginal-utility-weighted* prices are martingales, schematically $\mathbb{E}_t[M_{t+1}P_{t+1}]=P_t$ (with dividends), not raw prices.

## Evidence reviewed

**Random walk.** Early tests (Cowles–Jones 1937, later retracted by Cowles 1960; Osborne 1959; Cootner; Fama; Fama–Blume 1966) broadly support the RWH. Lo–MacKinlay (1988) variance ratios, using $\mathrm{Var}(r_{2w})=2\,\mathrm{Var}(r_{1w})$ under the RWH, reject it for weekly US **index** returns 1962–1985: variances grow faster than linearly, i.e. positive serial correlation — while **individual stocks** generally satisfy the RWH. French–Roll (1986): variance over weekends/holidays is much lower than over equal trading-day spans, so trading itself generates volatility (consistent with Black's 1986 noise traders). Long horizons (3–5 years): Fama–French (1988), Poterba–Summers (1988) find negative autocorrelation 1926–1986, but it cannot be rejected at conventional levels and Kim–Nelson–Startz (1991), Richardson (1993) show artefacts. Lo (1991): a long-memory test robust to short-range dependence finds little support for long memory; conventional short-term dependence suffices.

**Variance bounds.** If $P_t=\mathbb{E}_t[P_t^*]$ with $P_t^*$ the ex-post present value of dividends, the forecast error is orthogonal to $P_t$, so $\mathrm{Var}(P^*)=\mathrm{Var}(P)+\mathrm{Var}(P^*-P)\ge\mathrm{Var}(P)$. LeRoy–Porter (1981) and Shiller (1981) find dramatic violations in annual US data. Responses: small-sample bias under plausible DGPs (Flavin 1983, Kleidon 1986, Marsh–Merton 1986); and theory in which the bound fails while markets are efficient — dividend smoothing with geometric-random-walk earnings (Marsh–Merton 1986), risk aversion in a Lucas economy (Michener 1982). This is where Lo locates the **joint-hypothesis** point: "fully reflect" concerns both information content and the price-formation mechanism; excess volatility is equally consistent with inefficiency, risk aversion, or dividend smoothing.

**Over- and underreaction.** DeBondt–Thaler (1985): NYSE 1926–1982, 36-month winners and losers reverse over the next 36 months, many reversals in January; Chopra–Lakonishok–Ritter (1992) confirm after market-risk and size adjustment; Lehmann (1990): a monthly losers-minus-winners zero-investment strategy is almost always profitable, NYSE/AMEX 1962–1985. Counterpoints: Chan (1988) — CAPM-adjusted contrarian returns are consistent with EMH; Lo–MacKinlay (1990c) — **at least half** of Lehmann's profits come from positive *cross*-autocorrelations, not own-reversal (two serially uncorrelated but positively cross-autocorrelated stocks produce contrarian profits without any overreaction). Earnings: Ball–Brown (1968) — up to 80% of earnings-surprise information is anticipated, and they first document post-announcement drift; Bernard–Thomas (1990) — underreaction to what current earnings imply for future earnings. Small frictions (costs, taxes) often eliminate the profits.

**Anomalies** (defined as regular, reliable, widely known, inexplicable): size (Banz 1981); January/turn-of-year (Keim 1983, Roll 1983, Rozeff–Kinney 1976); Value Line (Copeland–Mayers 1982); short-term reversal; medium-term momentum (Jegadeesh 1990; Chan–Jegadeesh–Lakonishok 1996; Jegadeesh–Titman 2001); P/E (Basu 1977); orange-juice futures (Roll 1984); calendar effects (Lakonishok–Smidt 1988). Two readings: violation of EMH, or unexploitable because of risk/costs (or academics' lack of imagination). Example: the January effect is largely **bid–ask bounce** — December closes at the bid, January at the ask; a bid-to-ask move on a \$5 NYSE stock with the pre-2000 \$0.125 minimum spread is 2.5%. Lo's key methodological claim: realistic costs, impact, liquidity, rare events, institutional rigidities and non-stationarity cannot be incorporated in academic paper profits; the value of anomalies is decided "in the laboratory of actual markets," where skill and luck are easily confused. Data snooping (Brown et al. 1992; Lo–MacKinlay 1990b) can manufacture anomalies such as the size effect.

**Behavioural critique.** Catalogue of biases (overconfidence, overreaction, loss aversion, herding, mental accounting, miscalibration, hyperbolic discounting, regret). Lo's modified Kahneman–Tversky example: most choose sure +\$240k (A) over 25% × \$1m (B), and 75% × −\$1m (D) over sure −\$750k (C). But A+D = {+\$240k w.p. 0.25, −\$760k w.p. 0.75} while B+C = {+\$250k, −\$750k} with the same probabilities: B+C = A+D plus a sure \$10k. If London faces A/B and Tokyo C/D, locally "matters of taste" aggregate to a dominated consolidated position.

**Impossibility and arbitrage limits.** Grossman–Stiglitz (1980): if prices fully revealed information, no one would pay to acquire it; equilibrium needs enough inefficiency to compensate information and trading costs, with noise traders (Black 1986) supplying the rents. EMH defence: arbitrage bounds biases. Dutch book: someone with $P(E)=0.5$ and $P(E^c)=0.75$ accepts $B_1$ (+\$1 if $E$, −\$1 otherwise) and $B_2$ (+\$1 if $E^c$, −\$3 if $E$); taking the other side with \$50 on $B_1$ and \$25 on $B_2$ yields +\$25 in either state. Whether arbitrage capital is large enough is empirical; Kindleberger (1989) documents irrationality overwhelming it for months or years.

## Relative efficiency and the AMH

EMH alone is not refutable; it needs auxiliary structure, so rejections are uninformative about which part failed. More useful is **relative** efficiency (futures vs spot, auction vs dealer) — like rating an engine at 60% efficiency rather than testing whether it is perfect. Given Grossman–Stiglitz, EMH is an unrealisable ideal and a benchmark.

Agent-based ecology (Farmer 2002): strategies are species, capital is population; markets become more efficient over time but far from perfectly, and patterns disappear only slowly, during which substantial profits accrue and new patterns appear.

**AMH.** Bounded rationality (Simon's satisficing) was dismissed because the stopping rule seems to require knowing the optimum; Lo's answer is that stopping points are set by trial and error and natural selection. Heuristics are near-optimal in the environment that shaped them; when the environment shifts they look like biases — better called *maladaptive* (the fish flopping on land). Statement: *prices reflect as much information as dictated by the combination of environmental conditions and the number and nature of "species"* (pension funds, retail, market makers, hedge funds). Many species competing for scarce resources gives high efficiency (10-year Treasury notes); few species and abundant resources give low efficiency (Italian Renaissance paintings). Profit opportunities play the role of food; competition depletes them, populations decline, the cycle restarts, sometimes ending in extinction or a regime shift. Classical EMH is the steady-state limit under constant environmental conditions.

Implications stated:
1. Biases abound; their price impact depends on the size of the biased population relative to competitors with better heuristics. Example: in autumn 1998, the demand for liquidity and safety overwhelmed fixed-income relative-value hedge funds; they had profited handsomely before August 1998, their numbers fell sharply afterwards (failures, redemptions, fewer launches), and many reappeared as performance recovered.
2. Strategies go through cycles of profit and loss as conditions, entry/exit and opportunities change.
3. Fear and greed are adaptations; Lo–Repin (2002): autonomic physiological responses of experienced traders correlate with market events, and channelling emotion is part of trading skill (emotion as the reward/punishment numeraire, Rolls 1999; Damasio 1994). Selection is "survival of the richest."
4. Any risk–reward relation is unstable; the equity risk premium is time-varying and **path-dependent** because selection changes the investor population (e.g., post-tech-bubble exits). Of prices, probabilities and preferences, preferences are the most fundamental and least understood.

Lo concedes the AMH is not yet "operationally meaningful" in Samuelson's sense. Closes with Markowitz's Nobel anecdote (Friedman: portfolio theory "was not Economics" — "But now it is").

## Assessment

- **Valuable:** a compact, accurate map of the EMH debate, with three points of lasting use to a quant: the joint-hypothesis problem (variance bounds as the textbook case); the Lo–MacKinlay cross-autocorrelation decomposition, which warns against reading reversal profits as overreaction (index-level positive autocorrelation with individual-stock random walks is the lead–lag signature); and relative efficiency as the operational target. The \$10k-dominance example is a sharp argument for consolidated, firm-level risk aggregation.
- **Weak:** the AMH here is a narrative, not a model: no dynamics, no testable restrictions, no measure of "species" or "resources," and it can rationalise almost any pattern ex post (the 1998 example is illustrative, not evidence). "Fully reconcile the EMH with all of its behavioral alternatives" is a claim, not a result. Evidence cited is secondary; no new data.
- **Practical reading:** treat factor premia and alpha as capacity- and crowding-dependent and cyclical, not constants; judge anomalies on net-of-cost live implementability, with skepticism toward data-snooped paper profits; expect expected-return estimates to depend on the investor population's recent history. Later empirical work (Lo 2004 *JPM*; Lo's 2017 book *Adaptive Markets*) develops these ideas further.
