# The Cross-Section of Expected Stock Returns: What Have We Learnt from the Past Twenty-Five Years of Research?
**Authors:** Avanidhar Subrahmanyam (UCLA Anderson)
**Year:** 2010 (the file is the draft of September 16, 2009)
**Journal/Venue:** European Financial Management / survey
**Source file:** `Finance/AbnormalReturnsReview_Subrahmanyam_2010.pdf`

## Thesis
This is a short survey (about 25 pages of text, no new estimates) of the post-CAPM literature on cross-sectional return predictors. It covers US NYSE/Amex/Nasdaq stocks at monthly and longer horizons and excludes microstructure-horizon predictability. More than fifty predictors are grouped by motivation:
1. informal "Wall Street wisdom" or chance discovery;
2. variants of risk–return (RR) models;
3. behavioral misreaction;
4. frictions (illiquidity, arbitrage constraints).

The central claim is negative: the picture is "murky" because
- the correlation structure among predictors is ignored;
- controls are neither comprehensive nor consistent;
- robustness to simple methodological variations is rarely shown.

The profession is "segmented into groups of like-thinking scholars" with little cross-talk.

## I. Informal predictors
- **Value and size.** Low P/E (Basu 1977), size (Banz 1981), low price (Miller–Scholes 1982), dividend yield via taxes (Brennan 1970). Fama–French (1992) establish size and B/M; FF (1993) propose the three-factor model and the distress interpretation. Daniel–Titman (1997): after controlling for the characteristics, returns are not strongly related to FF factor betas. Zhang (2006): B/M and momentum predictability is stronger under high information uncertainty (small size, low analyst following).
- **Short-term reversal.** One-month reversal (Jegadeesh 1990; Cooper 1999; Subrahmanyam 2005) is attributed either to overreaction or partly to illiquidity-driven reversals (Avramov–Chordia–Goyal 2006).
- **Momentum.** Jegadeesh–Titman (1993) use 3–12-month formation windows. Refinements and conditioning results:
  - return consistency matters (Grinblatt–Moskowitz 2004);
  - profits fall with size and analyst coverage (Hong–Lim–Stein 2000; replicated in Europe by Doukas–McKnight 2005);
  - much larger after up markets (Cooper–Gutierrez–Hameed 2004);
  - concentrated in low-credit-quality stocks (Avramov–Chordia–Jostova–Philipov 2007);
  - explained by business-cycle predictability in the US (Chordia–Shivakumar 2002), but this is not confirmed internationally (Griffin–Ji–Martin 2003);
  - pervasive across countries and asset classes (Rouwenhorst 1998; Asness–Moskowitz–Pedersen 2009);
  - linked to small-trader underreaction (Hvidkjaer 2006);
  - same-calendar-month seasonality up to 20 annual lags (Heston–Sadka 2008).
- **Long-term reversal** (3–5 years; DeBondt–Thaler 1985, 1987; Chopra–Lakonishok–Ritter 1992). Conrad–Kaul (1993) attribute it to low-price stocks. Loughran–Ritter (1996) counter that low price proxies for low past returns and that CK's survival requirement induces bias.
- **Haugen–Baker (1996):** past returns, volume and accounting ratios (ROE, P/E) dominate; beta and total volatility do not matter.

## II. Risk–return variants
- **Conditional and intertemporal models:**
  - conditional CAPM with human capital (Jagannathan–Wang 1996);
  - cash-flow vs discount-rate beta, with only cash-flow beta priced (Campbell–Vuolteenaho 2004);
  - ICAPM with the real rate and maximum Sharpe ratio as state variables, with lower pricing errors than FF3 on the 25 size/B/M portfolios (Brennan–Wang–Xia 2004);
  - consumption beta plus cash-flow duration explaining more than 80% of FF portfolio variation (Da 2009);
  - benchmarking by delegated managers lowers expected returns of benchmark-covarying assets (Brennan–Cheng–Li 2009; Gomez–Zapatero 2003).
- **Idiosyncratic risk evidence is contradictory:**
  - priced positively (Lehmann 1990);
  - high idiosyncratic vol earns low returns (Ang–Hodrick–Xing–Zhang 2006);
  - EGARCH expected idiosyncratic vol is positively related to returns (Fu 2009).
- **Factor alternatives:** macro factors (term, credit) drive out FF loadings (Petkova 2006). Principal components of size/B/M portfolios (in the spirit of Connor–Korajczyk) absorb the size and B/M effects (Zhang 2009).
- **Consumption CAPM revival:**
  - cumulative multi-quarter consumption risk (Parker–Julliard 2005);
  - labor-income/consumption conditioning (Santos–Veronesi 2006);
  - idiosyncratic consumption risk (Jacobs–Wang 2004);
  - cay (Lettau–Ludvigson 2001);
  - long-run consumption risk (Malloy–Moskowitz–Vissing-Jorgensen 2005; Bansal–Dittmar–Kiku 2006);
  - Q4-to-Q4 consumption growth (Jagannathan–Wang 2007);
  - garbage growth as a consumption proxy (Savov 2009).
- **Other:** unconcentrated industries earn more (Hou–Robinson 2006); low media coverage earns more (Fang–Peress 2009).

## III. Behavioral predictors
- **Extrapolation:** past growth predicts negatively (Lakonishok–Shleifer–Vishny 1994), as do analysts' long-term growth forecasts (La Porta 1996).
- **Accounting-based:**
  - accruals are negative (Sloan 1996);
  - accounting-model value/price is positive (cited as "Sloan, Frankel and Lee 1998");
  - asset growth is negative (Cooper–Gulen–Schill 2008);
  - issuance is negative (Daniel–Titman 2006);
  - capital investment is negative, an empire-building story (Titman–Wei–Xie 2004).
- **Retail flows:** small-trade order flow predicts negatively over 6–24 months (Hvidkjaer 2008; Barber–Odean–Zhu 2009).
- **Distress:** bankruptcy risk earns low returns (Dichev 1998; Campbell–Hilscher–Szilagyi 2008). Griffin–Lemmon (2002) show the effect comes from distressed low-B/M firms. Together this contradicts the FF distress reading of B/M and points to underreaction to balance-sheet information.
- **Linked firms and sentiment:** customer–supplier links (Cohen–Frazzini 2008). After high sentiment, young and volatile firms underperform (Baker–Wurgler 2006).
- **Events and governance:**
  - analyst revision drift (Womack 1996), present only for experienced analysts; inexperienced analysts' revisions reverse (Sorescu–Subrahmanyam 2006);
  - PEAD for up to about three months (Bernard–Thomas 1989, 1990);
  - governance index (Gompers–Ishii–Metrick 2003), which is sensitive to industry controls (Johnson–Moorman–Sorescu 2009);
  - unionization raises the cost of equity (Chen–Kacperczyk–Ortiz-Molina 2009).

## IV. Frictions
- **Levels of illiquidity:**
  - quoted spread (Amihud–Mendelson 1986);
  - Amihud (2002) $|R|/\text{\$Vol}$;
  - price impact on order flow (Brennan–Subrahmanyam 1996);
  - turnover, negatively related to returns (Datar–Naik–Radcliffe 1998; Brennan–Chordia–Subrahmanyam 1998);
  - a Kyle-model illiquidity measure (Chordia–Huh–Subrahmanyam 2008).
  
  Only sell-side price impact is priced (Brennan–Chordia–Subrahmanyam–Tong 2008).
- **Liquidity risk:** the volatility of turnover is negatively related to returns, which cuts against a liquidity-risk premium (Chordia–Subrahmanyam–Anshuman 2001). Betas to aggregate liquidity are priced (Pástor–Stambaugh 2003; Acharya–Pedersen 2005).
- **Information risk:**
  - PIN is priced (Easley–Hvidkjaer–O'Hara 2002), but only its liquidity component (Duarte–Young 2009);
  - variation in Kyle's lambda (Sadka 2006);
  - local ownership (Coval–Moskowitz 2001);
  - nonsynchronicity $1-R^2$ (Hou–Moskowitz 2005).
- **Short-sale constraints:**
  - high borrow costs mean low future returns (Jones–Lamont 2002);
  - high short interest with low institutional ownership means low returns (Asquith–Pathak–Ritter 2005);
  - less predictability where institutional ownership is high (Nagel 2005);
  - institutional demand explains the disappearance of the size effect (Gompers–Metrick 2001);
  - falling ownership breadth predicts underperformance (Chen–Hong–Stein 2002);
  - analyst dispersion predicts low returns (Diether–Malloy–Scherbina 2002), but this is confined to distressed stocks (Avramov et al. 2009).

## V. Methodological concerns (the substantive part)
1. **Choice of risk controls.** FF3 is the default even though FF factors may not be risk factors (Daniel–Titman 1997) and macro factors may dominate them (Petkova 2006). Connor–Korajczyk PCA factors and ML factor extraction (Lehmann–Modest 2005) were abandoned. Adding UMD as a "risk" control is poorly motivated, since momentum is unlikely to be risk.
2. **Errors-in-variables in loadings.** The options are portfolio-assigned betas (FF 1992), a Shanken (1992) correction, or risk-adjusted returns on the left-hand side (BCS 1998). Their consequences for findings are not understood.
3. **Loading estimation window.** Rolling 60-month windows (BCS 1998) vs full-sample (Petkova 2006) is another researcher degree of freedom.
4. **Time-varying betas.** Loadings conditioned on macro variables (Ferson–Harvey 1999) or on characteristics plus macro (Avramov–Chordia 2006, which weakens size and B/M). But a misspecified beta dynamic can do worse than unconditional betas (Ghysels 1998). High-frequency conditional CAPM betas do not explain size and B/M (Lewellen–Nagel 2006). Conditional betas are not standard practice.
5. **Microstructure noise bias.** Noise such as bid–ask bounce biases mean returns upward (Blume–Stambaugh 1983). This contaminates predictive regressions when predictors correlate with noise (Brennan–Wang 2009; Asparouhova–Bessembinder–Kalcheva 2009). Fixes are mid-quote returns or WLS weighted by prior gross return.
6. **Fama–MacBeth weighting.** Averaging slopes of unequal precision equally is inefficient. The Litzenberger–Ramaswamy correction or panel GLS (Brennan–Subrahmanyam 1996) exist but are rarely used.
7. **Inconsistent controls.** Fama–French (2008) find accruals, issuance and momentum the most robust predictors. But they use only FM regressions and sorts, with no conditional betas, liquidity, order-flow or information-asymmetry controls. Liquidity controls are rare generally (e.g. Cooper–Gulen–Schill 2008), and behavioral and friction papers do not control for the RR-variant predictors.

## Assessment
- **Strength:** a compact, well-referenced map of the 1985–2009 predictor zoo. Its Section V anticipates the later multiple-testing and factor-zoo literature (Harvey–Liu–Zhu 2016; Green–Hand–Zhang 2017; Hou–Xue–Zhang 2020) by identifying ignored cross-correlation, inconsistent controls and methodological fragility as the binding problems.
- **Weakness:** it reports almost no magnitudes, t-stats or sample details. It offers no quantitative synthesis (e.g. a joint regression of the fifty predictors) and no framework for choosing among the four explanations. It diagnoses without resolving.
- **For a quant researcher, the Section V list is a usable robustness protocol for any new signal:**
  - test against several factor definitions (FF, macro, statistical);
  - handle beta estimation error and choose the window deliberately;
  - use mid-quote returns or noise-robust weighting for low-priced or illiquid names;
  - use precision-weighted cross-sectional regressions;
  - include liquidity and the known robust predictors (accruals, issuance, momentum, asset growth) as controls before claiming novelty.
