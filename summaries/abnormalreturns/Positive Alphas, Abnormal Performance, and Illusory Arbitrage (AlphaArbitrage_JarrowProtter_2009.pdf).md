# Positive Alphas, Abnormal Performance, and Illusory Arbitrage

Robert Jarrow and Philip Protter. April 16, 2010 working paper, Johnson School Research Paper Series 19-2010, SSRN 1593051. Source: [AlphaArbitrage_JarrowProtter_2009.pdf](../../Finance/AlphaArbitrage_JarrowProtter_2009.pdf). Despite the filename's 2009 date, the local document is the April 2010 draft. It contains a series cover followed by twenty numbered pages; all were read, including the proofs and inverse-Bessel example. References below use printed pages, one less than the PDF page number. There is no empirical dataset, numerical experiment, table, figure, or separate appendix.

## Why this matters and how to read the claims

Performance evaluation often identifies a regression intercept with investment skill. The authors ask a sharper question: what does an intercept mean when the benchmark factors, their time-varying loadings, and the information available to the observer are all treated explicitly? They seek to derive a factor-return representation directly from no-arbitrage, then explain how a smaller information set can produce an apparent alpha even when the true market has no arbitrage.

The two advertised contributions are that nonzero alpha implies an arbitrage opportunity when the correct factors are used, and that strict local martingales associated with price bubbles can create an illusion of arbitrage after information is reduced. The distinction between local and true martingales is substantive: a positive local martingale can lose unconditional expectation without acquiring a predictable drift in its full-information stochastic differential equation.

However, this draft contains gaps that materially affect both headline claims. The factor-portfolio construction assumes a spanning property not implied by its rank calculation. The incomplete-information argument switches between projecting a Brownian motion and projecting a positive strict local martingale, which have different behavior. Its example changes the observed process rather than merely changing the information available for trading an unchanged observable price. This note reconstructs the useful equations and explicitly separates them from conclusions that the displayed assumptions do not establish. These are mathematical qualifications to this version, not claims that the general subject of filtration shrinkage is invalid.

For an implementation, the useful research objective is therefore to diagnose which conditional pricing model and observation process an estimated intercept rejects. An economically exploitable arbitrage requires an admissible self-financing strategy with the appropriate pathwise payoff property. A positive conditional expected residual is not sufficient on its own.

## Full-information market and the pricing-density restriction

The economy lives on a filtered probability space with physical measure $P$ and filtration $\mathcal G_t$ generated by a $d$-dimensional Brownian motion $W$. The horizon is finite, $0\leq t\leq T$. There are $N$ strictly positive risky prices and a money-market account

$$
B_t=\exp\left(\int_0^t r_s\,ds\right).
$$

The interest-rate integral must exist. Dividends are omitted for notational simplicity. The risky return dynamics are assumed to have an Itô form,

$$
\frac{dS_i(t)}{S_i(t)}=\mu_i(t)\,dt+\sigma_i(t)'dW_t,
$$

with predictable coefficients and the integrability needed for the stochastic integrals. The paper states locally bounded coefficients. Its claim that this specification is without loss of generality should be read within an Itô diffusion setting: Brownian adaptation alone does not force every positive price process to have absolutely continuous finite variation and this exact representation.

Let $\Sigma_t$ be the $N\times d$ volatility matrix, with row $\sigma_i'$. Its rank is assumed constant at $n\leq\min(N,d)$. The economically relevant dimension is the row space of traded volatility, not just the count of listed assets. Even if $N=d$, a rank-deficient matrix does not give a complete Brownian market. The source's comment that equality of the two counts gives completeness needs the full-rank spanning qualification.

Under no free lunch with vanishing risk, for the locally bounded continuous price setup there is an equivalent measure $Q$ making each $S_i/B$ a local martingale. Define the density process and state-price density by

$$
Z_t=E_P\left[\left.\frac{dQ}{dP}\right|\mathcal G_t\right],
\qquad \rho_t=\frac{Z_t}{B_t}.
$$

Here $Z$ is a strictly positive true martingale, normalized initially to one. A merely positive local-martingale candidate is not enough to define the desired probability measure: its terminal expectation must be one, and equivalence requires a positive terminal density. These requirements remain necessary when using Girsanov later in the paper.

With a Brownian representation $dZ_t/Z_t=c_t'dW_t$, integration by parts gives

$$
d(S_i\rho)=S_i\rho\left[(\mu_i-r)\,dt+\sigma_i'dW+c'dW\right]
+d\langle S_i,\rho\rangle.
$$

The product is a local martingale only if its finite-variation drift vanishes. Since

$$
\frac{d\langle S_i,\rho\rangle_t}{S_i(t)\rho_t}
=\sigma_i(t)'c_t\,dt,
$$

the state-price restriction in equation (6), printed page 7, is

$$
\mu_i-r=-\sigma_i'c.
$$

Writing $\lambda=-c$ produces the familiar market-price-of-risk system

$$
\mu-r\mathbf1=\Sigma\lambda.
$$

This is the central valid local pricing relation. The change of measure $dW^Q=dW+\lambda\,dt$ removes the risky excess drifts when its density is a true martingale. In an incomplete market, components of $\lambda$ orthogonal to the traded volatility row space do not change this drift restriction. Thus the no-arbitrage measure, and a bubble definition relying on its expectations, need not be unique.

The covariance language in the paper is infinitesimal. Over a short interval $\Delta$, mean returns are approximately $\mu_t\Delta$ and covariances approximately $\sigma_i'\sigma_j\Delta$. At a fixed monthly or quarterly horizon with stochastic coefficients, those approximations are not exact regression identities. Integrating the stochastic differential equations preserves time variation and path dependence that a single fixed beta can hide.

## Intended factor construction and the missing spanning condition

The authors define $K$ by the Brownian coordinates with nonzero coefficients in the density process, then seek $K$ mutually orthogonal factor portfolios. They want a factor return with unit exposure to a selected Brownian coordinate and no exposure to the others. After removing redundant assets, write $\Sigma$ as an $n\times d$ full-row-rank matrix. For target exposures collected in an $m\times d$ matrix $E$, the required risky weights satisfy

$$
\Theta\Sigma=E.
$$

Lemma 1 proposes

$$
\Theta=E\Sigma'(\Sigma\Sigma')^{-1}
$$

and concludes that a solution always exists. Multiplying back shows the actual condition:

$$
\Theta\Sigma=EP,\qquad
P=\Sigma'(\Sigma\Sigma')^{-1}\Sigma.
$$

$P$ is the orthogonal projection onto the traded volatility row space. Consequently, the proposed weights solve the target equation exactly only when $EP=E$. Every selected target direction must be in that row space. Full row rank guarantees an inverse of $\Sigma\Sigma'$, but does not turn the rectangular matrix into a map spanning arbitrary Brownian coordinates.

A minimal counterexample, derived here to clarify the issue, is $\Sigma=(1,1)$ and target $E=(1,0)$. The proposed weight is $1/2$, whose actual exposure is $(1/2,1/2)$, not $(1,0)$. This market can be arbitrage-free and yet cannot trade a pure first-coordinate Brownian shock. The inequality $K\leq n$ does not resolve this geometric obstruction.

There is a second implementation issue in the portfolio definition. If $\theta$ represents fractions of portfolio wealth in risky assets, a self-financing portfolio with the remaining wealth in cash has return

$$
\frac{dX}{X}=r\,dt+\theta'\left(\frac{dS}{S}-r\mathbf1\,dt\right).
$$

The source writes $dX/X=\theta'dS/S$, without imposing $\theta'\mathbf1=1$ or including the cash position. That expression agrees with the self-financing total return only when the omitted financing term vanishes. The corrected excess-return formula is the appropriate one for reproducing factor portfolios. Positivity of the stochastic exponential, admissibility, and integrability of the weights also need checking; rank alone does not bound weights near degeneracy.

Finally, counting nonzero Brownian coefficients is basis dependent. A predictable orthogonal change of Brownian basis changes which coordinates appear in the density representation. A coefficient depending on all Brownian coordinates need not produce a density adapted to the filtration generated only by the coordinates being integrated. The source moves from a full-filtration representation to a reduced-factor-filtration representation without proving that extra measurability property. A reproducing model must specify the filtration of both coefficients and innovations rather than identify systematic dimension solely from coordinate labels.

## Beta pricing under an explicit valid spanning assumption

The intended factor theorem can be reconstructed conditionally. Suppose tradable, self-financing factor portfolios exist with mutually orthogonal return innovations $dM_k^X$, nonzero quadratic-variation rates $v_k$, and a pricing-density representation whose component covarying with traded returns is spanned by those innovations:

$$
\frac{d\rho}{\rho}=-r\,dt+\sum_{k=1}^K b_k\,dM_k^X+dM^\perp,
$$

where $M^\perp$ has zero covariation with all traded prices. The state-price restriction applied to each factor gives

$$
\mu_{X_k}-r=-b_kv_k.
$$

For an asset, define instantaneous beta by

$$
\beta_{ik}=\frac{d\langle S_i,X_k\rangle/(S_iX_k)}
{d\langle X_k\rangle/X_k^2}.
$$

Because the factor innovations are orthogonal, their separate covariance-to-variance ratios supply the projection coefficients. With correlated factors the corresponding multivariate covariance-matrix inverse is required. Combining the state-price equations gives

$$
\mu_i-r=\sum_k\beta_{ik}(\mu_{X_k}-r).
$$

Subtracting factor returns leaves a continuous local-martingale residual,

$$
\frac{dS_i}{S_i}-r\,dt
=\sum_k\beta_{ik}\left(\frac{dX_k}{X_k}-r\,dt\right)+dM_i^\perp.
$$

These are the intended equations (9) and (11), now with the spanning and financing conditions made explicit. Under the source's ideal unit-coordinate construction, $\beta_{ik}=\sigma_{ik}$ and

$$
dM_i^\perp=\sum_{k>K}\sigma_{ik}\,dW_k,
\qquad d\langle M_i^\perp\rangle_t
=\delta_i^2(t)\,dt,
\quad \delta_i^2=\sum_{k>K}\sigma_{ik}^2.
$$

The prose on printed page 12 gives residual variance $\delta_i\,dt$; it must be $\delta_i^2\,dt$. When $\delta_i>0$, normalizing the residual produces a Brownian innovation under the required quadratic-variation conditions. If $\delta_i=0$, the residual vanishes there and division by it is not defined. Orthogonality of innovations also does not automatically imply independence of the entire factor-price processes: their stochastic coefficients can depend on the residual Brownian history.

Adding a drift $\alpha_i\,dt$ tests this specified representation. If factors truly exhaust the relevant pricing-density exposures and all assumptions hold, a nonzero conditional alpha contradicts that representation. It does not follow that a conventional CAPM or multifactor sample intercept establishes arbitrage. The existence theorem does not identify observable factor portfolios or estimate their loadings, and an incomplete market may admit other pricing densities. The logical rejection is joint: the factors, information, continuous-time approximation, and statistical specification must be assessed together.

A simple distinction is useful. A factor-neutral payoff with positive drift and nonzero residual diffusion still has downside risk. To infer an instantaneous riskless arbitrage, one needs a portfolio annihilating all diffusion exposures while retaining nonzero excess drift, with an admissible implementation. The matrix condition $\mu-r\mathbf1\notin\operatorname{col}(\Sigma)$ is the relevant local obstruction; a positive residual relative to an arbitrarily chosen smaller factor set is not that condition.

## Filtration shrinkage: the useful mechanism and the draft's inconsistency

Section 4 fixes a full-information market with no arbitrage and introduces a smaller filtration $\mathcal F_t\subset\mathcal G_t$. The short rate is assumed observable in $\mathcal F$. The authors focus on incomplete markets and posit a selected equivalent measure under which a discounted asset is a strict local martingale. They refer to derivative prices as a possible way to select a measure, but provide no derivative universe or identification procedure.

For a nonnegative local martingale $U$ and smaller filtration, its optional projection

$$
\widehat U_t=E[U_t\mid\mathcal F_t]
$$

is a supermartingale under appropriate integrability and regularity. Its Doob–Meyer decomposition may contain a nontrivial decreasing compensator, even when $U$ has no drift in the larger filtration. If $U$ is a true martingale, tower conditioning instead makes its projection a true martingale. This difference is the mathematical motivation for the paper's bubble argument.

However, equation (14) specifically projects the Brownian motion $W$, writing $M_t=E[W_t\mid\mathcal F_t]$, then the following paragraph treats $W$ as a nonnegative local martingale and permits $M=N-A$ with nonzero increasing $A$. Brownian motion is neither nonnegative nor strict: on a finite horizon it is an integrable true martingale. For $s<t$,

$$
E[M_t\mid\mathcal F_s]
=E[W_t\mid\mathcal F_s]
=E[W_s\mid\mathcal F_s]=M_s.
$$

Its projection cannot generate the proposed nonzero compensator. Moreover, projection of $\int\sigma\,dW$ is not generally obtained by keeping the same unobserved $\sigma$ and replacing $W$ by its projection. An innovation or filtering theorem is needed. The paper itself calls equation (14) an assumption; that assumption does not validate the subsequent change from Brownian motion to a strict local martingale.

The intended generic algebra is that a projected model might have an extra finite-variation term $dC$ beyond a reference factor model. A singular component of $dC$ cannot be removed by a drift change absolutely continuous relative to the martingale's quadratic variation. But absolute continuity alone does not guarantee an equivalent measure either: a solvable drift equation and a true-martingale density are also needed. The text's unqualified Girsanov assertions omit these conditions.

There is an even more basic economic check. An admissible strategy predictable in $\mathcal F$ is also predictable in $\mathcal G$. If the traded prices and gains processes are unchanged and observable in both filtrations, an arbitrage available with less information would also be available with more information. Restricting information cannot create a genuine arbitrage in that unchanged market. A projected-price model can suggest a trade that is not implementable against the actual price, which is a coherent interpretation of an illusion; it is not an arbitrage in the original market.

## Inverse-Bessel example and an explicit reconstruction

Example 5 starts with three independent standard Brownian coordinates $B^1,B^2,B^3$ beginning at zero, sets $x_0=(1,0,0)$, and defines

$$
R_t=\|B_t-x_0\|,
\qquad U_t=R_t^{-1},\qquad U_0=1.
$$

$R$ is a three-dimensional Bessel process. It satisfies $dR=R^{-1}dt+d\beta$. Itô's formula cancels the drift of its reciprocal:

$$
dU_t=-U_t^2d\beta_t.
$$

Three-dimensional Brownian motion does not hit the specified point, so the reciprocal remains defined. $U$ is a positive strict local martingale. Stopping before $R$ becomes smaller than $1/n$ produces a localizing sequence. The expectation need not stay one as the stopping boundary is removed, because the required uniform integrability of the stopped family is absent. Uniform integrability of values at deterministic times, mentioned by the authors, is not the stronger class-D property over stopping times.

Now observe only the first Brownian coordinate, $\mathcal F_t=\sigma(B_s^1:s\leq t)$. The actual reciprocal $U_t$ is not observable in this filtration. Its projection is $N_t=E[U_t\mid\mathcal F_t]$. The source cites the decomposition

$$
N_t=N_0+M_t-\int_0^t\frac1s\,dL_s^1,
$$

where $L^1$ is local time of $B^1$ at level one. Local time increases on the set $B^1=1$, a set of zero ordinary time measure. Thus the compensator is singular with respect to $dt$, while the martingale component has absolutely continuous quadratic variation.

An explicit formula, derived here from the example rather than supplied by the draft, makes the mechanism reproducible. Conditional on $B_t^1=x$, the radius formed by the other two coordinates has density $re^{-r^2/(2t)}/t$. Therefore, for $t>0$ and $a=|x-1|$,

$$
n(t,x)=\int_0^\infty
\frac{r e^{-r^2/(2t)}}{t\sqrt{a^2+r^2}}\,dr
=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{t}}e^{a^2/(2t)}
\left[1-\Phi\left(\frac a{\sqrt t}\right)\right],
\qquad N_t=n(t,B_t^1).
$$

Away from $x=1$, its time and diffusion derivatives cancel in Itô's formula. At $x=1$, the first derivative jumps by $-2/t$. The generalized Itô–Tanaka term is half that jump times local time, giving precisely $-t^{-1}dL_t^1$. The full process has no such finite-variation drift; it appears in the conditional projection because two spatial coordinates have been integrated out.

The example establishes a change in semimartingale structure under projection. It does not establish that the original $U$ and the projected $N$ are interchangeable traded assets. Also, the positive sign for the analogous $C$ on printed page 17 conflicts with the preceding definition $C=-\int\sigma\,dA$; the sign depends on which object is being projected and must be fixed before interpreting a positive alpha. A singular drift is not an ordinary constant regression intercept $\alpha\,dt$.

## What a researcher can reproduce, and what remains missing

The paper supplies a theoretical construction, not a tested estimator. There are no fund returns, sample dates, benchmark weights, beta-estimation windows, regression standard errors, out-of-sample tests, transaction costs, or reported abnormal-return magnitudes. Its closing suggestion that hedge-fund performance during a bubble might be illusory is a research conjecture, not evidence about any particular fund or crisis sample.

A faithful computational study could simulate the three Brownian coordinates exactly on a chosen grid, calculate $U$, calculate the conditional projection $n(t,B^1_t)$, and compare their unconditional expectations and conditional increments. It should report discretization, starting-point, Monte Carlo size, and tail-truncation choices; the reciprocal has rare large realizations, making naive small-sample averages unstable. An ordinary Euler update of $dU=-U^2d\beta$ can produce negative prices, whereas computing the reciprocal of the three-dimensional radius preserves the defining construction at grid times.

Such a study would validate the projection example, not repair the factor-spanning theorem or establish a profitable strategy. A second, separate calculation could check factor construction by computing $EP-E$, verifying financing, and testing pricing-density drift cancellation. If the row-space residual is nonzero, the supposed factor portfolio simply does not have its claimed Brownian exposure.

For empirical performance evaluation, the safe takeaway is narrower than the abstract: latent state information, nonlinear observation maps, stochastic betas, and strict-local-martingale behavior can invalidate a naive intercept interpretation. A reproducible pricing test must state which prices are observed, which filtration defines conditioning, which measure defines a bubble, which factor portfolios are actually tradable, and how admissibility is enforced. This draft highlights those issues, but its stated assumptions and displayed proofs do not deliver an unconditional theorem equating a measured Jensen alpha with arbitrage.
