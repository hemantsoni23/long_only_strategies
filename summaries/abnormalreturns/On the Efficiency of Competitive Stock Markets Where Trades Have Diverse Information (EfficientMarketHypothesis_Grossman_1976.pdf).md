# On the Efficiency of Competitive Stock Markets Where Trades Have Diverse Information

**Sanford Grossman (1976).** *The Journal of Finance* 31(2), May, 573–585. Source: [EfficientMarketHypothesis_Grossman_1976.pdf](../../Finance/EfficientMarketHypothesis_Grossman_1976.pdf). The title above preserves the scan's printed “Trades”; the text concerns traders with diverse information. All fourteen PDF pages were inspected, including the thirteen article pages and final rights notice. Critical equations and welfare footnotes were checked visually. The first-page footnote says a separate uniqueness appendix was omitted and available from the author; it is not included in this file and was not inspected. This is a theoretical equilibrium paper, with no historical dataset or estimated performance results.

## Why this matters and the innovation

An observed market price is both a term of trade and a source of information. If informed traders change their demands when they learn something, the clearing price depends on what they learned. Other traders can then infer information from the price. A model that conditions portfolio choice only on private research, while treating the resulting price as uninformative, misses this feedback.

Grossman constructs a competitive equilibrium in which one price aggregates many independent noisy signals into a sufficient statistic for a future payoff. Each trader's private signal becomes redundant once the price is observed. This is an exact result under a particular Gaussian information structure and exponential preferences, rather than an informal assertion that crowds are wise.

The same result creates an incentive problem. A price-taking individual who can obtain the pooled information for free from the price has no reason to pay for a redundant private signal. If nobody buys information, however, sufficiently inexpensive research becomes privately valuable. Thus perfect information aggregation and endogenous costly information production need not be jointly sustainable.

The paper also distinguishes conditional allocation efficiency from ex ante welfare. Once signals have been realized and aggregated, competitive allocations can match those of an informed planner. That does not by itself insure investors against wealth changes caused by the arrival of those signals. This qualification appears in the welfare footnotes and is important when translating informational efficiency into economic efficiency.

## Assets, preferences, and information

There are two dates and two assets. One unit of the risk-free investment costs one at date zero and returns $R=1+r$, with $r>0$, at date one. The risky asset costs $P_0$ and has random date-one payoff $P_1$. Trader $i$ has initial wealth $W_{0i}$, invests $X_{Fi}$ in the safe asset, and holds $X_i$ units of the risky asset. The budget and terminal wealth equations are

$$
W_{0i}=X_{Fi}+P_0X_i,
\qquad
W_{1i}=RW_{0i}+(P_1-RP_0)X_i.
$$

Utility is constant-absolute-risk-aversion exponential utility,

$$
U_i(W)=-e^{-a_iW},\qquad a_i>0.
$$

Risk aversion may differ across traders. There are no short-sale constraints, position limits, transaction costs, or price-impact terms in their optimization. The risky asset's aggregate supply $\bar X$ is fixed and known in the principal theorem.

Trader $i$ observes

$$
y_i=P_1+\epsilon_i.
$$

The payoff has common prior distribution $P_1\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, with $\sigma^2>0$. Signal errors are independent of $P_1$ and jointly normal with mean zero and identity covariance: each has variance one and different traders' errors are independent. The unit error variance is a normalization, not an empirical estimate. The main aggregation argument assumes $n>1$ signal types.

All traders know the prior, signal structure, preferences relevant to price formation, and the equilibrium price rule. Their information at trading is $I_i=(y_i,P_0)$. The price is itself a random function of the signal vector $y=(y_1,\ldots,y_n)$ before signals arrive. Rational expectations requires that beliefs about this price rule agree with the prices generated by their resulting demands.

The normal specification permits negative payoffs and prices. The source explicitly imposes no nonnegativity restriction; parameters can make negative-price probability small, but a Gaussian model cannot generally remove it altogether. The theorem is therefore an analytically tractable asset-market model, not a fully constrained model of limited-liability equity.

## Portfolio demand and the equilibrium fixed point

When terminal wealth is conditionally normal, maximizing expected exponential utility is equivalent to maximizing

$$
\mathbb E[W_{1i}\mid I_i]
-\frac{a_i}{2}\operatorname{Var}(W_{1i}\mid I_i).
$$

Substituting the budget equation and differentiating in $X_i$ yields the demand in equation (11), p. 576:

$$
X_i^d=
\frac{\mathbb E[P_1\mid I_i]-RP_0}
{a_i\operatorname{Var}(P_1\mid I_i)}.
$$

This expression is exact for the conditional Gaussian case, not a local mean-variance approximation. Its lack of a wealth term is a consequence of constant absolute risk aversion and unconstrained trading.

For a candidate price rule $p(y)$, equilibrium requires the identity

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{n}
\frac{\mathbb E[P_1\mid y_i,p(y)]-Rp(y)}
{a_i\operatorname{Var}(P_1\mid y_i,p(y))}
=\bar X
$$

for every relevant signal realization. This is equation (12). The candidate price changes both the expected appreciation term and the information used in conditional moments. Solving a clearing equation while freezing beliefs at $\mathbb E[P_1\mid y_i]$ would solve a different problem.

The source describes a learning interpretation: traders could initially ignore the information in prices, repeatedly observe clearing prices and payoffs, and learn their joint distribution. They would then revise their demands upon seeing the prospective clearing price. A rational-expectations equilibrium is a price rule at which this additional inference does not require another revision. This is an interpretation of the equilibrium, not a proved convergence theorem for a specified learning algorithm. No finite learning sample, update gain, or stopping rule is supplied.

## The sufficient statistic and posterior distribution

Define the signal average and total absolute risk tolerance as

$$
\bar y=\frac1n\sum_i y_i,
\qquad S=\sum_i a_i^{-1}.
$$

Given $P_1$, the average signal is normal with mean $P_1$ and variance $1/n$. Its posterior distribution under the common prior has moments

$$
m(\bar y)=\mathbb E[P_1\mid\bar y]
=\frac{\mu+n\sigma^2\bar y}{1+n\sigma^2},
$$

$$
v=\operatorname{Var}(P_1\mid\bar y)
=\frac{\sigma^2}{1+n\sigma^2}.
$$

These are equations (25a)–(25b), pp. 580–581. Posterior precision is prior precision plus $n$ units of signal precision. The variance is constant across realizations, while the posterior mean moves linearly with the pooled signal.

Lemma 1 factors the joint conditional density of $(y_i,\bar y)$ into one factor independent of $P_1$ and another depending on $P_1$ only through $\bar y$. Lemma 2 then applies Bayes' rule to show

$$
\mathcal L(P_1\mid y_i,\bar y)
=\mathcal L(P_1\mid\bar y).
$$

A compact way to understand the same Gaussian calculation is to consider $y_i-\bar y$. It has zero covariance with $P_1$ and $\bar y$ and is jointly normal with them, so it contributes no information about $P_1$ once the average is observed. This is a restatement of the source's density argument, not an assumption that the original signal was uninformative.

Before the price is observed, an individual signal leaves posterior variance $\sigma^2/(1+\sigma^2)$. Pooling leaves $\sigma^2/(1+n\sigma^2)$, strictly smaller for $n>1$. The price will therefore reveal information more precise than any one signal, while not revealing the terminal payoff perfectly at finite $n$. “Fully revealing” here means revealing the pooled sufficient statistic, not eliminating the payoff's remaining uncertainty.

## Equilibrium price and allocations

Theorem 1, p. 578, gives an affine price rule

$$
P_0^*(y)=\alpha_0+\alpha_1\bar y,
$$

with

$$
\alpha_0=
\frac{\mu S-\sigma^2\bar X}
{(1+n\sigma^2)RS},
\qquad
\alpha_1=\frac{n\sigma^2}{(1+n\sigma^2)R}.
$$

Equivalently, and more conveniently for implementation,

$$
RP_0^*=m(\bar y)-\frac{v\bar X}{S}.
$$

The price is the discounted posterior mean less a risk-bearing adjustment. Since $\alpha_1>0$, observing the price reveals $\bar y=(P_0^*-\alpha_0)/\alpha_1$. Consequently, conditioning on one's own signal and price is equivalent, for payoff inference, to conditioning on $\bar y$ alone.

Insert the common posterior moments into each demand:

$$
X_i^d=
\frac{m(\bar y)-RP_0^*}{a_iv}
=\frac{\bar X}{a_iS}.
$$

Summing gives $\sum_i X_i^d=\bar X$, proving market clearing. The demand verification completes the fixed point: a price constructed to reveal the sufficient statistic induces exactly the beliefs and holdings needed to support that price.

The equilibrium risky allocations are proportional to risk tolerance. They do not depend on the realized signal in this special model. News changes the clearing price and thus the value of endowments, while the common posterior and CARA structure leave the allocation of a fixed aggregate risky supply unchanged. This does not mean information has no effect on asset values, or that every model with heterogeneous information has signal-invariant holdings.

The source's footnote 1 addresses an apparent paradox: if everyone can ignore private information in the final equilibrium demand, how does the information enter prices? The displayed demand is an equilibrium demand conditional on a price rule that already incorporates information. It is not a classical off-equilibrium demand curve that can be specified independently of that rule. Simply instructing everyone never to collect signals would remove the informational mechanism supporting the candidate equilibrium.

## Uniqueness and the limits of the result

Theorem 2 proves uniqueness within a given information content. If $P_0^*$ is an equilibrium and a different price $P_0^{**}=H(P_0^*)$ is an invertible strictly monotone transformation, the two prices convey the same information. Conditional means and variances therefore remain unchanged. A lower price strictly increases every trader's demand, so the same aggregate supply cannot clear at both prices. Equality is understood on relevant states, rather than at arbitrary points outside the support.

The article reports uniqueness within the class of all linear price functions, referring to its omitted appendix. That proof is not reproduced in the supplied PDF. The body explicitly leaves open whether nonlinear price functions with different information content can also be equilibria. A faithful reconstruction can verify the displayed affine equilibrium without claiming a global uniqueness result that the supplied article does not establish.

Known, fixed supply is also essential to the clean inversion. If supply is a random variable $x$ independent of payoffs and signal errors and unobserved by traders, equilibrium becomes a function $p(y,x)$. The clearing equation must now match the realized $x$. A rule depending only on $y$ cannot satisfy this identity for different independent supply realizations.

In the regular linear setting, price must mix the aggregate signal with supply variation, preventing direct recovery of the signal average from price alone. A high price can reflect favorable information or low supply. The article gives this argument and the modified equilibrium condition (32), rather than a complete solved noisy-price model with numerical coefficients. Dependence on an additional variable by itself is not a general mathematical impossibility theorem for arbitrary pathological encodings; the economically relevant argument concerns the regular price rules under discussion.

## Costly research and the nonexistence argument

At the fully revealing equilibrium, a trader who stops paying for $y_i$ but continues to observe $P_0^*$ has the same posterior distribution and the same attainable portfolio utility before the cost. Paying any positive cost for that redundant signal is therefore unattractive.

The price-taking interpretation is crucial. The paper distinguishes a signal type from an individual trader of that type. An individual believes that ceasing to acquire information will not change the market's price rule, because other traders still transmit the type's information. This is not a model of a small strategic trader who understands that removing its unique signal changes the entire equilibrium information set.

The same free-riding argument applies if a smaller positive set of signal types remains: the revealing price pools the surviving information. If nobody obtains information, a trader can benefit from research when its cost is sufficiently small. Combining these observations yields the information-acquisition nonexistence problem described on p. 582. The “sufficiently small” qualification for the zero-information deviation matters; prohibitively expensive research need not attract an entrant.

Noise can preserve a private return to research by leaving something valuable that is not inferable from price. The paper identifies this economic mechanism without calibrating how much noise or information expenditure is optimal. It does not provide an empirical alpha hurdle, a manager-fee formula, or a quantitative equilibrium informed fraction. Those would require additional primitives and a solved noisy information-acquisition model.

## Welfare, a reproducible calculation, and practical boundaries

Section 4 compares allocations after a signal realization. Because price reveals a sufficient statistic, a planner who observes the entire signal vector has no superior conditional payoff distribution to exploit. Standard competitive efficiency applies to the exchange economy with these shared conditional preferences. Thus the revealing allocation cannot be conditionally Pareto improved merely by giving the planner the underlying signals.

Footnote 3 limits that conclusion. Before signals arrive, equilibrium prices and endowment values are random. A planner able to insure wealth across signal realizations may improve ex ante expected utilities even when no conditional improvement is possible after each realization. Markets in promises contingent on the future signal realization could provide that additional insurance. Conditional allocative efficiency and ex ante completeness are different claims.

Footnote 4 adds another CARA-normal special case: with common beliefs, risky allocations depend on risk tolerance and supply rather than on which common posterior is held. The informed equilibrium need not Pareto dominate a different equilibrium in which everyone ignores all signals. That observation does not establish a general social irrelevance of information; the author explicitly attributes it to the strong portfolio-separation structure.

A minimal numerical reconstruction requires $n$, $\mu$, $\sigma^2$, the $n$ risk-aversion coefficients, $R$, aggregate supply, and signal realizations. Calculate $\bar y$, $S$, posterior mean and variance, then the price and individual holdings. Verify price inversion, posterior redundancy, and exact market clearing. For repeated draws, generate one common payoff and independent unit-variance Gaussian errors, rather than drawing each trader's signal around a different payoff.

As a constructed check, choose $n=2$, $\mu=10$, $\sigma^2=1$, $R=1.05$, $a_1=a_2=1$, $\bar X=2$, and signals 11 and 13. Then $\bar y=12$, $S=2$, posterior mean is $34/3$, variance is $1/3$, and price is $11/1.05\approx10.47619$. Each trader holds one risky unit. Observing this price recovers the signal average exactly. These values are supplied here to test the equations; they are not a calibration or reported experiment in Grossman.

The model omits noisy or delayed observation of prices, heterogeneous priors, unequal signal precision, signal correlation, strategic market power, constrained portfolios, and the cost of opening markets. It also does not prove that a proposed learning process converges to its rational-expectations equilibrium. Its durable result is a precise tension: prices can aggregate decentralized information so effectively that the individual incentive to produce that information disappears. A realistic implementation must specify both how prices reveal information and why someone still pays to acquire it.
