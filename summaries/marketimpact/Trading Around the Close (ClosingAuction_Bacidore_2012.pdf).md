# Trading Around the Close

Jeff Bacidore, Ben Polidore, Wenjie Xu, and Cindy Y. Yang. Investment Technology Group research paper, 7 November 2012.

Source: [ClosingAuction_Bacidore_2012.pdf](../../Finance/ClosingAuction_Bacidore_2012.pdf). All 11 PDF pages were read, including the exchange-rule footnotes and final references. Page numbers below are PDF page numbers. This is a practitioner research and product paper, rather than a fully documented econometric study. Its institutional descriptions concern the rules presented in 2012.

## Why this matters

The economically relevant time of a closing-auction order is not necessarily the time at which the auction executes. An order submitted to the closing book can affect continuous-market prices when the exchange first publishes the imbalance. If an execution algorithm waits until the final minutes to trade its residual, it may already be paying the price adjustment generated by its own auction demand. The paper makes this timing distinction explicit and connects it to two different investor objectives: implementing a portfolio rebalance and investing or redeeming a fund flow priced at closing net asset value.

The central empirical claim is that the initial public imbalance triggers an immediate price adjustment, after which the average price path through the remainder of continuous trading is approximately flat relative to the initial imbalance. The final auction then tends to print roughly half a quoted spread away from the 4 p.m. midpoint in the imbalance direction. For an institution with a large auction order, this separates the potentially capturable information effect from the remaining liquidity-provision charge.

The contribution is not a new general optimal-control theorem. The authors combine a practical account of exchange rules, 2011 imbalance and volume evidence, and hypothetical trade schedules to motivate the ITG Dynamic Close algorithm. The precise objective distinction is more durable than the particular exchange deadlines. The paper does not disclose the optimizer, numerical calibration, or realized outperformance of the product.

## Auction mechanics and the information set

The Nasdaq description on page 2 distinguishes market-on-close (MOC), limit-on-close (LOC), and imbalance-only (IO) orders. MOC and LOC can participate in the auction independently of an imbalance. IO orders execute against opposite-side imbalances; they are designed to provide offsetting liquidity without adding execution demand on the already-heavy side. The cutoff for ordinary on-close entry and unrestricted changes is 3:50 p.m. The closing imbalance feed starts then and is updated every five seconds. A trader must therefore choose its original MOC or LOC commitment before seeing that feed.

After 3:50 p.m., IO orders can still be entered, with the last entry at 3:59:59. The main text broadly says that modifications are prohibited, but footnote 1 states the relevant exception: an IO order may be increased in quantity or made more aggressive in price after the cutoff. A faithful implementation should preserve that exception rather than treating the prose summary as the complete rule.

The Nasdaq feed contains imbalance direction and quantity, reference price, near price, and far price. Far price uses the closing book, including on-close and IO interest. Near price also incorporates liquidity in the continuous book. Reference and near prices change with the market. At 4 p.m., the continuous and closing books merge; the clearing price maximizes executable shares, with tie-breaking criteria involving imbalance and distance from the quote, and additional price safeguards. Better-priced orders execute at the clearing price. Orders at that price face order-type and time priority, so an LOC limit equal to the close is not a guarantee of completion.

The NYSE rules differ materially, rather than merely starting five minutes earlier. MOC and LOC orders can normally be entered and canceled until 3:45 p.m. Thereafter they are accepted only on the opposite side of a published Regulatory Imbalance. A Closing Offset (CO) order can enter on either side until 4 p.m.; it is a distinct mechanism from late MOC or LOC entry. Informational imbalance messages appear every five seconds from 3:45 p.m., showing reference price, paired quantity, imbalance quantity, and indicative clearing price.

A Regulatory Imbalance is a special message, not a synonym for every nonzero informational imbalance. The paper describes a threshold exceeding 50,000 shares of disparity between MOC and marketable LOC interest, or an imbalance significant relative to average daily volume with the requisite approval. Such messages occur only about 11% of the time in the authors' evidence. A small visible imbalance does not automatically authorize a new offsetting MOC order.

The side restriction is attached to the Regulatory Imbalance as published. In the authors' example, an original 50,000-share sell imbalance permits buy MOC or LOC submissions until the close even if subsequent information shows the imbalance has disappeared or become a buy imbalance. This helps explain why NYSE imbalance direction can reverse. It is not a dynamic rule allowing only orders that reduce the latest imbalance.

Floor brokers and designated market makers have additional access. From 2 p.m. to 3:45 p.m., floor brokers receive closing-book information every 15 seconds, including CO interest unavailable in other feeds. Closing D-quotes may create or increase an imbalance until 3:59:50. They enter the public imbalance calculation at 3:55 p.m. NYSE closing prices are set by the DMM pairing MOC/LOC orders, floor interest, the Display Book, and the DMM's own interest. At-the-price limit interest opposite an imbalance may be filled only partly or not at all. Pages 2-4 and Figures 1-2 establish these differences.

These rules imply three distinct information events: the public's first imbalance, the later inclusion of previously unreported floor interest, and the final match. Treating the entire period as one smooth volume curve loses the order-entry constraints and changes in the public information set.

## What the empirical exercise actually measures

Figure 3 uses all NYSE- and Nasdaq-listed stocks on full trading days in 2011. It plots one-minute volume from 3:30 p.m. to 4 p.m. as a percentage of daily volume, separately by exchange. The figure labels the series as common stocks. The text reports a local volume spike immediately after each exchange's first imbalance message: 3:45 p.m. for NYSE and 3:50 p.m. for Nasdaq. A smaller NYSE spike occurs at 3:55 p.m., when floor interest enters the public computation.

The accompanying imbalance transition study also uses NYSE and Nasdaq common stocks in 2011. It records the initial direction and final direction of the imbalance, rather than merely comparing quantities. For NYSE it includes published order imbalances from 3:45 to 4 p.m.; earlier informational messages are excluded. The Regulatory Imbalance, when present, agrees with the 3:45 message. For Nasdaq, the categories are buy, no imbalance, no cross found, and sell.

The study is descriptive. It supplies neither stock counts nor stock-day counts, vendor identifiers, filtering rules for anomalous messages, detailed ADV construction, or a sampling-error analysis for the transition frequencies. Figure 3's full-day restriction is explicit; it should not silently be assumed to specify every other empirical panel. The paper does not say whether the average volume curve equal-weights stocks, stock-days, or daily volume fractions after some aggregation.

The price analysis separates two horizons: first announcement through the end of continuous trading, and the 4 p.m. midpoint through the actual auction. Figure 5 restricts attention to liquid common stocks, with panel labels specifying daily volume greater than four million shares. Observations are bucketed by initial imbalance as a percentage of ADV, and the within-bucket median return from the 4 p.m. midpoint to the closing print is measured in quoted-spread units. Exact bucket boundaries, spread-sampling convention, and the number of observations per bucket are absent. The figure is not a regression coefficient table.

## Imbalances shrink differently across exchanges

The transition matrices on page 5 convey both a substantial liquidity response and exchange-specific constraints:

| Exchange and initial state | Ends buy | Ends flat | Ends no cross | Ends sell |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Nasdaq buy | 53% | 46% | 0% | 1% |
| Nasdaq flat | 8% | 84% | 0% | 7% |
| Nasdaq no cross | 1% | 0% | 99% | 0% |
| Nasdaq sell | 1% | 49% | 0% | 50% |
| NYSE buy | 83% | 2% | Not reported | 15% |
| NYSE flat | 9% | 81% | Not reported | 10% |
| NYSE sell | 14% | 3% | Not reported | 84% |

Percentages are reproduced as printed and can fail to sum to 100 because of rounding. The source calls these Tables 1 and 2; the surrounding paragraph mistakenly refers to Tables 2 and 3. The actual table captions resolve the numbering.

Nasdaq buy and sell imbalances are neutralized about half the time but almost never reverse. This fits the restriction to offsetting IO entry after the first publication. Yet a reduction cannot be identified entirely with newly arriving offsetting orders: the imbalance is recalculated at a moving reference price. Continuous-market prices moving in the imbalance direction can change which orders are executable and thereby reduce the reported imbalance even without the same change in aggregate submitted demand.

NYSE reversals occur about 14%-15% of initially directional cases. To examine them, the authors select a subsample of Regulatory Imbalance observations whose direction actually flips by the end and follow the normalized imbalance path. Figure 4 expresses each message's quantity relative to the first quantity and plots percentiles. The median at 3:55 p.m. remains in the original direction but is only about 20% of its original size; the pronounced discontinuity occurs when floor interest is added.

That last statistic requires careful conditioning. It is drawn from the selected reversal subsample, not an unconditional statement that every NYSE imbalance loses 80% of its size at 3:55. The figure provides evidence that floor interest offsets previously reported demand and that early public information is incomplete. It does not separately identify brokers' causal response from interest they already held but had not yet disclosed.

## Price discovery before the match

For an actively traded NYSE stock, an initial imbalance equal to 1% of ADV produces about four basis points of price impact in the authors' example. Most of the effect occurs within seconds of publication. After this initial adjustment, the mean subsequent return through continuous trading is reported to be statistically indistinguishable from zero, even for relatively large initial imbalances. The paper does not publish the regression, t-statistic, confidence interval, market-adjustment formula, or exact event window behind that assertion.

The final auction increment behaves differently. Figure 5 shows median 4 p.m.-midpoint-to-auction returns close to half the spread over a wide range of imbalance sizes. A typical buy imbalance thus leaves the auction near the contemporaneous offer, and a sell imbalance near the bid. In the most liquid sample, initial imbalance size appears much more informative about the sign of this last increment than about its magnitude. Some sparse-looking NYSE buckets visibly depart from one-half; without counts or error bars, their interpretation is limited.

A compact representation of the mechanism is useful, but the following notation is explanatory rather than an estimated model in the source. Let $I$ be signed initial imbalance divided by ADV; let $M_-$ denote the midpoint immediately before publication, $M_+$ the midpoint just after adjustment, $M_4$ the last regular-session midpoint, $C$ the auction price, and $s_4$ the corresponding spread. The authors' picture is approximately

$$
E[M_+-M_-\mid I]=g(I),\qquad E[M_4-M_+\mid I]\approx 0,
$$

with

$$
\operatorname{median}(C-M_4\mid I)\approx \frac{s_4}{2}\operatorname{sign}(I).
$$

Here prices must be represented in consistent units; $g(I)$ is not supplied as a fitted function. The four-basis-point example is one scale observation, not a universal linear slope. Nor do these relations imply that every individual price path is flat after announcement. They describe conditional averages and medians over noisy observations.

The distinction explains why allocating 90% to MOC and trading the remaining 10% just before 4 p.m. need not capture the earlier impact created by the auction commitment. The continuous-market residual may avoid part of the final half-spread charge, depending on how it executes, but the main imbalance-information move has already occurred.

## Rebalance and flow objectives

A rebalance exchanges existing portfolio holdings for other holdings. The authors argue that implementation shortfall relative to a pretrade price is the appropriate economic objective. If a buy order raises its own closing benchmark, buying everything at that higher closing price produces zero measured close slippage but does not eliminate the fund's economic cost. With liquidity-induced temporary impact, the investor buys high or sells low and subsequently experiences reversal.

For clarity, write $Q$ for a positive quantity, $d=+1$ for a buy and $d=-1$ for a sell, $P_0$ for a pretrade benchmark, $x_A$ for the auction allocation, and $x_j$ for continuous-market fills at $P_j$, with $x_A+\sum_j x_j=Q$. Ignoring explicit fees only for this accounting identity, implementation shortfall is

$$
IS=d\left[x_A C+\sum_jx_jP_j-QP_0\right].
$$

This is the relevant quantity to reduce for the rebalance argument. It requires a model of how allocations change both $C$ and the continuous-market fills. The paper supplies qualitative timing evidence but not the functional forms needed to solve this minimization numerically.

Small rebalances may be cheapest entirely in the auction if its liquidity dominates continuous trading. As quantity becomes large relative to that liquidity, moving part outside reduces auction impact. Continuous trading should be disproportionately concentrated before first publication to avoid paying the induced price move. Some trading can continue after publication because reducing the remaining auction quantity can still reduce total impact. Figure 7 illustrates this pattern, with a large closing allocation and nonzero pre- and post-announcement trading.

A flow transaction raises cash for redemptions or invests new capital, while the external investor transacts at the fund's closing NAV. The relevant benchmark is now the price paid to that investor. The corresponding close-slippage identity is

$$
CS=d\left[x_A C+\sum_jx_jP_j-QC\right]
=d\sum_jx_j(P_j-C).
$$

The auction component cancels mechanically. For a redemption, if NAV pays the redeeming investor \$20 while the fund realizes only \$19.99 from the sale, the remaining fund investors bear the one-cent shortfall. This example explains whose wealth is affected; it does not claim that all possible fund accounting arrangements have identical incentives.

For a small flow, auction execution provides the required closing benchmark and avoids the execution risk of trying to beat it. For a sufficiently large flow, the institution can trade part before the market observes its on-close demand. The remaining auction commitment then generates a price effect relative to those earlier fills. Trading the same residual after publication pays the already-adjusted price, plus continuous-market costs, while the auction would deliver zero close slippage. Figure 8 consequently illustrates no continuous execution between the first publication and the auction.

The schedules are hypothetical expected-cost illustrations, not empirical optimal weights. The text uses 90% auction and 10% open-market allocations as examples, and the charts carry a five-million buy-order label. No optimization program, participation constraint, impact coefficient, or threshold separating small and large orders is disclosed. Figure 8's internal label says “Rebalance” even though its caption and surrounding section describe a flow trade; the flat post-announcement schedule confirms the intended comparison. The apparent label error should not be converted into a contradictory economic claim.

Risk also matters. Footnote 15 says the illustrated objective minimizes expected shortfall without risk aversion. The authors state that a risk-averse trader would allocate more to the auction and back-load more continuous trading toward 4 p.m. There is no supplied risk functional or frontier, so that statement should not be used to infer a particular urgency parameter.

## Boundaries of the conclusion

The paper's strongest lesson is to align the trading objective with who bears the benchmark slippage, then align the schedule with the time auction information enters prices. It does not establish that every close-targeting algorithm should stop at the first imbalance. That recommendation belongs to the illustrated flow objective and depends on predictable impact, execution costs, information timing, and completion constraints. Rebalances explicitly retain post-announcement trading in the authors' example.

The product description says ITG Dynamic Close offers Rebalance and Flow modes, adapts toward implementation-shortfall reduction or close outperformance, and can offset Regulatory Imbalances. It provides no realized performance table, randomized comparison, independent validation sample, or transaction-cost accounting. Claims of significant practical improvement are therefore proposals motivated by the evidence rather than directly reproducible product-performance results.

A faithful empirical reproduction would need the original message feed, exchange clock alignment, quote definition at 4 p.m., common-stock universe and corporate-action filters, order-imbalance normalization, exact ADV history, event matching around late prints, and the unidentified standard-error procedure. A faithful optimizer reproduction additionally needs the missing impact and risk models. Those are substantive gaps, not minor implementation details that can be filled while still claiming to reproduce the authors' result.

The document is best retained as an early, sharply formulated explanation of endogenous closing benchmarks and the timing of information leakage from auction demand. It supplies useful numerical anchors and institutional distinctions while leaving the optimal execution machinery largely proprietary.
