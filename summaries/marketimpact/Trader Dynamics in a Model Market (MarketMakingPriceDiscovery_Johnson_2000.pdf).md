# Trader Dynamics in a Model Market

Neil F. Johnson, Michael Hart, Pak Ming Hui, and Dafang Zheng. *International Journal of Theoretical and Applied Finance* 3(3), 2000, 443–450. [Supplied PDF](../../Finance/MarketMakingPriceDiscovery_Johnson_2000.pdf), eight pages, read in full with all six figures and equations inspected. References below use journal pages. This short paper presents extensions of an agent-based game and illustrative simulations; it does not estimate an equity-market model or report implementable trading returns.

## Why this matters and what is new

The paper studies two mechanisms that conventional price models often treat as external inputs. First, the profitability of a decision rule depends on how many other traders use related rules. Second, participation depends on confidence and can therefore fluctuate even when the pool of potential traders is fixed. The authors show that mixing traders with different memory lengths can improve average game winnings, and that allowing traders to abstain can create large endogenous changes in activity and the model price.

These are modifications of Challet and Zhang's Minority Game, not a new derivation of that game. The mixed-memory experiment connects the composition of the trading population to crowding and predictability in the history generated by the game itself. The participation experiment replaces mandatory trading with a confidence threshold and compares short- and long-memory populations. The paper provides an approximate crowd-based explanation for the first result and time-series illustrations for the second.

For quantitative research, the value is conceptual and computational. A strategy can become crowded without any change in its individual forecasting rule, and a participation filter can synchronize entry rather than simply scale down risk. Neither result establishes that longer lookbacks earn financial alpha, that abstention causes real-market crashes, or that the model measures actual market depth. The simulated rewards are points for choosing the minority action, and the displayed price is an added accounting rule.

## The underlying game

There are initially $N$ traders, with $N$ odd. At each discrete time $t$, every trader chooses room 0 or room 1, interpreted illustratively as buying or selling. Traders in the less crowded room win. The winning room is encoded as a binary outcome $y_t$, and the public information consists only of the sequence of these outcomes. There is no fundamental value, dividend, private signal, or external return series.

A trader with memory $m$ observes the last $m$ outcome bits. There are $2^m$ possible histories. A deterministic strategy is a binary lookup table assigning an action to every possible history, so the full set contains $2^{2^m}$ strategies. The history space has dimension $m$; the strategy representation has $2^m$ binary coordinates. Confusing these objects would radically change a simulator's state space.

At initialization each trader draws $s$ strategies, with replacement. After a turn, every strategy in that trader's collection receives one virtual point if it would have predicted the winning room, whether or not the trader used it. The trader receives a separate real point if the action actually chosen was in the minority. At the next turn the trader uses a highest-scoring strategy. These scores therefore distinguish a counterfactual forecasting record from the realized success of the trader's selected decisions.

The source does not specify tie-breaking between equally scored strategies, the initial history, initialization of strategy scores, the random seed, the exact draw distribution beyond random selection, or run length and burn-in. Uniform independent draws and random tie-breaking are natural implementation choices, but should be labeled as such. They are not recoverable author settings. The basic algorithm is otherwise clear: form the history; choose the best current strategy; collect simultaneous actions; determine the minority; award virtual and real points; append the outcome and repeat.

The agents do not solve a dynamic optimization problem. They follow a score-based adaptive rule. There is no budget, position size, inventory constraint, borrowing, bid–ask spread, or wealth-dependent participation in the basic game. A point is not a dollar payoff, and the model does not derive demand from expected utility.

## Crowding, the exact reward identity, and the approximation

Let $B_t$ denote the number choosing room 0. The number of winners is $\min(B_t,N-B_t)$, so the average number of real points per trader on that turn is exactly

$$
W_t=\frac{\min(B_t,N-B_t)}{N}
=\frac12-\frac{|B_t-N/2|}{N}.
$$

Because $N$ is odd, $W_t\le(N-1)/(2N)<1/2$. For $N=101$, the best possible aggregate allocation awards 50 points, or approximately 0.49505 per trader. A hypothetical external coin-toss predictor of the winning room can succeed half the time because it does not enter the population and change which room is the minority. This illustrates the distinction between forecasting a fixed sequence and participating in a market-like system whose sequence depends on actions.

The source approximates average winnings by $W\sim0.5-\sigma/N$, where $\sigma$ is the standard deviation of the number choosing a given action. This is a heuristic replacement of the mean absolute deviation in the exact identity by a standard-deviation scale. It is not an algebraic equality. Even with a centered Gaussian approximation, mean absolute deviation is $\sqrt{2/\pi}\,\sigma$, not $\sigma$. The paper does not estimate that shape correction; reproducing its analytic curves means retaining its stated approximation while recognizing the distinction.

Crowding arises because many agents may select the same currently successful rule. More available strategies per trader can intensify coordinated choice of a highly ranked rule rather than diversify the population. The source invokes a crowd/anticrowd description, using a reduced strategy count $2\cdot2^m$ in its crowded-regime criterion $2\cdot2^m\ll Ns$. That reduced count is different from the full number $2^{2^m}$ stated in the preceding discussion. The connection to the reduced crowd/anticrowd representation is inherited from the cited earlier work; a simulator should not silently replace the full lookup-table universe with this reduced number unless deliberately implementing that approximation.

## Mixed-memory design and results

The numerical experiment fixes total population at 101 and splits it between memory-three and memory-six traders:

$$
N_3+N_6=101,
\qquad x=N_3/101.
$$

In Figure 1, every memory-six trader holds seven strategies, while the memory-three traders' strategy count varies across six cases, $s_1=2,3,\ldots,7$. The horizontal axis varies $N_3$ from a pure memory-six population toward a pure memory-three population. Figure 2 reverses the role of the strategy counts: $s_1=7$ throughout, and $s_2=2,\ldots,7$ for memory-six traders. Data are described as long-time averages over many runs; neither the number of repetitions nor uncertainty intervals is reported.

Figure 1 demonstrates that finite mixing can increase average winnings above the homogeneous endpoints. For the $s_1=2,s_2=7$ case, the visually read average rises from about 0.42 at the memory-six end to roughly 0.457 near 60 memory-three traders, before falling toward about 0.43 at the memory-three end. These are approximate readings from the graph, not tabulated estimates. Increasing the number of strategies for memory-three traders lowers the right-hand side strongly: the $s_1=7$ endpoint is around 0.33. The effect is nonlinear and does not say that all mixtures dominate all pure populations.

Figure 2 makes the qualification especially clear. With $s_1=7$ and $s_2=2$, the pure memory-six population achieves approximately 0.48, and adding memory-three traders initially reduces performance. Several curves with larger $s_2$ have modest intermediate improvement, but eventually converge toward the low memory-three endpoint. Memory length, strategy diversity, and population composition interact. There is no universal “more heterogeneity is always better” conclusion.

The information process is endogenous. The authors report that replacing actual game history with randomly generated history fails to reproduce Figures 1–2. Their explanation is that memory-three traders leave correlations in longer outcome sequences that memory-six traders can exploit. Shorter-memory agents cannot condition on those longer patterns; longer-memory agents can. The random-history comparison is described but not plotted or quantified, so the paper supplies a qualitative robustness result without a separate effect estimate.

Calling this an arbitrage opportunity is informal. The game provides no asset whose payoff can be locked in without risk. What is demonstrated is exploitable conditional structure within a particular adaptive population. The feedback between that structure and the population using it is central to the mechanism.

## Analytic approximation for the mixed population

The authors assume that contributions to action-count fluctuations from the two memory groups can be added as variances:

$$
\sigma^2\approx\sigma_3^2+\sigma_6^2,
\qquad
\sigma_3\approx C_3xN,
\qquad
\sigma_6\approx C_6(1-x)N.
$$

This suppresses the cross-group covariance term. The paper motivates it as removal of internal frustration between groups, but it is an approximation, not an established independence theorem. The text momentarily calls $\sigma_3$ and $\sigma_6$ variances even though their use in the displayed decomposition and linear scaling is as standard-deviation contributions; the equations determine the intended interpretation.

Equation (3.1) defines

$$
C_m=\frac12\left\{\sum_r
\left[
\left(1-\frac{r-1}{2\cdot2^m}\right)^s
-\left(1-\frac{r}{2\cdot2^m}\right)^s
\right]^2\right\}^{1/2}.
$$

The rank $r$ labels weakly related groups of strategies, each incorporating a crowd and an oppositely acting anticrowd. The difference between the two powers is the order-statistic probability that a best-ranked choice falls at a particular rank when an agent holds $s$ strategies. Squaring and summing provides the fluctuation contribution used in this crowd approximation.

For the short-memory group, the source keeps only $r=1$. For the memory-six group it sums up to

$$
R_6=\min\{N_6,2\cdot2^6/2\}
=\min\{N_6,64\}.
$$

Thus $C_6$ depends on population composition through the truncation as well as on $s_2$. A reproduction must not hold it fixed across every value of $x$. The corresponding $C_3$ uses $m=3$ and $s=s_1$; $C_6$ uses $m=6$ and $s=s_2$.

Substituting these expressions into the reward approximation yields equation (3.2):

$$
W\approx\frac12-
\sqrt{C_3^2x^2+C_6^2(1-x)^2}.
$$

Figures 3–4 apply this formula to the same parameter combinations as Figures 1–2. They reproduce the broad nonlinear shape, including interior maxima for some mixtures. The approximation tends to understate simulated winnings. The authors interpret that gap as evidence of additional inter-trader cooperation or correlation requiring a fuller theory. No fitted correction, residual table, or goodness-of-fit statistic is supplied.

For intuition only, if both coefficients were held fixed and positive, minimizing the expression inside the square root would give $x^*=C_6^2/(C_3^2+C_6^2)$. This is a derived property of the simplified formula, not an optimization result reported or used in the paper. The actual composition dependence of $C_6$ and omitted cross-group covariance limit that shortcut. The source's objective is explanatory approximation rather than estimation of an optimal investor allocation across lookback horizons.

## Participation based on confidence

The second extension allows an agent to remain inactive. Confidence is measured by the recent success rate $r$ of the best-performing strategy over a trailing window of $T$ turns. An agent participates only when confidence reaches the threshold $r_{\min}$; below the threshold it watches and continues to evaluate strategies. This distinguishes the memory length $m$, which defines the lookup-table input, from $T$, which defines the evaluation window.

The source is not fully explicit about whether “best-performing” in this extension is selected by the original cumulative virtual score or by the trailing-window success rate itself. It also leaves equality at the threshold, active-population ties, and turns with no participants unspecified. Those conventions matter because an odd potential population does not guarantee an odd active population. When buys and sells tie, or nobody trades, the next winning-history bit needs an explicit rule before a simulation is well defined.

Let $N_{\mathrm{buy}}(t)$ and $N_{\mathrm{sell}}(t)$ count active decisions. The model's activity measure is

$$
N_{\mathrm{active}}(t)=N_{\mathrm{buy}}(t)+N_{\mathrm{sell}}(t).
$$

The authors call this quantity volume. It counts participants, with effectively unit-sized actions, rather than matched shares or dollar turnover. Equation (4.1) defines an illustrative price:

$$
P(t+1)=P(t)+
\frac{N_{\mathrm{buy}}(t)-N_{\mathrm{sell}}(t)}{D},
$$

where $D$ is constant. A positive $D$ converts order imbalance into an additive price move. Its numerical value, initial price, and physical units are not provided. It is not estimated order-book depth. The rule does not enforce inventory conservation, a clearing counterparty, or positive prices over arbitrarily long paths. Its purpose is to translate coordinated decisions into a visual price series.

The displayed experiments use $N=1001$, two strategies per trader, $T=500$, and $r_{\min}=0.51$. Figure 5 uses memory six; Figure 6 uses memory two. For memory six, the plotted activity stays above zero, generally ranging from a few dozen to a few hundred participants in the displayed segment, and the price moves without comparably abrupt one-step jumps. The memory-two example has frequent zero-activity periods, spikes reaching several hundred participants, and abrupt price changes. These are selected trajectory segments, not distributions of jump sizes or event frequencies.

The authors say that larger $T$ produces similar results but provide no range, comparative figure, or quantitative sensitivity. They also state that activity fluctuations are especially large around $r_{\min}=0.5$, resembling a phase transition. The referenced result is unpublished and the threshold-sweep plot is absent. This supports reporting a proposed critical-point interpretation, not a measured critical exponent or a formal proof of criticality.

Transaction costs motivate the discussion of confidence thresholds, but costs do not enter the simulations as monetary debits. A threshold of 0.51 cannot be translated into a spread, fee, or expected net return without a payoff model. Similarly, the source's examples of Vodafone in November 1995 and Hanson in January 1996 are qualitative analogies to large simultaneous price and volume changes, not an empirical validation sample.

## What a faithful reconstruction can establish

A faithful implementation can reproduce the stated rules and parameter sweeps while making missing conventions explicit. It should keep strategy virtual scores separate from realized points, preserve actual endogenous histories for the main mixed-population results, calculate $W$ from minority counts, and compare those simulated values with the approximate crowd formula. For the confidence extension, it should separately store lookup memory, score history, participation, and imbalance before applying the price update.

Exact figure matching is not possible from the article alone because run lengths, seeds, tie handling, inactive-history updates, and the price scale are missing. Numerical precision should therefore be judged against the broad shapes and qualitative differences, with simulation uncertainty estimated by a new implementation rather than attributed to the authors. The supplied paper has no historical universe, sample exclusion rules, transaction-cost backtest, or out-of-sample forecast test.

The results isolate two useful endogenous mechanisms within a small game: heterogeneous information-processing horizons can relieve some crowding, and common confidence rules can make liquidity participation discontinuous. Their limitations are equally specific. The crowd approximation neglects cross-group dependence and replaces mean absolute imbalance with a standard-deviation scale; the participation experiment leaves several transition conventions undocumented; and the price series is generated from imbalance without a full financial market. These qualifications define where the model is informative and prevent its simulated points and visual jumps from being mistaken for measured investment performance.
