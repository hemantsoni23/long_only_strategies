# Do Behavioral Biases Affect Prices?

**Authors:** Joshua D. Coval and Tyler Shumway. **Publication:** *Journal of Finance* 60(1), February 2005, pp. 1–34. **Source:** [AssetPricingBias_CovalShumway_2005.pdf](../../Finance/AssetPricingBias_CovalShumway_2005.pdf), 35 PDF pages including the archive cover. Page references below use printed journal pages; add one for PDF pages. The complete article, tables and figure were inspected. This is an observational microstructure study, not a structural estimation of prospect-theory utility or an investment-strategy backtest.

## Why this matters and what is new

The paper connects a trader's own recent profit and loss to subsequent risk-taking, aggressive trading, and the persistence of the prices those trades establish. That chain is more informative than simply finding unusually high turnover or price reversals. Professional proprietary traders who lose during the morning take more afternoon risk and more often cross the effective spread. Their position-expanding trades subsequently reverse more than comparable trades by morning winners. Behavioral distortions can therefore be visible in individual decisions and transaction prices even when competition removes much of their price effect within minutes.

The setting provides unusually direct identification of the decision maker, capital at risk, and evaluation horizon. These are Chicago Board of Trade Treasury-bond futures locals trading their own accounts, rather than retail brokerage customers or delegated portfolio managers. Daily statements and typically small overnight positions make the trading day a plausible reference period. Own-account trading weakens the alternative explanation that risk-taking responds to convex fund flows or compensation contracts. It does not remove wealth effects, financing constraints, omitted information, or errors in inferred inventories.

The innovation is the sequence of linked tests: morning profits versus afternoon exposure; morning profits versus price-setting trades; price-setting trades versus subsequent reversals; and the cross-section of morning losses versus aggregate afternoon volatility. The last link is substantially less conclusive than the first three because the effective sample is only 236 days. The source's language of “loss aversion” primarily refers here to increased gambling after losses relative to the daily reference point. It is not an estimate of the slope discontinuity or curvature of an individual utility function, and it does not establish that every alternative behavioral bias is absent.

## Competing mechanisms and identification

Let a trader's morning profit be $\Pi^M$ and subsequent risk exposure be $Q^A$. The benchmark null is that their conditional relation is zero after controlling for relevant state variables. This requires more than rationality: morning and afternoon profit opportunities must be uncorrelated, wealth effects negligible, margin constraints unimportant, and compensation and reputation incentives neutral. These auxiliary conditions are central to interpreting a rejection.

Self-attribution bias can make a successful trader overconfident about interpreting order flow; representativeness can make a profitable morning seem excessively informative about afternoon opportunities; and the house-money effect can increase willingness to gamble after gains. Each predicts a positive profit–risk relation in this setting. Risk-seeking over losses predicts the opposite. Ordinary declining absolute risk aversion also predicts more risk after gains if the gains materially change wealth. A binding margin restriction can reduce risk after losses, whereas gambling to escape an impending margin call can mimic risk-seeking over losses. The study therefore uses normalization, inventory controls, alternative samples and nonlinear specifications, rather than interpreting a simple unconditional difference as decisive.

A disposition effect supplies another mechanism: a losing trader may carry a losing existing position longer, mechanically increasing measured afternoon risk. The authors explicitly test the hazard of unwinding midday positions and separately examine aggressive trades that expand inventory. The latter distinction matters because initiating additional exposure is stronger evidence of active risk-seeking than passively retaining an inherited position. There is no randomized loss shock or instrument in the article; causal language should remain conditional on these controls and institutional arguments.

## Data, sample and trading-day construction

The primary data are the complete 1998 audit trail of the CBOT T-bond futures pit, obtained from the CFTC. Each transaction records buyer and seller identities, price, timestamp and an account-purpose code distinguishing customer, clearing-firm, other-trader and personal-account trades. There are more than five million transactions and 1,082 trader identities. Front-month contracts account for 97.4% of transactions and are the focus. At least one participant trades personally in 96.6% of front-month transactions.

The analyzed 426 locals each execute at least 1,500 personal-account trades and participate on at least 100 days during the year. All tracked transactions for these locals are personal-account transactions. The principal panel contains 82,595 trader-days across 236 full trading days. These are ex-post sample-selection criteria, not an online eligibility rule that could have been applied at the beginning of 1998. The article does not provide the raw audit trail, the identities or an executable extraction program.

The floor session runs from 7:20 a.m. to 2:00 p.m. Morning ends at 11:00 a.m.; the afternoon is the remainder of the session. The literal time midpoint is 10:40, but the authors choose 11:00 to better approximate the midpoint of the typical lunch break. Reimplementation should preserve this split rather than substitute noon or equal-duration halves. Partial trading days are excluded by the full-day design. The exact contract-roll rule and treatment of tied transaction timestamps outside the price-setting analysis are not exhaustively specified.

Assume zero opening inventory each day. Signed personal-account trades accumulate to inventory $I_i(u)$ at intraday time $u$. Cash received from sales minus cash paid for purchases, plus current marked-to-market inventory, gives running trading profit. Schematically, with signed purchases $q_{ik}$ and contract dollar multiplier $M$,

$$
I_i(u)=\sum_{k:t_k\le u}q_{ik},\qquad
\Pi_i(u)=M\left[-\sum_{k:t_k\le u}q_{ik}P_k+I_i(u)P(u)\right].
$$

This is a reconstruction of the accounting described on pp. 9–10, not an extra estimated model. Morning profit is the 11:00 mark, and afternoon profit is subsequent profit over the afternoon. Errors arise if opening positions exist, if locals hedge with options, close positions in evening sessions or transact through other locals. Around 65% of trader-days appear to end with absolute inventory of ten contracts or less, supporting but not proving the zero-inventory approximation. Inventory-dependent variables are winsorized at the first and 99th percentiles. The paper does not fully specify whether every cutoff is pooled or trader-specific; that choice should be logged in a replication.

## Risk measurement and normalization

The authors use three exposure measures: transaction count, mean trade size, and cumulative risk-weighted inventory, called total dollar risk. The last requires a separate intraday price-risk model estimated from Futures Industry Institute Data Center time-and-sales prices for 1989–1998. Beginning-of-minute front-month prices yield absolute one-minute changes. An ordered logit predicts categories of the next minute's absolute price change from the magnitudes of changes during the preceding five minutes and time-of-day indicators for five-minute intervals. Fitted category probabilities imply the expected absolute price change for each minute of each full 1998 trading day.

Writing category representative moves as $a_j$ and predicted probabilities as $p_{jm}$, the minute risk forecast is $v_m=\sum_j a_jp_{jm}$. The trader's exposure is obtained by multiplying this risk by position size and adjusting for inventory changes within the minute; minute exposures are summed over the morning or afternoon. The source does not disclose all category boundaries, threshold estimates, fitting-window details, or the exact within-minute inventory adjustment. These are required to reproduce total dollar risk exactly. Absolute rather than signed position exposure is economically necessary for a risk measure, but the prose does not spell out every implementation operation.

The measure is roughly a standard-deviation-like dollar exposure at a one-minute horizon. Summing such exposures is not generally the standard deviation of cumulative trading P&L: that would require accounting for intertemporal covariance, signs and holding intervals. It should therefore be treated as the paper's cumulative exposure proxy. Trade count and trade size provide checks that do not depend on the same inferred-inventory construction.

Profits are divided by each trader's full-sample standard deviation, separately for mornings and afternoons, without subtracting the mean. This retains zero profit as the economically relevant reference point. Risk measures are separately demeaned and divided by their trader-specific morning or afternoon standard deviations. Thus,

$$
\pi^M_{it}=\frac{\Pi^M_{it}}{s_i(\Pi^M)},\qquad
R^A_{it}=\frac{Q^A_{it}-\overline Q_i^A}{s_i(Q^A)}.
$$

The absolute 11:00 inventory control is also demeaned and standardized by trader. The regression prose additionally describes inventory measured in thousands of contracts, creating a units issue to resolve against original code before recreating interactions or hazard effects. Normalizations use the whole sample; this is suitable for the stated descriptive analysis but must be replaced with lagged estimates in a genuine predictive trading application. Price-setting counts are demeaned but not standardized because they are sparse.

Table I gives mean morning/afternoon profits of \$1,808.33/\$661.78, compared with medians of \$750/\$187.50. Mean trade counts are 116.62 and 73.25, and mean sizes 10.03 and 9.35 contracts. Large raw profit standard deviations and much smaller medians indicate pronounced heterogeneity. There are 55,877 profitable-morning observations and 26,718 losing-morning observations. Losing mornings are followed by normalized afternoon trade count 0.124 versus −0.066, size 0.086 versus −0.046, and dollar risk 0.141 versus −0.100. Morning losers already took more morning risk, making controls essential.

## Main conditional risk-taking tests

The baseline equation, estimated separately for the three risk measures, is

$$
R^A_{it}=\alpha+\beta_\pi\pi^M_{it}+\beta_I J^M_{it}
+\beta_{\pi I}\pi^M_{it}J^M_{it}+\beta_RR^M_{it}+\epsilon_{it},
$$

where $J^M$ is the normalized absolute midday inventory. Morning risk controls for persistence in trader activity; inventory controls inherited exposure; the interaction allows winning and losing positions to be unwound differently. Because of the interaction, $\beta_\pi$ alone is the marginal profit coefficient at zero normalized inventory, not a constant effect at every inventory level.

Four estimators are compared: pooled OLS; separate time-series regressions for each trader with coefficients averaged across traders; separate daily cross-sectional regressions averaged over days; and trader/date fixed effects with panel-corrected standard errors allowing heteroskedasticity and contemporaneous dependence across locals. “Fama–MacBeth by trader” is therefore a coefficient-averaging exercise across trader time series, whereas “by date” averages daily cross-sections. These methods stress different dependence structures; they do not by themselves establish unrestricted robustness to all serial and cross-sectional dependence.

Table II's morning-profit coefficients are:

Parentheses contain reported $t$ statistics. The rendered table really prints −0.7061 for the trade-size fixed-effect estimate, whereas the text describes a 7–11% standard-deviation effect and neighboring estimates are around −0.07 to −0.11. This is an apparent source inconsistency; do not silently substitute −0.07061 as though verified. The other estimators independently support the central conclusion. The dollar-risk response after controls is economically much smaller than the count response: a one-standard-deviation lower morning profit corresponds to approximately 0.8–1.6% of a standard deviation more dollar risk.

The authors report stronger results when excluding mornings more than two trader-specific profit standard deviations from zero. To address differences between winners and losers, they also retain all losers and an equal-sized set of the largest winners on each day. This leaves 65,061 trader-days; dollar-risk coefficients remain −0.0078, −0.0095, −0.0151 and −0.0085, with $t$ statistics −2.83, −2.10, −3.57 and −2.58 respectively. These checks weaken explanations based purely on extreme losses, although actual margin balances are unobserved.

Daily conditional double sorts first assign morning-profit quintiles and then morning-risk quintiles within each profit group. Afternoon risk is averaged in each of the 25 cells. Table III generally shows more afternoon exposure after low profits at comparable morning-risk levels, but monotonicity is not exact in every cell; the source itself notes an upturn for high-profit/high-dollar-risk observations. The equivalent inventory-based sorts are reported as similar but not tabulated.

## Nonlinearities, unwinding and the relevant horizon

A binary logit asks whether the standardized afternoon risk exceeds zero, that is, whether risk exceeds that trader's average. Its predictor includes a morning-loss indicator, inventory, their interaction and morning risk. The paper writes the loss-indicator term as $-\beta_\pi1\{\pi^M<0\}$, but Table IV's dollar-risk panels report positive coefficients while the narrative describes a positive loss effect. Panels A and B and Panel C therefore cannot all be interpreted mechanically with the same printed sign convention. The paper also gives 31.2% versus 27.0% in the introduction and 31.3% versus 26.9% later. The substantive reported difference is roughly four percentage points, or 15–16% relative, rather than a 15-percentage-point increase. Reproduce the binary model from data using an explicitly coded loss indicator before relying on the table for exact predicted probabilities.

The semiparametric specification uses 20 profit groups, group indicators, group-by-inventory interactions and morning-risk controls. Daily coefficient estimates are averaged and kernel-smoothed for Figure 1, with two-standard-error bands. Footnote 20 says group boundaries are formed using the entire year's profits so the zero-profit threshold does not shift across the year, although surrounding prose describes daily rankings. Preserve that qualification when constructing bins. The figure's risk-taking slope is steep over losses, flattens near the zero-profit threshold around the 30th percentile, and is relatively flat over gains. The small upturn at the highest profits is not statistically significant evidence for a house-money effect. Kernel and bandwidth details are not provided.

The Cox proportional-hazard model measures time after 11:00 until the midday position is unwound. Its covariates are signed inventory times the difference between the 11:00 price and the contract-weighted acquisition price, total morning profit, absolute inventory, and morning risk. A higher marked-to-market position value raises the unwind hazard, consistent with reluctance to realize losses. The pooled position-value coefficient is 0.0283 ($t=5.11$); morning profit is 0.0321 ($t=5.49$). The source translates a 30-contract position one tick underwater into a roughly 2.9% lower hazard, and a one-standard-deviation overall morning loss into another roughly 3.3% reduction. Such percentages are proportional hazard changes, not added minutes of holding time. Trader-by-trader averages lose significance, while daily cross-sectional averages remain significant. The article does not fully document censoring at the close or position-crossing treatment.

Finally, normalized next-day risk is regressed on the previous day's profit and risk, omitting inventory under the zero-overnight-position assumption. Neither continuous nor binary specifications find a detectable relation. These unreported estimates support the importance of the daily reference horizon, but the absence of published coefficients and uncertainty prevents a precise bound on multiday effects.

## Making the event-time and binary models explicit

The duration test can be implemented as a Cox model $h_i(u\mid z_i)=h_0(u)\exp(\beta^\top z_i)$, where event time $u$ starts at 11:00 and the event is liquidation of the inventory present at that time. Covariates $z_i$ are fixed using morning information in the reported specification. The baseline hazard is not a parametric distribution of holding times. A coefficient changes the instantaneous conditional liquidation rate multiplicatively; it does not directly forecast a holding period without the baseline hazard and a censoring convention. In particular, exposure added after 11:00 needs a declared rule for matching disposals to the original midday position. The article's absence of that detailed matching rule limits exact duration replication even though its economic contrast is clear.

For the binary risk outcome, write $Y_{it}=1\{R^A_{it}>0\}$ and fit $\Pr(Y_{it}=1\mid z_{it})=\{1+\exp[-z_{it}^\top\beta]\}^{-1}$ with a positive-one morning-loss dummy. This is an explicit coding convention for reconstructing the source's logit after resolving its printed sign inconsistency. A loss coefficient is a log-odds shift conditional on the other regressors; it is not itself a probability difference. Compute the loss-versus-gain contrast at identical covariates or report an explicitly defined average marginal effect. With a dummy–inventory interaction, the contrast varies with inventory. That is why the approximately four-percentage-point probability comparison cannot be inferred from one table coefficient alone.

Neither model establishes a structural reference-dependent utility function. The duration equation concerns an existing position and a stopping decision; the binary equation concerns whether total afternoon activity exceeds an individual's typical level. Their different outcomes make agreement informative, but also make it inappropriate to pool their coefficients as repeated measurements of one common risk-aversion parameter. A faithful replication preserves this separation before asking whether a richer utility model can explain both.

## From aggressive trades to temporary price impact

A local's trade is classified as price-setting when a purchase is above the previous transaction price or a sale below it. This is a transaction-price rule, not a comparison with observed quotes; the audit trail does not record contemporaneous bid and ask prices. The authors discard trades sharing a timestamp second with another trade to improve confidence about ordering and attribution. This restriction is important in a busy pit and may select unusual, less congested events. The retained rate is only 0.327 price-setting trades per local-afternoon.

Regress the trader-demeaned afternoon count on normalized morning profit, absolute midday inventory and their interaction. Table VI gives profit coefficients −0.0493 ($t=-18.37$) in pooled OLS, −0.0474 in trader averages, −0.0610 in date averages and −0.0484 under fixed effects. Dividing 0.0474–0.0610 by the stated mean 0.327 yields approximately 14.5–18.7% more price-setting trades for a one-standard-deviation lower profit at the reference inventory level. The narrative says 20–25%; that does not follow directly from the printed mean and coefficients. Retain the estimates and explicitly distinguish the arithmetic from the narrative claim.

The association appears separately for inventory expansion and contraction. Pooled coefficients are −0.0251 ($t=-13.80$) and −0.0291 ($t=-15.69$). Thus some increased activity unwinds existing positions, but some actively adds risk. This helps rule out an account relying entirely on slow liquidation. The count regression does not measure permanent information impact, because crossing a spread can move transaction prices without changing the efficient price.

To study permanence, condition on the last four prices. Relative to the preceding price-change direction, each subsequent movement is a continuation (C) or reversal (R), creating CC, CR, RC and RR paths. The example 25–24–25–26 is RC. RR accounts for 77% of classified events, CR for 15%, RC for 7% and CC for 1%. Further divide by inventory expansion/contraction and morning gain/loss, then measure the subsequent five-minute reversal. One- and ten-minute windows reportedly give similar results. The table caption describes a fraction of the initial move, while the column heading and discussion use ticks; the paper argues moves are nearly always one tick, but these units should not be assumed equivalent for multi-tick events.

Table VII's frequency-weighted mean reversal for position-expanding trades is 1.0122 after losses versus 0.7967 after gains, a difference of 0.2155 with $t=4.7$. The relative difference is approximately 27.0%. For contracting trades the corresponding numbers are 0.8035 and 0.8166, difference −0.0131 with $t=-0.3$. The strong loss effect is therefore concentrated in active expansion. In the dominant RR category it is 0.1510 ($t=3.1$); in RC it is 0.5498 ($t=3.0$). Rare CC events have a large 2.0371 difference but weaker precision ($t=1.9$). These are conditional execution-price reversals, not a market-neutral strategy return net of spread, latency or fees.

## Aggregate volatility, replication limits and implications

The final time-series regression explains standardized afternoon price-change volatility at second, minute, five-minute, ten-minute and half-day horizons using corresponding morning volatility and the fraction of selected traders who lost that morning. General loss fractions and average normalized losses have no significant relation. Restricting to traders classified as loss-averse, or as loss-sensitive price leaders, produces positive short-horizon estimates. Classification uses fitted trader behavior; no separate training period is specified, so this is an in-sample diagnostic with generated groups.

Table VIII reports a coefficient of 2.09 ($t=2.01$) on the loss-averse loss fraction at one second, 1.90 ($t=1.80$) at five minutes, 1.17 ($t=1.08$) at ten minutes and 0.81 ($t=0.70$) at the half-day horizon. For the price-leader group the corresponding coefficients are 2.37, 2.34, 1.39 and 1.40. Inference adjusts for first-order autocorrelation; the particular correction procedure is not fully described. With loss-fraction standard deviation 0.055, the first coefficient implies $2.09\times0.055=0.115$ standard deviations of the standardized volatility outcome. The prose calls this an 11.5% increase in volatility, but conversion to percentage change in raw volatility would require its mean and standard deviation. Analogous ten-minute and half-day effects are 0.064 and 0.045 standardized units, statistically insignificant. Do not confuse standardized effect sizes with proportional changes in raw variance or volatility.

A faithful replication needs the complete account-coded audit trail, chronological transaction ordering, a declared front-contract rule, price multipliers, inventory accounting, all normalization and winsorization conventions, the ordered-logit risk model, timestamp exclusion rules, and the exact formation of price-path groups and post-event prices. Save intermediate trader-minute inventories, trader-day profits, risk measures and qualifying price-setting events before estimation. Reproduce the descriptive counts and Table II before interpreting Table VII. Resolve the source's printed logit sign, table decimal and units discrepancies through recomputation rather than choosing values that fit the conclusion.

The practical finding is narrower and more useful than a generic claim that irrational traders move markets. Even experienced own-account market makers seek risk after losses, and that preference shows up in worse short-term execution outcomes when they expand inventory. Competitive liquidity provision then limits persistence. A risk manager could use the design to distinguish exposure added after losses from necessary liquidation, but implementation would require independent validation and real-time normalization. The article offers no calibrated position cap or automated intervention threshold. For asset-pricing interpretation, it demonstrates a behavioral contribution to very short-horizon transaction-price variation while providing little support for long-lived fundamental mispricing in this particularly liquid market.
