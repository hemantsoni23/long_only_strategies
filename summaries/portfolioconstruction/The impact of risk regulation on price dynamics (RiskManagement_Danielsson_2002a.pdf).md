# The impact of risk regulation on price dynamics

**Jón Daníelsson, Hyun Song Shin, and Jean-Pierre Zigrand (2004).** *Journal of Banking & Finance* 28, 1069–1087, DOI 10.1016/S0378-4266(03)00113-4. Source: [RiskManagement_Danielsson_2002a.pdf](../../Finance/RiskManagement_Danielsson_2002a.pdf). The filename reflects an earlier year; the PDF is the 2004 journal article, received in 2002 and accepted in 2003. All 19 pages, including six figures and references, are covered. The study is a theoretical simulation experiment, not an empirical estimate of the effect of actual regulation.

## Why this matters

A risk limit can be prudent for an individual institution yet amplify aggregate price movements when many institutions respond to the same estimated risk. This paper builds an explicit feedback loop: historical returns determine risk forecasts; those forecasts constrain portfolios; market clearing determines prices; and the resulting returns feed the next forecasts. The distribution of returns is consequently an outcome of the risk-management rule as well as of fundamental shocks.

Its incremental contribution is the combination of a solvable one-period equilibrium with backward-looking belief updating across periods. Unlike a model in which traders know the true stochastic law, traders here estimate moments from the return history generated by their own collective actions. Regulated and unregulated economies are driven by the same exogenous payoff sequence, so their differing price paths isolate the effect of the constraint within the simulated structure.

The central mechanism is an increase in effective risk aversion among constrained traders. Primitive preferences do not change, but a binding variance limit produces the same demand reduction as a larger absolute-risk-aversion coefficient. Assets move from relatively risk-tolerant investors toward more risk-averse investors, requiring a larger risk premium for positive aggregate-risk exposures. The simulations show lower prices, larger forecast risk, and more persistent responses to adverse shocks.

The paper explicitly stops short of a welfare verdict against risk regulation. It omits the agency problems, depositor protection, and other benefits that can justify regulation. Its results identify a possible system-wide cost in a particular model, not the net desirability of capital rules.

## Assets, horizons, and trader population

Time is discrete and interpreted as daily in the simulation. There is one risk-free asset and two risky assets. Each asset bought at date $t$ matures at $t+1$, paying $d_{i,t+1}$, and traders roll into a new one-period asset. Risky gross returns are

$$
R_{i,t+1}=\frac{d_{i,t+1}}{q_{i,t}}.
$$

These are gross payoff-to-price ratios, not log returns or price changes of a long-lived security. Let $q_t$ be the two-vector of risky prices and $\theta=(\theta_1,\theta_2)'$ their fixed aggregate unit supply. Let $R_{0,t+1}$ be the risk-free gross return. The source effectively normalizes the safe asset's purchase price to one in its demand and pricing formulas.

A trader with coefficient $a$ has constant absolute risk aversion and maximizes expected utility of next-period wealth. With conditionally Gaussian beliefs, the choice reduces to a mean–variance problem in terminal currency wealth. The risky position $y_t(a)$ is measured in asset units, so payoff covariance—not return covariance—enters its terminal-wealth variance.

Risk aversion ranges over $[\ell,1]$, with $\ell>0$. The equilibrium equations integrate with unit density over that interval, giving total trader mass $1-\ell$. Although the prose describes a uniform distribution, replacing this measure by a probability density $1/(1-\ell)$ without rescaling supply would change the equilibrium formulas. Retaining the source's measure convention is essential for reproducing its harmonic-mean coefficient and feasibility threshold.

CARA preferences make risky demand independent of the individual wealth level. The authors exploit this to avoid tracking the cross-sectional wealth distribution, assigning each period's new risky supply to outsiders. This strips out wealth-distribution feedback, institutional default, and many balance-sheet effects. It is a deliberate simplification of the feedback being studied.

## Backward-looking beliefs and information timing

Traders share a Gaussian forecast for next-period gross returns, with mean $\mu_t$ and covariance $\Sigma_t$. They update both by exponentially weighted historical observations with decay $\rho=.97$. A consistent one-step convention is

$$
\mu_{t+1}=\rho\mu_t+(1-\rho)R_{t+1},
$$

$$
\Sigma_{t+1}=\rho\Sigma_t+(1-\rho)
(R_{t+1}-\mu_t)(R_{t+1}-\mu_t)'.
$$

This notation follows the sequence in the source's diagram: date-$t$ beliefs determine demand and prices, $d_{t+1}$ is then realized, $R_{t+1}$ is observed, and beliefs advance. Printed equations (1)–(2) instead use $R_t$ while their preceding mapping uses $R_{t+1}$. A replication must adopt a consistent lag convention; the equations above are a transparent indexing repair, not a claim that the original code used a particular convention.

The mean in the covariance innovation is the old forecast. Updating the mean first and then using the revised mean changes the variance recursion. For positive-semidefinite initial covariance, the displayed convex combination preserves positive semidefiniteness, though inversion additionally requires nonsingularity or an explicitly chosen regularization rule.

The decay implies a half-life of approximately 22.76 trading days. The independent-observation effective weight count $(1+\rho)/(1-\rho)$ is approximately 65.67, a diagnostic of the weighting scheme rather than an effective sample-size theorem for endogenous dependent returns. Neither quantity replaces the paper's distinct 500-period simulation burn-in.

For portfolio demand the paper introduces payoff beliefs $(m_t,S_t)$. If these are contemporaneous transformations of the stated return beliefs, the identities would be $m_t=Q_t\mu_t$ and $S_t=Q_t\Sigma_tQ_t$, where $Q_t=\operatorname{diag}(q_t)$. These relations are circular with price determination. The source says payoff beliefs follow from the updating process but does not give a fully operational conversion or specify whether a lagged price normalization is used. Its explicit equilibrium characterization is conditional on supplied payoff beliefs. That missing bridge prevents treating the complete dynamic simulation as fully specified by the printed recursions alone.

## The VaR constraint is a centered loss bound

The regulated trader must satisfy

$$
\Pr_t\{E_t[W_{t+1}]-W_{t+1}\ge\operatorname{VaR}\}\le\bar p.
$$

Loss is measured relative to expected terminal wealth, rather than current wealth or zero profit. Under the trader's normal belief, for $0<\bar p<1/2$ and positive VaR allowance this is equivalent to

$$
\operatorname{Var}_t(W_{t+1})\le
\left[\frac{\operatorname{VaR}}{\Phi^{-1}(1-\bar p)}\right]^2
=: \bar v.
$$

Thus the modeled rule is a constant cap on terminal-wealth variance, $y_t'S_ty_t\le\bar v$. The cap has currency-squared units. The paper's numerical values 100 and 1000 are variance allowances, not confidence percentages, VaR amounts, or equity leverage ratios. Without a specified tail probability they cannot be translated into unique VaR amounts.

Normality is a belief assumption used to convert a probability limit into a variance constraint. The latent payoff process can be unconditionally nonnormal, and traders' forecasts need not equal its conditional law. Consequently a portfolio meeting the modeled forecast constraint need not meet the same exceedance probability under the actual data-generating process.

## Individual demand and effective risk aversion

Let $e_t=m_t-R_{0,t+1}q_t$ be the vector of expected payoff in excess of funding its purchase. The constrained CARA–normal demand can be written

$$
y_t(a)=\frac1{a+\phi_t(a)}S_t^{-1}e_t,
\qquad \phi_t(a)\ge0.
$$

The source defines $\phi$ by normalizing the Lagrange multiplier with expected marginal utility. Equivalently, it is the extra coefficient on variance in the certainty-equivalent first-order condition. Complementarity requires $\phi_t(a)=0$ when the variance cap is slack and $y_t'S_ty_t=\bar v$ when the multiplier is positive.

All traders hold the same risky direction $S_t^{-1}e_t$, scaled by effective risk tolerance. Aggregating and clearing the risky supply gives

$$
q_t=\frac{m_t-\Psi_tS_t\theta}{R_{0,t+1}},
\qquad
\Psi_t=\left[\int_\ell^1\frac{da}{a+\phi_t(a)}\right]^{-1}.
$$

Without regulation, $\Psi_0=1/\log(1/\ell)$. Regulation weakly raises this coefficient for fixed payoff beliefs. For an asset with $(S_t\theta)_i>0$, its price then falls; for a negative aggregate-risk exposure the sign reverses. The positive-beta qualification is needed. Nor must regulated prices be lower at every date in the dynamic comparison, because the two economies develop different beliefs.

The coefficient is proportional to the harmonic mean of effective risk aversion, not its arithmetic mean. With mass $1-\ell$, the literal harmonic mean is $(1-\ell)\Psi_t$. This normalization is also why the no-equilibrium boundary contains $1-\ell$.

## Solving the equilibrium coefficient and repairing the printed branch

Define $A_t=\theta'S_t\theta$ and $\kappa_t=\sqrt{A_t/\bar v}$. Since $e_t=\Psi_tS_t\theta$, demand is $y_t(a)=\Psi_t\theta/[a+\phi_t(a)]$. Enforcing the individual cap shows

$$
a+\phi_t(a)=\max\{a,z_t\},\qquad z_t=\Psi_t\kappa_t.
$$

The cutoff $z_t$ is the effective-risk-aversion floor imposed by the common risk limit. If $z_t\le\ell$, nobody is constrained, which is equivalent to $\kappa_t\le\ell\log(1/\ell)$.

In the partially binding region $\ell<z_t<1$, market clearing requires

$$
1=\Psi_t\left[\frac{z_t-\ell}{z_t}-\log z_t\right],
\qquad
\kappa_t+\ell=z_t(1-\log z_t).
$$

This scalar equation is increasing in $z_t\in[\ell,1]$ because its derivative is $-\log z_t\ge0$. It is easy to solve by bracketing and avoids reliance on a special-function convention.

Let $W_{-1}$ denote the nonprincipal real Lambert branch, with values at most $-1$. The consistent closed form derived from that clearing equation is

$$
\Psi_t=
\begin{cases}
[\log(1/\ell)]^{-1},&0\le\kappa_t\le\ell\log(1/\ell),\\
-\dfrac{\kappa_t+\ell}{\kappa_t W_{-1}[-(\kappa_t+\ell)/e]},
&\ell\log(1/\ell)<\kappa_t<1-\ell.
\end{cases}
$$

The printed binding expression on PDF page 11 has numerator $\kappa_t-\ell$ and lacks the leading minus sign. On its stated binding domain that formula is negative, contradicting both the definition of $\Psi_t$ and the demand aggregation. The equation above is a derived correction, not an unreported reproduction of the authors' code. At the lower threshold it joins the unregulated branch continuously. As $\kappa_t$ approaches $1-\ell$ from below, the displayed limiting solution has $z_t\to1$ and $\Psi_t\to1/(1-\ell)$.

For fixed $S_t$, feasibility fails when $\kappa_t>1-\ell$: even if every trader uses the full variance allowance, their aggregate risk-bearing capacity is insufficient for the supplied payoff exposure. The knife-edge equality deserves separate care because all traders can be constrained and aggregate demand need not identify a unique excess-return scale. The source gives its limiting branch value at equality; routine numerical work should distinguish that boundary from the strictly interior unique solution.

An independent scalar check using the printed $\ell=.0011$ gives the nonbinding threshold $.00749369$ and unregulated $\Psi_0=.14679017$. At $\kappa=.1$, the corrected binding coefficient is $.20734367$, while the printed branch is approximately $-.20283174$. Substituting the corrected solution into the aggregation equation yields numerical residual below $10^{-15}$. These are algebra checks, not reruns of the missing simulation code.

## Simulation inputs and what is missing

The reported experiment has two risky securities. Its parameter table gives

$$
\theta=(1.9,.5)',\qquad R_0=1.00013,\qquad \rho=.97,\qquad \ell=.0011,
$$

$$
\widehat m=(1.5,1.2)',\qquad
\widehat S=\begin{pmatrix}.6&.25\\.25&.4\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Here $\widehat m$ and $\widehat S$ are unconditional payoff moments of the latent data-generating process, not automatically the traders' current estimates $m_t,S_t$. The covariance entries are variances and covariances, not standard deviations. The latent correlation is approximately $.5103$. The gross daily safe return corresponds to a net daily return of $.013\%$ and approximately $3.303\%$ compounded over 250 days. This conversion is illustrative; the source does not establish a calendar convention for every reported parameter.

The latent payoffs follow a multivariate GARCH process, conditionally Gaussian but unconditionally nonnormal, intended to reproduce volatility clustering. The paper says the process is estimated from observed equity returns, but does not supply the complete GARCH recursion, coefficients, estimation sample, innovation construction, or executable code. The unconditional moments above cannot identify that process. Different admissible dynamics can have these same moments and produce different feedback paths.

Starting values are described as reasonable but arbitrary. The first 500 observations are discarded, and recorded date zero is the 501st observation. Across repetitions, independent random numbers are used. Within a paired comparison, the regulated and unregulated economies receive the same latent payoff realizations. Their price histories, observed returns, and resulting risk estimates differ. This common-random-number design is central: it attributes the divergence to the policy-and-belief feedback within the chosen model rather than to a different sequence of fundamentals.

A direct reconstruction therefore needs at least: the full payoff process; initial means and covariance matrices; the payoff/return moment conversion; the timing of the updating equations; the exact market-clearing procedure; and the treatment of a missing equilibrium. The paper gives enough structure to reconstruct conditional demand and identify the feedback, but not enough to reproduce all six figures numerically.

There is a further numerical inconsistency. The parameter table states $\ell=.0011$, which implies unregulated $\Psi_0=.14679017$. Figures 3 and 4 label the unregulated coefficient as $.11$. The latter is close to what $\ell=.00011$ would imply, $.10970891$, but that is only a diagnostic possibility. Neither the parameter nor the figure should be silently changed. This discrepancy prevents treating the plotted levels of $\Psi$ as a verified implementation of the printed calibration.

## Ordinary-path comparison

The typical-path experiment uses $\bar v=100$ and shows approximately 250 trading days. Figure 1 compares risky-asset prices with regulation, drawn solid, against prices without regulation, drawn dotted. Regulated prices are generally lower, with gaps that widen during stress. They are not uniformly lower: the text reports price differences ranging from zero to about 40%, and there are short episodes of the opposite ordering. Those reversals do not invalidate the fixed-belief comparative static, because each economy has its own evolving estimates.

Care is needed with the ratios. The figure axis and the body text identify the ratio as $q_1^u/q_1^r$, unregulated over regulated. The caption describes the opposite direction. A ratio above one supports the lower-regulated-price interpretation. A ratio of $1.5$, for example, means the regulated price is one-third below the unregulated price, not 50% below it. This example explains denominators; it is not an additional digitized result from the chart.

Figure 2 plots estimated return variance, particularly $\sigma_{11}$, rather than standard-deviation volatility. Its ratio is likewise unregulated over regulated, generally below one, although the caption again reverses the verbal direction. The text and horizontal axis describe 250 observations, while the caption refers to 400 realizations. These are source inconsistencies, not grounds for inventing an exact sample size. The substantive result is greater forecast risk under the binding common limit in the illustrated path. A statement about forecast variance should not be relabeled as a measured increase in realized return variance without an additional calculation.

The mechanism is recursive. A price decline raises measured risk; the variance limit raises effective risk aversion for previously aggressive traders; their reduced risky demand lowers the clearing price further. Meanwhile the observed return path feeds into the next estimate. The exercise illustrates an endogenous amplification channel; it does not estimate a policy treatment effect in real markets.

## Response to an adverse payoff shock

The shock experiment uses the looser allowance $\bar v=1000$. At a steady state, the first asset receives a one-day adverse payoff shock of magnitude $-5.3$. The paper describes this as an expected minimum over 2,500 observations, interpreted as ten years at 250 trading days per year. This is a payoff shock, not a return of $-5.3\%$, and its description does not by itself specify a quantile or a tail probability. The exact expected-minimum calculation for the GARCH process, whether the shock replaces or adds to the simulated payoff, and the steady-state initialization are not fully documented.

Results are averaged over $10^6$ independent repetitions. This large count can reduce simulation error conditional on a correct implementation, but the paper does not report Monte Carlo standard errors or confidence bands. It cannot remove uncertainty about the model, calibration, or omitted algorithmic inputs.

Figure 3 shows a deeper initial price fall and slower recovery under regulation. Stress continues to worsen for several days after the initiating event; the largest relative price discrepancy occurs with a delay. The accompanying discussion characterizes the extra price impact as under 20%. The middle-panel ratio is $q_1^u/q_1^r$, notwithstanding the caption's reversed wording. The final panel plots the equilibrium risk coefficient $\Psi$, showing the rise in effective aggregate risk aversion and subsequent relaxation. Its level inherits the calibration inconsistency described above.

Figure 4 uses the determinant of the forecast payoff covariance matrix, $|S_t|$, as an aggregate risk statistic. For two assets this is $S_{11}S_{22}-S_{12}^2$, with units of payoff to the fourth power. It is not a standard deviation, a single-asset variance, or the determinant of the return covariance $\Sigma_t$ unless a separate conversion is made. The determinant is higher in the regulated economy following the shock, and the plotted ratio $|S_t^u|/|S_t^r|$ lies below one. A determinant depends jointly on both marginal variances and their correlation; it is a measure of covariance-ellipse scale, not a complete description of every exposure's risk.

## Tightening the limit and the equilibrium boundary

Figure 5 varies the common risk allowance. Its horizontal positions are logarithmically spaced, with labels such as 10, 50, 100, 500, 1,000, 5,000, and 10,000. The solid price comparison becomes closer to one as the allowance is relaxed; the covariance-determinant ratio does the same. Thus tighter constraints are associated with larger price discounts and stronger risk amplification in the reported exercise.

The paper reports loss of equilibrium at a limit near 12. There is an unresolved labeling problem: the body discusses $\bar v$ approaching 12, while the caption says $\log(\bar v)<12$. The displayed ticks look like raw allowance values on a logarithmic axis. It would be unjustified to convert the threshold into $e^{12}$ or to declare either interpretation numerically verified without code.

The conditional feasibility calculation provides a separate check, not a resolution of that plotting problem. For the printed latent covariance, $\theta'\widehat S\theta=2.741$, so the fixed-covariance lower allowance is $2.741/(1-.0011)^2\approx2.7470$. This does not contradict a higher threshold in a dynamic simulation: traders' endogenous forecast covariance can exceed the latent unconditional matrix. Substituting $\widehat S$ for the unknown limiting $S_t$ would be an additional assumption.

Figure 6 plots effective individual risk aversion over original risk aversion and constraint tightness. Its flat section for the least risk-averse traders and its kink toward the unconstrained diagonal follow directly from $a+\phi(a)=\max\{a,z\}$. The binding limit causes otherwise different aggressive traders to behave alike at the margin. This loss of diversity in demand is one way a common rule can intensify the collective response.

## A reproducible implementation plan and interpretation limits

An implementation should first test the static layer with a supplied positive-definite $S$, payoff mean $m$, supply $\theta$, and allowance. Compute $\kappa$, check the slack and feasibility thresholds, solve $z(1-\log z)=\kappa+\ell$ by bracketing in the binding interior, recover $\Psi=z/\kappa$, and obtain prices and individual demands. Verify market clearing, complementary slackness, and the variance cap. At $\kappa=0$ use the slack solution directly. Distinguish infeasibility from a failed numerical solver.

Only after that should the dynamic layer generate a common payoff path, update each economy's beliefs with the agreed timing, solve its equilibrium, and record price, return, estimated variance, covariance determinant, and the coefficient $\Psi$. Test the no-regulation limit, positive definiteness, the burn-in convention, and whether any generated price becomes nonpositive. Report explicitly how the gross return is defined in those exceptional states. Paired simulation summaries should retain the two histories separately and declare whether an average of ratios or a ratio of averages is being used.

The source does not model an order book, bid–ask spreads, transaction costs, leverage margins, or default resolution. Its discussion of market liquidity is an equilibrium-demand interpretation, not a calibrated microstructure mechanism. The benefit of regulation in reducing institutional failure or agency problems is also outside the experiment. Consequently the finding does not establish that risk limits are socially harmful overall. It establishes that a limit calibrated as if prices and risk distributions were exogenous can omit a destabilizing feedback when many institutions respond together.

The strongest reusable result is the combination of a constrained demand system with endogenous covariance learning. The policy lesson is to evaluate the collective response to a rule, including equilibrium existence and the evolution of beliefs. Exact magnitudes from the displayed figures remain conditional on an incompletely documented simulation and on the source's numerical and labeling inconsistencies.
