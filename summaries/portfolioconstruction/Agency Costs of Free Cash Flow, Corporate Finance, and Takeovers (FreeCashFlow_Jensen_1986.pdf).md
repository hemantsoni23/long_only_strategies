# Agency Costs of Free Cash Flow, Corporate Finance, and Takeovers

Michael C. Jensen, *American Economic Review* 76(2), May 1986, Papers and Proceedings, pp. 323-329. [Source PDF](../../Finance/FreeCashFlow_Jensen_1986.pdf). The supplied file contains one JSTOR cover page and seven article pages; all eight were read and visually checked. PDF page 2 is printed p. 323. The article has no mathematical appendix, regression table, or original downloadable dataset. Its contribution is a theory of managerial incentives, supported by previously reported evidence and contemporary examples.

## Why this matters

A profitable firm can destroy value by reinvesting cash in projects whose expected returns fall below their cost of capital. Jensen makes the conflict over that cash central to financing and takeover decisions. Managers can benefit from controlling a larger organization even when shareholders would prefer a smaller firm and a payout. Debt can help by turning a reversible payout intention into a contractual obligation with consequences for control.

The hypothesis is conditional on investment opportunities and on what happens to the borrowing proceeds. Debt issued to repurchase equity removes discretionary resources and commits future cash flows. Debt issued for cash that management retains can add discretionary resources. Treating both transactions as the same increase in leverage misses the mechanism.

For a quantitative investor, the useful unit of analysis is consequently a financing or acquisition decision interacted with cash generation, investment opportunities, and governance. The paper does not establish a generic high-leverage premium, a free-cash-flow stock factor, or a calibrated optimal debt ratio. It explains why apparently healthy operating performance can coexist with a serious control problem, and why a takeover can either spend surplus cash badly or force its return to investors.

## Economic definition and the agency problem

Free cash flow here means cash remaining after funding every available positive-net-present-value project at the relevant cost of capital. It is an economic counterfactual, not a directly reported accounting field. A company may report large operating cash flow while needing all of it for attractive investment. Another may report large capital expenditures that are themselves the waste the theory seeks to explain.

A schematic accounting representation is $F=C-I^+$, where $C$ is available cash and $I^+$ is the cost of the economically desirable investment program. This notation is supplied for clarity; Jensen does not estimate $I^+$ or provide a project-selection model. An empirical substitute such as operating cash flow minus observed capital expenditures can differ sharply from $F$ when management overinvests. Using that substitute without qualification can conceal the very agency problem under study.

Shareholders prefer projects with positive NPV and distribution of residual resources. Managers may value a larger resource base because it brings power, compensation tied to growth, and more promotion opportunities inside the organization. Internally financed projects also avoid the scrutiny that comes with approaching external capital markets. The paper cites managerial compensation and organizational studies as motivation; it does not estimate the strength of these preferences.

Product-market competition can discipline costs, but the force is weaker in activities earning rents or quasi-rents and in some new activities. A mature operation with protected cash generation can therefore support inefficient investment or organizational slack for a long time. Internal controls and the market for corporate control matter most when those other disciplinary forces are weak.

The distinction between average and marginal profitability is essential. Strong earnings from existing assets do not imply attractive returns on additional assets. This is the basis of the oil-industry example: large cash flows and a need to contract can occur simultaneously. A model or screen that equates high past profitability with abundant growth opportunities would reverse the argument.

## Debt as a commitment and a control mechanism

Section I distinguishes returning current cash from committing future cash. A dividend or cash repurchase reduces today's funds, but management can retain tomorrow's receipts. Even a stated permanent dividend increase remains reversible, although a subsequent cut may be costly in the stock market. Debt creates enforceable payment obligations and gives creditors remedial rights if management fails to honor them.

The central transaction is debt creation without retention of the issue proceeds. In a debt-for-equity exchange, investors receive a contractual claim in place of a residual claim. In a debt-financed repurchase, cash leaves the firm while future debt service limits discretionary spending. The additional rights on nonpayment can help overcome internal resistance to cost cuts, abandonment of projects, and asset disposals.

One can summarize the mechanism with a stylized residual budget,

$$
F_t^{\mathrm{discretionary}}=C_t-I_t^+-S_t,
$$

where $S_t$ is scheduled debt service. This is an explanatory reconstruction, not a structural equation estimated in the paper. It suppresses refinancing, cash reserves, uncertainty, covenants, and the possibility that insufficient funds force cancellation of worthwhile investment. Those suppressed features are exactly why maximizing debt is not the proposed policy.

Jensen retains the familiar costs of leverage, including conflicts involving creditors and bankruptcy costs. His addition is a control benefit. A schematic value decomposition consistent with the argument is

$$
V(D)=V(0)+B_{\mathrm{control}}(D)+B_{\mathrm{tax}}(D)-C_{\mathrm{debt}}(D).
$$

At an interior differentiable optimum, marginal benefits and marginal costs balance. The paper supplies no functional forms, parameter estimates, distribution of cash flow, or solution for $D$. The equation is a way to organize the tradeoff, not a formula from which to calculate a target leverage ratio.

The benefit should be greatest for cash-rich firms with few attractive opportunities, particularly those that ought to shrink. It should be weaker for rapidly growing firms that already need frequent outside financing: the financing process itself gives investors and intermediaries opportunities to review projects and management. Raising debt for a growth firm can instead add financing risk or crowd out useful investment.

Taxes provide an additional explanation for some payouts and exchanges. The paper describes the tax treatment prevailing in its historical setting; those statements should not be used as current tax advice. The control hypothesis is intended to explain patterns that a tax-only story leaves incomplete, including some preferred-for-common exchanges.

## Financing announcements: what the cited evidence says

Section II draws on more than a dozen earlier studies summarized by Jensen and Smith and by Smith. It does not pool the underlying observations into a new estimation. The article reports these two-day stock-price effects:

| Transaction category | Reported result | Interpretation offered |
|---|---|---|
| Debt exchanged for common equity | Gain of 21.9% | Stronger commitment to future payout |
| Debt or income bonds exchanged for preferred | Gain of 2.2% | Smaller increase in commitment strength |
| Common equity exchanged for debt | Loss of 9.9% | Relaxed payout commitment |
| Convertible preferred called with forced conversion to common | Loss of 0.4% | Shift toward a residual payout claim |

These are endpoints of ranges across transaction studies, not coefficients from a common regression or a confidence interval. The article says most leverage-increasing exchanges or repurchases produce significant gains and most leverage-reducing transactions produce significant declines. It does not supply sample sizes, standard errors, every event date, the exact two-day alignment, or the return model used in each underlying study.

The exceptions are informative. Targeted repurchases can reduce the probability of a takeover and are associated with price declines. New sales of debt or preferred stock also bring cash under managerial control and need not have the positive effect of a debt-for-equity exchange. Some of these declines are statistically insignificant in the cited evidence. Therefore the paper's own taxonomy rejects a simple rule that any increase in measured leverage should raise equity value.

Announcement returns can reflect information about prospects, taxes, control changes, transfers across claimant groups, or revised takeover probabilities. Jensen argues that free-cash-flow incentives help organize the pattern. He does not isolate their causal share or show that the whole equity-price response equals an increase in total enterprise value.

## Leveraged buyouts, ownership incentives, and strip financing

Section III describes going private and leveraged buyouts as an organizational response to the control problem. Favorable candidates have stable business histories, substantial cash generation, and limited growth opportunities. In 1984, going-private transactions totaled \$10.8 billion and accounted for 27% of public acquisitions by number. The 27% is not a share of acquisition dollar value. Cited studies report average premiums above 50%, but the article does not provide a premium distribution, common measurement date, or matched control group.

Debt-to-equity ratios of 10:1 are described as not uncommon. That ratio corresponds arithmetically to debt of roughly 90.9% of debt plus equity, but it is not a recommended target, an estimated optimum, or necessarily a market-value capital ratio measured consistently across deals. The paper is documenting the intensity of commitment in these transactions.

Strip financing addresses conflicts within the capital structure. Jensen's deliberately simplified example compares an all-equity firm with an otherwise identical firm financed by several debt and preferred layers plus equity. Suppose investor $i$ must own the same fraction $x_i$ of every claim, and the holdings remain inseparable. If class $k$ receives payoff $X_k$ and total distributable cash is $X$, then

$$
\sum_k x_iX_k=x_i\sum_kX_k=x_iX.
$$

Reallocating a fixed total between senior and junior claims cannot change that investor's aggregate payoff. The financing structure can nevertheless change control because missed coupons or other specified payments activate rights to intervene, replace management, or reorganize. A security can therefore matter through its contractual rights even when proportional ownership neutralizes its distributional effect on final cash receipts.

The example's assumptions are demanding. Perfect proportionality, identical objectives, no outside conflicting claimants, and enforceable joint holdings make consensual reorganization easier. They do not establish that actual highly levered firms can always avoid bankruptcy. The paper itself says strict proportional ownership is undesirable or infeasible in practice, mentioning historical tax and bank-equity restrictions. Riskless senior debt need not participate in the strip, while managers and deal sponsors may appropriately hold disproportionately more equity.

The actual practice described concerns mezzanine securities subordinate to senior debt and above common equity. Top managers frequently receive 15-20% of equity. Venture investors and their funds retain the major equity stake, control boards, and monitor management. Their junior exposure creates a strong interest in reducing waste and generating debt-service cash. Equity incentives, concentrated monitoring, and contractual leverage operate together; the paper does not statistically separate them.

Jensen reports fewer than a handful of bankruptcies among these ventures to that date, with more private reorganizations. He expressly says evaluation requires more time and another recession. The observation supplies neither a survival rate with a defined denominator nor evidence that high leverage is safe through a full cycle. It is a contemporary assessment from 1986.

## Oil: high cash generation and a declining optimal asset base

Sections IV-V use oil to show why profitable firms may need to shrink. The roughly tenfold oil-price increase from 1973 through the late 1970s initially supported expansion and higher expected future prices. Later, lower consumption and price expectations, higher real interest rates, and increased exploration and development costs reduced the attractiveness of additional capacity and reserves. Existing assets could still earn substantial profits while marginal projects failed to cover capital costs.

The ten largest oil companies generated \$48.5 billion of cash flow in 1984, 28% of the cash flows of the top 200 firms in the cited *Dun's Business Month* survey. This is a cash-flow statistic, not a direct measurement that every dollar was free after all positive-NPV opportunities. Jensen's free-cash-flow classification combines cash generation with evidence and judgment about investment opportunities.

He points to diversification into retailing, electrical equipment, office equipment, and mining: Mobil/Marcor, Exxon/Reliance Electric and Vydec, Sohio/Kennecott, Arco/Anaconda, and Amoco/Cyprus. Some losses reflected adverse industry conditions, not solely managerial incompetence. The article explicitly mentions both bad luck and lack of expertise outside oil. It offers cases illustrating the hypothesis rather than an exhaustive acquisition sample.

Two cited investment studies support the overinvestment interpretation. McConnell and Muscarella's 1975-1981 oil-company announcements associate increases in exploration and development spending with share-price decreases, with the opposite response to decreases. That sign contrasts with their industrial-company investment findings and the cited SEC evidence on research and development. The article supplies neither the oil-event count nor a new regression controlling for contemporaneous oil news.

Picchi's study of 30 large oil firms reports average pretax returns below 10% on exploration and development outlays in 1982-1984. Estimated present values of future discovery, extension, and enhanced-recovery cash flows ranged from below \$0.60 to \$0.90 per dollar invested. Such a value-to-cost ratio below one is consistent with negative NPV, conditional on the cash-flow forecasts and discount assumptions. Those inputs, uncertainty bounds, and treatment of unsuccessful exploration are not reproduced here. The 10% accounting or investment-return description and the present-value ratios should not be combined into an invented common return estimate.

The takeover and restructuring examples have different scopes:

| Example | Magnitude reported | What the article attributes to it |
|---|---:|---|
| Gulf/Chevron, Getty/Texaco, DuPont/Conoco | More than \$17 billion combined shareholder gains | Merger-related efficiency and restructuring gains |
| Jacobs's 98 firms with substantial oil reserves, December 1984 | About \$200 billion potential gains | Estimated opportunity from eliminating inefficiencies, not realized profits |
| Phillips, Unocal, and Arco restructurings | 20-35% of market value; \$6.6 billion combined | Takeover-threat or voluntary restructuring effects |
| Repurchases in those restructurings | 25-53% of equity; more than \$4 billion each | Large payouts alongside asset sales and spending cuts |
| Diamond-Shamrock | Negative 2% announcement-day return | Dividend reduction and renewed spending despite a smaller repurchase |

The Diamond-Shamrock package cut dividends by 43%, repurchased 6% of shares for \$200 million, sold 12% of a newly created master limited partnership, and increased exploration spending by \$100 million per year. Its mixed contents are why a share repurchase alone is not the correct explanatory variable. The article interprets the entire allocation of resources and commitments.

For Phillips and Unocal, takeover pressure rather than completion of an acquisition is part of the mechanism; Arco's restructuring is described as voluntary. Changes to organizational policy can therefore create value without a completed takeover. The cited numbers do not isolate a causal effect of hostility, debt, or asset sales separately. Likewise, the \$200 billion figure is a valuation estimate of remaining potential improvement, not a return available to a specified trade.

## Takeovers can spend surplus or discipline it

The theory treats acquisitions as both a manifestation of agency problems and a possible remedy. A cash-rich bidder with unused borrowing capacity can buy businesses with poor expected returns instead of distributing resources. A target with weak internal controls can be acquired by an owner willing to stop waste, change management, or dismantle an inefficient organization. It is therefore incorrect to summarize the paper as saying takeovers necessarily improve efficiency.

The conditional predictions in Section VI are concrete. Diversifying acquisitions by firms with abundant cash and weak opportunities should have lower total gains than transactions with a clear economic restructuring rationale. In an industry that needs to contract, mergers inside the industry may facilitate exit, whereas buying unrelated businesses can preserve managerial scale at investors' expense. Tobacco, forest products, food, and broadcasting are proposed analogues to oil, each with its own reasons for cash generation or limited investment. The \$6.3 billion Beatrice LBO is cited as the largest to that date, not as a tested performance observation.

Jensen also distinguishes a bad use of a bidder's money from a social comparison with an even worse alternative. A cash acquisition transfers funds to target shareholders who can reinvest elsewhere. Even an acquisition that generates operating inefficiencies might waste less than the bidder's abandoned internal projects would have wasted. This is a counterfactual argument, not a measured welfare result. A bidder's loss does not itself establish social benefit, and the article supplies no complete accounting of employees, creditors, taxes, or alternative investment uses.

High leverage during a hostile takeover or defense can be temporary. Debt service creates pressure to cancel expansion, sell divisions worth more outside the firm, and use proceeds to reduce debt toward a sustainable level. A snapshot of peak leverage can consequently misrepresent the intended long-run financing policy. The source describes the logic but does not provide a dynamic optimization problem, maturity schedule, debt-paydown forecast, or probability of successful execution.

The theory also predicts more than one pre-acquisition performance pattern. Successful bidders can accumulate the surplus that finances poor acquisitions. Targets can be poorly managed underperformers, but they can also have excellent operating records and large retained cash flows. The latter are attractive because they withhold distributions, not because their core operations are failing. This makes previous stock or operating performance an insufficient stand-alone measure of governance quality.

Cash- and debt-financed takeovers are predicted to generate larger benefits than stock-financed acquisitions under this mechanism. The article immediately notes that stock acquisitions may involve different firms with more growth opportunities and less free cash flow. Financing choice is endogenous. An unconditional comparison by payment type does not identify the effect of debt discipline, and the paper leaves that distinction for later investigation.

## What can be reconstructed, and what remains untested

The reproducible content of this seven-page article is the mechanism, the transaction taxonomy, the conditional predictions, and the quoted magnitudes. It does not contain a self-contained empirical design from which to recreate the underlying event-study estimates. Doing that would require the cited studies' samples, exclusions, event timestamps, benchmark-return models, estimation windows, handling of confounding announcements, and tests of significance. The two-day return reports should remain two-day reports until those conventions are recovered.

A new test would also need an observable, predetermined proxy for the unobserved opportunity set. Cash flow alone cannot distinguish surplus from financing for valuable growth. Interacting cash generation with investment opportunities and the use of financing proceeds would be closer to the hypothesis, but that is a proposed research design, not an experiment performed in this article. Because governance changes, financing decisions, and takeover threats are selected jointly, causal interpretation would require an additional identification strategy.

For a credit analyst, the shareholder perspective needs further qualification. Debt can improve operating discipline and simultaneously expose an existing lender to higher default risk, weaker collateral protection, or transfers to equity. Creditor returns require contract seniority, covenants, recovery, maturity, and payment information absent from this essay. The paper's shareholder gains cannot be read as gains to every claim in the capital structure.

For an investment strategy, announcement returns usually occur when the information reaches the market. There is no demonstrated pre-announcement signal, executable entry rule, holding period beyond the cited event windows, risk adjustment common to all examples, or treatment of fees and financing costs. The theory can guide which corporate actions deserve analysis; it does not by itself establish a tradable abnormal-return strategy.

The article ends by emphasizing the breadth of predictions still awaiting tests. Its lasting contribution is to add the cost of managerial discretion over surplus cash to the financing tradeoff. An application preserves that contribution by examining what cash is truly surplus, which future payments are credibly committed, who receives control rights after failure, and which valuable investments might be lost when the commitment becomes too tight.
