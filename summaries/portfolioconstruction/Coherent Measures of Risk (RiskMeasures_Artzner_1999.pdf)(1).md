# Coherent Measures of Risk

**Philippe Artzner, Freddy Delbaen, Jean-Marc Eber, and David Heath.** *Mathematical Finance* 9(3), July 1999, 203–228. **Source:** [RiskMeasures_Artzner_1999.pdf](../../Finance/RiskMeasures_Artzner_1999.pdf), all 26 pages inspected, including proofs, margin examples, and references. Printed pages are cited below. The article is a theoretical framework with exact examples; its historical margin rules are descriptions of the systems studied at the time, not statements of current regulation.

## Why this matters

A firm cannot safely decentralize capital limits if the sum of desk-level requirements fails to cover the requirement of the aggregate book. Nor should a margin algorithm charge different risk for two descriptions of the same terminal payoff. This paper turns such operational requirements into mathematical properties and shows that they lead to a common representation: capital is the worst expected discounted loss across a specified set of probability scenarios.

The paper's contribution has three connected parts. It defines capital through an acceptance set and a reference investment, characterizes the coherent measures generated by those decisions, and proves dual representations through scenarios and through supporting standard risks. Those representations explain why superficially different margin systems can be mathematically related, why one system can overcharge because it admits impossible scenarios, and why VaR can conceal concentrations while penalizing diversification.

The framework does not choose a unique risk measure or estimate market probabilities. Its purpose is to separate logical consistency from the economic selection of acceptable states and scenarios. It accommodates incomplete markets and nonmarket risks, and initially requires no agreed physical probability distribution.

## The primitive object: future net worth and a reference investment

Fix dates $0$ and $T$ and a finite set of possible terminal states $\Omega$, with $n$ states. A position is a vector $X\in\mathbb R^n$ of terminal net worths. Low or negative $X$ is adverse. The source deliberately works with future values rather than only changes from an initial market price: an OTC contract or insurance liability may have no universally accepted initial valuation.

A reference instrument costs one unit at date zero and pays $r(\omega)>0$ in state $\omega$ at date $T$. It may be a default-free bond with deterministic payoff, but the setup permits a strictly positive random reference payoff. Thus $X/r$ is a statewise ratio, and discounting must remain inside expectations when $r$ is random.

Given an acceptance set $\mathcal A$, capital is

$$
\rho_{\mathcal A,r}(X)=\inf\{m\in\mathbb R:X+mr\in\mathcal A\}.
$$

A positive value means capital must be added and invested in the reference instrument. A negative value means that capital can be withdrawn while the position remains acceptable. The result is in date-zero units of the reference currency, not simply a terminal loss quantile.

For multiple currencies, the terminal net worth in the reporting currency is $\sum_i e_iA_i(T)$, where $e_i$ is the terminal exchange rate and $A_i(T)$ the terminal value of the currency-$i$ position. Initial exchange rates are normalized to one. Acceptance can be made invariant under currency conversion, but numerical capital need not be invariant when supervisors use different reference investments. Changing numeraire and changing the asset into which capital is invested are distinct operations.

The horizon is supplied externally. It might be a liquidation interval, a rehedging interval, or the period of insurance coverage. Remark 2.1 permits interpreting $X$ as the result of a strategy, but the paper proves its main statements in a one-period finite-state setting. It does not develop a dynamically time-consistent capital process.

## Acceptance geometry and the four coherence conditions

The acceptance-set axioms require all nonnegative net worths to be acceptable, no everywhere-strictly-negative net worth to be acceptable, and the set to be convex and a positive cone. A stronger exclusion, $\mathcal A\cap\{X\le0\}=\{0\}$, rules out acceptance of any nonzero pure loss.

The associated numerical axioms are

$$
\rho(X+ar)=\rho(X)-a,
$$

$$
\rho(X+Y)\le\rho(X)+\rho(Y),
$$

$$
\rho(\lambda X)=\lambda\rho(X),\quad\lambda\ge0,
$$

$$
X\le Y\ \Longrightarrow\ \rho(Y)\le\rho(X).
$$

These are reference-asset translation, subadditivity, positive homogeneity, and monotonicity. A measure satisfying all four is coherent. Relevance, $X\le0$ and $X\ne0\Rightarrow\rho(X)>0$, is a separate condition, not a fifth component silently included in the definition.

Translation calibrates capital in units that can actually be injected. Homogeneity means that scaling a terminal random payoff scales its capital requirement. It does not claim that doubling an actual trading position doubles its liquidation proceeds: nonlinear impact should first be reflected in the mapping from holdings to $X$. Subadditivity then governs the resulting payoff variables, not an incorrectly linearized liquidation model.

Subadditivity makes the sum of two capital limits a valid upper bound for the merged book. It removes incentives to split a book solely to lower required capital. The economic merger interpretation presumes that the merged resources really can support the combined obligations; legal firewalls and nontransferable capital can change what the relevant aggregate payoff is.

Monotonicity excludes ordinary mean-plus-standard-deviation loss measures on unrestricted risks. A statewise improvement can increase dispersion enough to raise such a statistic. A negative capital number for a profitable or sufficiently safe position is not itself a violation: requiring every number to be nonnegative would conflict with cash translation.

Propositions 2.1–2.2 establish the correspondence. A suitable acceptance cone generates a coherent capital functional, and recovering acceptance from $\rho\le0$ gives its closure. Conversely, a finite-valued coherent functional is convex and continuous in this finite-dimensional setting; its nonpositive sublevel set is closed and satisfies the acceptance axioms. Starting from the closed acceptance set returns the original functional. Closure matters when the infimum defining capital approaches an unattained acceptance boundary.

## The scenario representation and its proof mechanism

Proposition 4.1 is the central representation theorem:

$$
\rho(X)=\sup_{Q\in\mathcal P}E_Q[-X/r]
$$

for a nonempty family $\mathcal P$ of probability measures on the finite state space, if and only if $\rho$ is coherent relative to $r$.

The easy direction follows immediately: each expectation is linear, cash translated, and monotone; taking a supremum preserves the necessary inequalities. The reverse direction uses the representation of a sublinear functional by supporting linear functionals. Define $E^*(Z)=\rho(-rZ)$. Monotonicity makes its supporting functionals positive, and translation plus homogeneity forces them to assign value one to the constant payoff one. On a finite state space, such functionals are probability vectors. The source acknowledges the corresponding mathematical result in Huber's work rather than claiming the convex-separation machinery itself as new.

A maximal representing set is

$$
\mathcal P_\rho=\{Q:E_Q[-Y/r]\le\rho(Y)\ \text{for every }Y\in\mathbb R^n\}.
$$

Replacing a representing set by its closed convex hull does not change the supremum. Therefore arbitrary families are not uniquely identified by the risk functional; a canonical closed convex representation is the meaningful comparison. Adding scenarios can only increase capital. A single physical or pricing probability distribution is not necessary, although either may be among the scenarios.

For finitely many scenarios $Q_j$, construct a matrix $B_{j\omega}=Q_j(\omega)/r(\omega)$. Capital is $\max_j[-\sum_\omega B_{j\omega}X(\omega)]$. For a portfolio payoff linear in holdings, this is a maximum of affine functions and can be optimized through an epigraph variable and linear inequalities, provided the holding constraints are linear. If the scenario set is a polytope rather than an explicit finite list, the inner worst expectation is itself a linear program.

Relevance has a particularly transparent characterization in Proposition 3.1: the union of the scenario supports must cover every state. If all scenarios assign zero weight to one state, a pure loss only in that state is invisible. Full support of a single scenario suffices, but is not necessary; different scenarios can collectively cover the state space.

Finite dimensionality is essential to the theorem as stated. Extending it to general spaces requires additional topological and continuity assumptions if one wants countably additive probabilities rather than more general finitely additive functionals. This note does not import the finite-state theorem unqualified into every stochastic-process setting.

## SPAN as a set of generalized scenarios

Section 3.1 studies a simplified historical SPAN calculation for futures and options with a common expiry. Fourteen scenarios combine an upward or downward volatility move with futures-price moves of zero or plus/minus one-third, two-thirds, and the full specified range. Two additional scenarios represent extreme up and down futures-price moves.

The risk is the largest scenario loss, counting 100% of each ordinary scenario and 35% of each extreme scenario. The option prices are generated by an external pricing model, typically Black's model. The scan range, volatility shifts, and model inputs are not numerically specified in the article.

The extreme weighting can be represented by a probability measure placing 0.35 on the extreme state and 0.65 on the no-move state, whose incremental loss is zero. The ordinary scenarios are point masses. This converts an apparently ad hoc weighted stress rule into a maximum of expected losses under sixteen generalized scenarios.

The distinction between a designed scenario and a Monte Carlo draw is substantive. A stress state is deliberately selected; a simulation draw is random under a prescribed distribution. Coherence does not make their probabilities physically correct, nor does the SPAN illustration prove that any choice of sixteen scenarios is adequate. It shows the structural form of the capital aggregation step.

## The SEC spread example: 30 units versus 10

Section 3.2 considers a portfolio of European calls expiring at the risk horizon:

$$
A=2C_{10}-2C_{20}-3C_{30}+4C_{40}-C_{50},
\qquad C_K(S)=(S-K)^+.
$$

Its worst terminal payoff is $-10$, attained at $S=40$; checking the strikes and exterior linear segments suffices because the payoff is piecewise linear. Yet the spread-decomposition method described in the source charges a minimum of 30.

For $S_{H,K}=C_H-C_K$, a long low-strike spread with $H<K$ needs zero margin, while the reversed spread costs $(H-K)^+$. The method solves

$$
\min_{n_{H,K}\ge0}\sum_{H\ne K}n_{H,K}(H-K)^+,
\qquad A=\sum_{H\ne K}n_{H,K}S_{H,K}.
$$

One optimal solution uses $n_{10,20}=2$, $n_{40,50}=1$, and $n_{40,30}=3$, with all other coefficients zero, giving $3(40-30)=30$. The method operates on a formal list of instruments and its allowed decomposition, rather than directly on the true terminal payoff.

The dual reveals the problem. Candidate terminal call values $\pi_K$ are constrained to decrease with strike, with slope magnitude no greater than one, but are not constrained to be convex in strike. The assignment $\pi_{10}=\pi_{20}=\pi_{30}=10$, $\pi_{40}=\pi_{50}=0$ satisfies those limited constraints and values the portfolio at $-30$. It cannot arise from any terminal stock price because $\pi_{20}+\pi_{40}=10<2\pi_{30}=20$ violates convexity.

A long butterfly $B_{20}=C_{10}-2C_{20}+C_{30}$ never has negative terminal payoff, but decomposing it into the permitted spreads charges 10. Adding butterflies to the margin-free standard risks removes that defect. Since

$$
A=2B_{20}+2B_{30}-B_{40},
$$

with ten-unit strike spacing, the corrected requirement falls to 10. A genuinely possible state already attains a loss of 10, so the lower amount is not an arbitrary relaxation. Too few supporting standard risks correspond dually to too many, including impossible, scenarios.

This example does not assert that every operational margin should equal the worst contractual payoff: liquidation costs and other obligations might matter in a richer setup. It establishes the inconsistency inside the simplified payoff-only model actually specified.

## Extension from standard risks: the constructive primal problem

Section 4.2 generalizes the spread method. Let $\mathcal Y$ be any collection of standard terminal payoff vectors, and let $\Psi(Y)$ be their assigned capital numbers. No linear-space structure is required for $\mathcal Y$. A support for $X$ is a finitely supported family $\mu_Y\ge0$ and a real cash coefficient $\gamma$ such that

$$
X\ge\sum_{Y\in\mathcal Y}\mu_YY+\gamma r.
$$

Define the support cost and its minimum by

$$
\rho_\Psi(X)=\inf_{(\mu,\gamma)\text{ supporting }X}
\left\{\sum_Y\mu_Y\Psi(Y)-\gamma\right\}.
$$

The sign of $\gamma$ is important: a positive guaranteed reference payoff reduces capital. Condition 4.1 requires every support of zero to have nonnegative cost. Otherwise a negative-cost support can be scaled indefinitely, driving the capital assigned to zero to $-\infty$.

Proposition 4.2 says this consistency condition is necessary and sufficient for a coherent extension lying below the supplied charges on the standard risks. It is the **largest** such coherent functional. To see the direction, any coherent $\rho$ with $\rho(Y)\le\Psi(Y)$ must satisfy $\rho(X)\le\sum_Y\mu_Y\Psi(Y)-\gamma$ for every support; taking the infimum gives $\rho(X)\le\rho_\Psi(X)$.

Exact agreement with the supplied charges needs the stronger Condition 4.2: every support of a standard risk $Z$ must cost at least $\Psi(Z)$. Without that condition, combining other standards can undercut the quoted charge on $Z$, so coherence produces a lower number rather than preserving an inconsistent schedule. Proposition 4.3 then gives the largest coherent extension that agrees with $\Psi$.

For finite $\mathcal Y$, the formula is a linear program with one dominance inequality per state, nonnegative coefficients for standard risks, and a free reference-asset coefficient. Its dual restricts probability scenarios by $E_Q[-Y/r]\le\Psi(Y)$ for each standard $Y$. More standard risks add inequalities to the scenario set and can reduce the conservative extension. The limiting statements require feasibility and the no-negative-cost condition, not just a solver returning a number.

## Why VaR fails the aggregation test

The source uses the upper quantile of discounted net worth:

$$
\operatorname{VaR}_\alpha(X)
=-\inf\{x:P(X\le xr)>\alpha\},\qquad 0<\alpha<1.
$$

Here $\alpha$ is the probability of the adverse tail. Strict versus weak inequalities matter for discrete distributions. VaR obeys translation, positive homogeneity, and monotonicity, but not subadditivity on general risks.

One example uses two digital options paying 1,000 on disjoint high and low stock-price events, each with probability 0.008. Let their initial prices be $u$ and $l$, with the reference return one. A trader writing two copies of the upper digital has 1% VaR $-2u$, and one writing two copies of the lower digital has $-2l$: the payout event is too rare to enter the quantile. The midpoint position writing one of each has VaR $1000-u-l$. This demonstrates nonconvexity of the VaR acceptance geometry, not merely a tiny numerical failure in a tail approximation.

A second example has independent profits with density 0.90 on $[0,1]$ and 0.05 on $[-2,0]$. Each has 10% VaR zero, while their sum has positive 10% VaR. Discrete distributions are therefore not required for non-subadditivity.

The credit example invests one million borrowed at a zero base rate into bonds paying a 2% spread and defaulting independently across issuers with probability 1%. Concentrating in one issuer gives 5% VaR $-20{,}000$, because default lies outside the quantile. Diversifying equally across 100 issuers gives a materially larger chance of enough defaults to produce negative net worth. The paper reports the relevant multiple-default probability as exceeding 0.18; its point is that the diversified risk enters the quantile while concentration remains hidden. The example specifies no recovery mechanism beyond its stylized payoff discussion, so it should not be treated as a calibrated corporate-credit experiment.

Joint Gaussianity is an important exception. With deterministic unit discounting and $\alpha<0.5$, Gaussian VaR is $-E[X]-\Phi^{-1}(\alpha)\sigma(X)$, and the triangle inequality for standard deviation proves subadditivity. Normal marginal distributions alone do not ensure that joint condition. Nor does the exception establish monotonicity of mean–standard-deviation capital on unrestricted non-Gaussian risks.

## Pricing compatibility and hidden accumulation

Section 4.3 compares the closed convex scenario set defining risk with the set $\mathcal Q_r$ of arbitrage-free pricing probabilities using $r$ as numeraire. A nonempty intersection is an economically useful compatibility condition. If a pricing measure also belongs to the risk scenario set, a payoff with strictly negative price under that measure cannot be accepted with nonpositive required capital.

If the two closed convex sets are disjoint, separation produces a payoff $Y$ for which every pricing expectation lies below every risk-scenario expectation. The capital-adjusted payoff $Z=Y+\rho(Y)r$ is acceptable because $\rho(Z)=0$, yet it has strictly negative price under every pricing measure. A trader can receive money for taking such an acceptable liability, opening a channel for accumulation undetected by the capital rule.

The result does not equate risk scenarios with risk-neutral pricing measures. It requires overlap for the stated safeguard. Relevance alone only ensures that each state is seen somewhere; it does not ensure alignment with market prices or prevent every form of concentration.

## Tail conditional expectation and worst conditional expectation

Section 5.1 defines TCE as the negative mean discounted net worth below the source's VaR threshold, and WCE as

$$
\operatorname{WCE}_\alpha(X)
=-\inf_{A:P(A)>\alpha}E[X/r\mid A].
$$

WCE is coherent because it is a supremum over a common family of conditional probability measures. The paper proves TCE $\le$ WCE. It does **not** establish unrestricted coherence of a naïve conditional tail average for all atomic distributions.

Under a uniform probability on $n$ states and distinct ordered discounted outcomes $y_1<\cdots<y_n$, put $k=\lfloor n\alpha\rfloor$. Because the probability restriction is strict, the adverse event must contain at least $k+1$ states. Consequently,

$$
\operatorname{TCE}_\alpha(X)=\operatorname{WCE}_\alpha(X)
=-\frac1{k+1}\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}y_i.
$$

This finite-uniform-space quantity is not automatically the fractional-boundary empirical ES used in later work. At integer $n\alpha$, the strict inequality still requires one more state. Retaining the paper's convention is necessary to reproduce its propositions.

Proposition 5.2 says that for each fixed position, VaR is the pointwise infimum of coherent measures dominating VaR. It does not imply that one coherent measure attains VaR for every position simultaneously. An infimum of coherent measures need not be coherent. Under the additional uniform-state and distribution-invariance assumptions, Proposition 5.4 shows that any coherent measure dominating VaR also dominates WCE. The proof symmetrizes the outcomes in an adverse set, uses convexity, and extends from distinct outcomes by continuity.

## Combining separately specified risk classes

The last application places two risk sources on $\Omega_1\times\Omega_2$. Given coherent marginal risk rules, collect all joint scenario probabilities whose marginals belong to the two respective scenario sets. Their worst expected discounted loss gives the largest coherent extension consistent with the marginal rules, subject to the common reference convention and consistency assumptions of the construction.

This is a conservative dependence construction, not an independence model. Without joint restrictions, the optimizer can select adverse couplings. In a finite implementation it is a transport-type linear program over joint probabilities, with nonnegativity and marginal constraints. Adding justified dependence information shrinks the feasible couplings and can reduce the requirement; inventing a correlation from convenience changes the model rather than completing a missing algebraic step.

## Reproduction requirements and limits

The article can be reproduced through exact finite-state and linear-program examples. The required objects are the state payoff matrix, positive reference payoff, acceptance or scenario specification, and any prescribed charges on standards. The main checks are the four identities/inequalities, primal–dual agreement in the extension program, the 30-versus-10 call example, the digital-option counterexample, and the finite-uniform WCE formula.

The source contains no market sample dates, estimation windows, fitted coefficients, statistical significance tests, or trading-cost experiment. Its empirical-looking descriptions are examples of historical institutional rules. Numerical SPAN scan inputs, full operational margin exceptions, and a method for choosing or estimating the scenario family are not supplied. No claim of current regulatory compliance follows from reproducing the mathematical examples.

Coherence supplies disciplined aggregation and acceptance accounting. It cannot recover states omitted from the model, resolve a badly specified liquidation payoff, choose an acceptable tail severity, or estimate a joint law from sparse observations. The paper's most useful implementation lesson is to specify those economic objects explicitly and then use the dual representations to expose inconsistencies that a single reported VaR number can hide.
