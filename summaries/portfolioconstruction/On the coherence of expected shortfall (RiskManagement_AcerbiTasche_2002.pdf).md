# On the coherence of expected shortfall

**Carlo Acerbi and Dirk Tasche.** *Journal of Banking & Finance* 26 (2002), 1487–1503. PII S0378-4266(02)00283-2. **Source:** [RiskManagement_AcerbiTasche_2002.pdf](../../Finance/RiskManagement_AcerbiTasche_2002.pdf), 17 pages. The full article, including Appendix A and Table 1, was inspected. Page references use the printed journal pagination. This is a mathematical paper with exact counterexamples and statistical convergence results, not an empirical forecasting study.

## Why this matters

A risk engine that averages all losses beyond a sample quantile need not compute expected shortfall. When losses have atoms, including every observation at the threshold can average substantially more than the intended tail probability. The resulting statistic can violate subadditivity even though it is called expected shortfall or conditional value-at-risk. Credit portfolios, digital derivatives, rounded valuations, and finite scenario sets make this a routine implementation issue rather than an exceptional mathematical pathology.

The authors' contribution is to identify the exact, atom-corrected quantity that remains coherent for arbitrary distributions and to reconcile several definitions then circulating under overlapping names. They connect four representations: a quantile integral, a tail average of order statistics, a convex optimization problem, and a worst conditional expectation on a sufficiently rich probability space. The definitions can therefore be implemented independently and checked against each other.

The result also distinguishes two different kinds of robustness. Expected shortfall is insensitive to the arbitrary richness of the underlying probability space when the loss distribution is held fixed. It is continuous in the chosen tail probability. Neither statement establishes universal statistical robustness to extreme observations, estimation error, or an incorrect distributional model.

## Conventions and the quantile interval

The random variable $X$ is a portfolio profit and loss, with adverse outcomes on the left. A positive risk value denotes required capital. The parameter $\alpha\in(0,1)$ is the **tail probability**: $\alpha=0.01$ refers to the worst 1%, not to a 1% coverage level. Write $F(x)=P(X\le x)$ and distinguish

$$
q_\alpha^-(X)=\inf\{x:F(x)\ge\alpha\},\qquad
q_\alpha^+(X)=\inf\{x:F(x)>\alpha\}.
$$

The lower and upper quantiles can differ when the cumulative distribution is flat at height $\alpha$. An atom and a quantile gap are related but distinct phenomena. For instance, a two-point distribution can have a quantile interval spanning the gap between its support points when $\alpha$ equals the probability of the first point. All values $s$ in the closed interval $[q_\alpha^-,q_\alpha^+]$ satisfy

$$
P(X<s)\le\alpha\le P(X\le s).
$$

The paper's VaR is $-q_\alpha^+(X)$, equivalent to the usual lower $(1-\alpha)$-quantile of the loss $-X$. A system using $-q_\alpha^-(X)$ can give a different boundary value on a discrete distribution. The choice must be recorded when reproducing Table 1; it does not change the correctly defined ES.

Most definitions require only $E[X^-]<\infty$, where $X^- =\max(-X,0)$. Thus the adverse tail must be integrable. Results invoking full integrability explicitly assume $E|X|<\infty$. Unbounded expected gains need not invalidate a finite left-tail average at a fixed interior $\alpha$, but they do prevent indiscriminate use of identities involving $E[X]$.

## The competing risk quantities

The two tail conditional expectations are

$$
\operatorname{TCE}_{\rm lower}=-E[X\mid X\le q_\alpha^-],\qquad
\operatorname{TCE}_{\rm upper}=-E[X\mid X\le q_\alpha^+].
$$

“Lower” refers to the quantile, not the numerical risk value: the lower-quantile TCE is at least the upper-quantile TCE. The strict distinction is necessary because the conditioning sets can have different probability masses.

Worst conditional expectation is

$$
\operatorname{WCE}_\alpha(X)
=-\inf_{A\in\mathcal A:\,P(A)>\alpha}E[X\mid A].
$$

The event may use all information in the ambient sigma-algebra $\mathcal A$, not just information generated by $X$. Its probability restriction is **strictly greater than** $\alpha$. On a finite atomic space, an event of exactly the desired tail mass may not exist. WCE is coherent, since it is a supremum of negative conditional expectations over a common family of events, but it need not be determined by the marginal distribution of $X$ alone.

The paper defines CVaR operationally as

$$
\operatorname{CVaR}_\alpha(X)
=\inf_{s\in\mathbb R}\left\{\frac1\alpha E[(s-X)^+]-s\right\}.
$$

This is a convex optimization definition. It should not be replaced by a conditional expectation merely because the abbreviation contains “conditional.” In the terminology adopted here, CVaR equals ES for general distributions. Other papers used CVaR for TCE; the formula, rather than the label, determines which result applies.

## The atom correction that defines expected shortfall

For any $s\in[q_\alpha^-,q_\alpha^+]$, define

$$
\operatorname{ES}_\alpha(X)
=-\frac{E[X1_{\{X<s\}}]+s\{\alpha-P(X<s)\}}{\alpha}.
$$

All outcomes strictly worse than $s$ enter fully. Only the probability mass $\alpha-P(X<s)$ needed to complete the tail is taken from outcomes equal to $s$. Equivalently,

$$
\operatorname{ES}_\alpha(X)
=-\frac{E[X1_{\{X\le s\}}]+s\{\alpha-P(X\le s)\}}{\alpha}.
$$

The second formula subtracts excess threshold mass rather than adding the required fraction. The two formulas agree algebraically because $E[X1_{\{X=s\}}]=sP(X=s)$. Their agreement is a useful implementation check in a distribution with ties.

If $P(X=s)>0$, introduce the fractional tail indicator

$$
I_X=1_{\{X<s\}}+
\frac{\alpha-P(X<s)}{P(X=s)}1_{\{X=s\}}.
$$

Then $0\le I_X\le1$, $E[I_X]=\alpha$, and $\operatorname{ES}_\alpha(X)=-E[XI_X]/\alpha$. If there is no atom, the quantile condition makes the appropriate ordinary indicator have expectation $\alpha$. The fraction is a weight, not a claim that an event inside an indivisible atom already exists in the original probability space.

Definition 2.6 first states the formula with the lower quantile; Corollary 4.3 proves invariance to the choice of $s$ anywhere in the quantile interval. The tail mean is simply $\operatorname{TM}_\alpha=-\operatorname{ES}_\alpha$. Keeping both signs visible helps avoid reversing the inequalities in the proofs.

## Quantile integration and sensitivity to the tail probability

Proposition 3.2 and equation (3.3) establish

$$
\operatorname{ES}_\alpha(X)=-\frac1\alpha\int_0^\alpha q_u^-(X)\,du.
$$

A direct proof uses a uniform random variable $U$ on $(0,1)$ and the inverse-transform representation $Z=q_U^-(X)$. This $Z$ has the same distribution as $X$. Integrating over $U\le\alpha$ includes all outcomes below the threshold and the required fraction at it, exactly reproducing the corrected formula. The proof does not require $X$ to have a density.

**Source correction:** the displayed statement of Proposition 3.2 on printed p. 1492 has an erroneous upper integration limit. Its proof and equation (3.3) immediately below use $\alpha$, which is the correct limit. Integrating over all probabilities would yield the overall mean, not a tail mean.

The integral representation proves continuity in $\alpha$ on $(0,1)$ under the stated adverse-tail integrability assumption. It also proves that ES is nonincreasing as $\alpha$ increases: adding less adverse outcomes to the tail cannot increase its average loss. At points where the quantile function is well behaved, an explanatory derivative is

$$
\frac{d}{d\alpha}\operatorname{ES}_\alpha(X)
=\frac{\operatorname{VaR}_\alpha(X)-\operatorname{ES}_\alpha(X)}{\alpha}\le0,
$$

with the appropriate quantile value at regular points. This derivative is a derived interpretation, not a separately estimated result. It shows why continuity does not supply a universal bound on changes measured in money: a steep or very large tail can still make a small probability change economically important. Behavior as $\alpha\downarrow0$ requires separate tail analysis.

## Why subadditivity holds even with atoms

Appendix A gives a short proof based on fractional indicators. Let $Z=X+Y$, and let $I_X,I_Y,I_Z$ select mass $\alpha$ in the respective lower tails. For a quantile threshold $x$ of $X$, the product

$$
(X-x)(I_Z-I_X)\ge0
$$

pointwise: below $x$, $I_X=1$, while above $x$, $I_X=0$. At the threshold the product vanishes regardless of its fractional weight. Consequently,

$$
E[X(I_Z-I_X)]\ge xE[I_Z-I_X]=0.
$$

The corresponding inequality for $Y$ gives

$$
\alpha\{\operatorname{ES}_\alpha(X)+\operatorname{ES}_\alpha(Y)
-\operatorname{ES}_\alpha(X+Y)\}
=E[X(I_Z-I_X)+Y(I_Z-I_Y)]\ge0.
$$

This establishes subadditivity without assuming independence, continuity, a particular copula, or a Gaussian model. The proof is stronger than an argument based on a plotted diversified portfolio because it applies to every admissible joint distribution.

Positive homogeneity follows by scaling ordered outcomes. Translation invariance follows because adding cash $c$ raises the tail mean by $c$, so $\operatorname{ES}_\alpha(X+c)=\operatorname{ES}_\alpha(X)-c$. Monotonicity follows from ordering: if $X\le Y$ statewise, their quantiles satisfy $q_u^-(X)\le q_u^-(Y)$, hence ES of $X$ is at least that of $Y$. Together these establish coherence, with cash invested at a zero rate in this paper's simplified convention.

## The exact counterexample and what it isolates

Example 5.4 uses three states with probabilities $p,p,1-2p$, where $0<p<1/3$. Let $X_1=-N$ only in the first state and $X_2=-N$ only in the second, with $N>0$; both are zero elsewhere. The individual default events never overlap. For $p<\alpha<2p$, the relevant risks are

| Quantity | Either individual $X_i$ | Combined $X_1+X_2$ |
|---|---:|---:|
| $-q_\alpha^-$ and $-q_\alpha^+$ | $0$ | $N$ |
| Either TCE convention | $Np$ | $N$ |
| WCE on the three-state space | $N/2$ | $N$ |
| ES | $Np/\alpha$ | $N$ |

Individual VaR misses a loss whose probability is below the threshold. For individual TCE, the quantile is zero and the conditioning event contains the entire space, so its risk becomes the unconditional expected loss $Np$. The combined lower tail lies entirely at $-N$, so its TCE is $N$. Thus the sum of individual TCEs is $2Np<N$, violating subadditivity.

WCE can include the two small atoms together but cannot split one. It therefore averages one loss and one zero in an event of probability $2p$, yielding $N/2$ for an individual position. ES can assign fractional weight to a threshold outcome and reaches $Np/\alpha$, which is larger than $N/2$ in this regime. Yet the sum $2Np/\alpha$ exceeds $N$, as coherence requires.

For a transparent numerical specialization of the paper's symbolic example, take $p=0.02$, $\alpha=0.03$, and $N=100$. The individual VaR, TCE, WCE, and ES are respectively 0, 2, 50, and approximately 66.67 monetary units; the combined value of each is 100. These are derived arithmetic values, not market estimates. They make clear that “taking an average in the tail” is insufficient unless the exact tail mass is specified.

At $\alpha=p$, the quantile conventions separate: $q_\alpha^-=-N$ but $q_\alpha^+=0$. Individual lower-quantile TCE is $N$, upper-quantile TCE is $Np$, WCE is $N/2$, and ES remains $N$. This boundary case is a strong regression test for software because common quantile interpolation defaults produce different answers. ES changes continuously across the boundary while the other quantities can jump.

The general inequalities in Corollary 5.2 are

$$
\operatorname{TCE}_{\rm upper}\le\operatorname{TCE}_{\rm lower}\le\operatorname{ES},
\qquad
\operatorname{TCE}_{\rm upper}\le\operatorname{WCE}\le\operatorname{ES}.
$$

There is no unconditional ordering between lower-quantile TCE and WCE. When $X$ has a continuous distribution, all four tail quantities coincide. With atoms, equality has special probability-mass conditions and should be verified rather than presumed.

## Convex optimization and its minimizers

For integrable $X$, define the asymmetric absolute-deviation objective

$$
H_\alpha(s)=\alpha E[(X-s)^+]+(1-\alpha)E[(X-s)^-].
$$

Proposition 4.2 shows that this is convex and coercive, with minimizer set exactly $[q_\alpha^-,q_\alpha^+]$. The left and right derivatives encode $P(X<s)-\alpha$ and $P(X\le s)-\alpha$; zero belongs to the subgradient precisely at a quantile. The minimizer need not be unique.

The identity

$$
H_\alpha(s)=\alpha E[X]+\alpha\left\{\alpha^{-1}E[(s-X)^+]-s\right\}
$$

then proves that the CVaR objective has the same minimizers and minimum equal to ES. For $n$ equally weighted scenarios $x_i$, minimize $-s+(n\alpha)^{-1}\sum_i(s-x_i)^+$. Introducing nonnegative variables $z_i\ge s-x_i$ gives a linear program. With scenario profits affine in portfolio holdings and linear feasibility constraints, one can optimize holdings and $s$ jointly. This implementation consequence follows from the representation; this paper does not benchmark solvers or provide a particular investment universe.

At a nonunique optimum, two valid solver outputs for $s$ can differ while the ES values agree exactly. Testing equality of the optimizing threshold rather than equality of the objective would incorrectly report an error. Conversely, replacing the positive-part objective by an unconditional mean over all observations below a selected threshold can change the objective itself.

## Estimation from a finite sample

For independent copies $X_1,\ldots,X_n$, let $X_{1:n}\le\cdots\le X_{n:n}$ be the order statistics and $k=\lfloor n\alpha\rfloor$. Proposition 4.1 establishes convergence of the worst-$k$ average to the population tail mean. Its negative estimates ES. This requires $k\ge1$ for the finite-sample expression to be defined. Under full integrability, convergence also holds in $L^1$.

The result is striking at a flat portion of the population CDF. A single sample order statistic near $n\alpha$ can oscillate between the endpoints of a quantile interval. Nevertheless, the tail average converges: the effect of switching endpoints is controlled by the shrinking fraction of observations whose inclusion changes. The paper invokes a strong law for linear combinations of order statistics and explains the correction for tied observations.

A closely related, exact empirical-distribution implementation is

$$
\widehat{\operatorname{ES}}_\alpha
=-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k}X_{i:n}+(n\alpha-k)X_{k+1:n}}{n\alpha}.
$$

This follows directly from the quantile-integral definition. It uses the partial boundary observation rather than replacing $n\alpha$ by its integer part. When $n\alpha$ is an integer, omit the zero-weight next term. When $n\alpha<1$, the empirical ES equals the worst observation. It remains coherent as a finite scenario functional, but one adverse observation is not evidence of accurate population tail estimation.

For unequal scenario probabilities, sort outcomes with their probabilities, accumulate all probability strictly below the cutoff, and fill the remaining mass at the cutoff proportionally. Preserve common scenario alignment across portfolios when testing subadditivity: independently resampling each portfolio changes the joint distribution and is not a test of the theorem.

The source does not supply a central limit theorem, standard errors, sample-size prescription, dependence correction, or an out-of-sample backtest. Its independent-sample convergence theorem does not automatically cover a historical time series with volatility clustering. Those extensions require assumptions beyond the paper.

## A tail-allocation interpretation for finite weighted scenarios

The fractional-indicator argument also gives a useful finite optimization interpretation. Suppose scenario $i$ has probability $p_i>0$ and profit $x_i$, with $\sum_i p_i=1$. Assign tail masses $a_i$ satisfying $0\le a_i\le p_i$ and $\sum_i a_i=\alpha$. Then the same ES is

$$
\operatorname{ES}_\alpha(X)
=\max_{0\le a_i\le p_i,\,\sum a_i=\alpha}
\left(-\frac1\alpha\sum_i a_ix_i\right).
$$

This is a derived finite form of the paper's tail selection, rather than an additional estimation theorem. Its solution fills the worst outcomes first. If an allocation assigns positive tail mass to a better outcome while leaving unused probability capacity at a worse one, transferring mass between those outcomes weakly increases the objective. Repeating this exchange proves the greedy solution and the fractional-boundary formula.

Putting $q_i=a_i/\alpha$ gives an equivalent family of probability vectors: $q_i\ge0$, $\sum_iq_i=1$, and $q_i\le p_i/\alpha$. ES is the largest expected loss over this constrained family. The cap prevents arbitrary concentration of scenario probability; at most a factor $1/\alpha$ can be assigned to any original probability mass. In the equally weighted case, the cap is $1/(n\alpha)$, which explains why a partial final observation is required when $n\alpha$ is noninteger.

The feasible weights depend on the original scenario probabilities and the tail level, not on the portfolio. Therefore evaluating $X$, $Y$, and $X+Y$ with this same feasible family immediately proves subadditivity: the maximum of the sum of two linear objectives cannot exceed the sum of their separate maxima. At an atom, the tail allocation is allowed to use a fractional weight; WCE on an indivisible atomic space instead restricts the available events and may give a smaller value.

Ties do not create ambiguity in the scalar ES. Different optimal allocations among equal-profit states give the same objective. They can, however, affect component attribution when those states contain different asset-level profits. An implementation reporting asset contributions should record its tie allocation rather than assuming that a unique total risk implies a unique decomposition. This distinction follows from the finite optimization structure and is not a component-allocation procedure developed by the authors.

## Probability-space dependence and the limits of the result

Section 6 shows that ES is the largest WCE obtainable over all representations with the same marginal law. A sufficiently rich space permits splitting mass through an auxiliary uniform variable. For a finite vector, a copula construction preserves the entire joint distribution while supplying uniform coordinates whose quantiles generate the components. WCE and ES then coincide componentwise. Applying that construction to $(X,Y,X+Y)$ gives another proof of ES subadditivity.

This is not a license to change dependence when aggregating risks. The representation preserves the joint law; it changes the available events used to define WCE. The economic loss distribution is unchanged. Corollary 6.3 therefore explains exactly why a distribution-based risk engine should prefer ES to a WCE calculation whose answer can depend on arbitrary scenario atomization.

An implementation can reproduce the article without external data: implement both quantile conventions, all five risk definitions on finite states, the Table 1 boundary cases, the atom-corrected empirical average, and the convex objective. The principal validation is agreement of the ES representations and the exact inequalities. No empirical claim about the superiority of a fitted model follows from these algebraic checks. Coherence solves an aggregation and definition problem; it does not choose a horizon, estimate probabilities, or make rare losses well observed.
