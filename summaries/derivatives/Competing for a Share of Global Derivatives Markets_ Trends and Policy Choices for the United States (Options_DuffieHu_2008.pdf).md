# Competing for a Share of Global Derivatives Markets: Trends and Policy Choices for the United States

Darrell Duffie and Henry T. C. Hu. June 3, 2008 draft, explicitly described as substantially identical to the February 19, 2008 draft and speaking as of that date. The document is marked “Preliminary and Incomplete.”

Source: [Options_DuffieHu_2008.pdf](../../Finance/Options_DuffieHu_2008.pdf). All 57 pages were read, including the three appendices, references, and 147 endnotes. Page numbers below correspond to PDF and printed pages. This is a historical research and policy report, not an option-pricing model, current legal guide, or causal policy evaluation.

## Why this matters and the authors' contribution

The report challenges a broad narrative that derivatives business was leaving the United States for Europe. Its evidence indicates that the United States remained strong in exchange-traded products and generally maintained or increased its share of traditional OTC trading. At the same time, some complex, potentially high-margin businesses appeared more concentrated in Europe. The distinction between notional volume and economically valuable intermediation is central.

The contribution is a combined measurement and policy framework. The authors compare multiple incompatible-looking datasets, identify what each actually measures, and explain why financial-center competitiveness depends on the location of customers, skilled professionals, and complementary services. Their proposed response is broader than deregulating derivatives: improve the advantages of the financial center, coordinate regulation, retain effective investor protection and financial stability, and collect better location data.

This is not a demonstration that a larger domestic derivatives sector is always socially preferable. Efficient risk transfer can benefit users worldwide; some complicated products can be unsuitable or poorly understood. High dealer revenue is not necessarily high social value. The report was written during the developing subprime crisis and acknowledges large losses, operational weaknesses, and systemic-risk concerns. Its historical judgments should be interpreted using that information cutoff.

For reproduction, the task is to rebuild a set of descriptive indicators with consistent geography, denominators, timing, and product coverage, then assess how far they support the stated policy claims. There is no estimated treatment effect of a regulatory merger, immigration change, or tax reduction to replicate. The policy mechanisms remain largely reasoned hypotheses supported by existing research and industry commentary.

## Measurement comes before the market-share conclusion

A derivatives contract's notional amount usually refers to a reference principal or underlying value. It is not its market value, expected loss, value at risk, net credit exposure, or contribution to GDP. The report cites about \$516 trillion of global OTC notional outstanding at mid-2007. That figure communicates scale, but multiplying it by a policy-induced change in market share would not produce a welfare estimate.

Several geographical concepts coexist. An exchange has a headquarters; an electronic participant may trade from another country; a dealer can have separate sales, trading, booking, and risk-management locations; and a multinational bank's headquarters need not locate its derivatives staff. Hedge funds likewise have a legal domicile and a distinct investment-manager location. Each can be relevant to different benefits such as employment, tax revenue, client access, or systemic exposure.

The BIS triennial turnover data are primarily based on the location of a reporting dealer's sales desk, with trading-desk reports used when appropriate, and are adjusted for local double counting. The New York Fed's related survey uses trading-desk location; the report notes that its April 2007 comparison did not find a large difference. That empirical reassurance is specific to those data, not proof that all location conventions are interchangeable.

OCC data cover U.S. commercial banks. They omit some investment-bank and nonbank activity and can reflect global operations of reporting institutions rather than production physically occurring in New York. BBA credit-derivatives surveys have another scope, while exchange statistics include products and counting conventions different from OTC turnover. Comparing a stock of outstanding contracts with a flow of new trades is especially misleading.

A faithful reconstruction should maintain a separate record for each indicator: reference date or period, stock versus flow, product scope, institutional coverage, geographical assignment, currency units, and double-counting adjustment. Only then should ratios or growth rates be computed. The report's descriptive strength is recognizing many of these distinctions; its weakness is that some remaining tables and assertions do not fully reconcile them.

## Financial-center employment and the broader economic setting

The report places derivatives within wholesale financial services. Cited CEBR estimates put U.S. wholesale-financial-services growth at about 76% between 1997 and 2006, while its global share stayed near 37%. Financial services were reported at about 8% of U.S. GDP. The U.S. share of financial-services exports rose from 12.6% in 1995 to 19.7% in 2004, while the United Kingdom was reported at 23.8% in 2004.

These aggregates provide context, not a derivatives-specific output series. The employment category NAICS 523 includes securities, commodity contracts, and other financial investment activities. It contains many jobs unrelated to derivatives and does not isolate quant research, trading, sales, or operations. In 2006, New York City's concentration in this category was about 4.5 times the national concentration; Chicago's was about 96% above the national level. The report cites 191,000 New York City jobs in the broader securities category in October 2007.

A separate London estimate attributes 13,300 of 247,000 wholesale-finance jobs directly to derivatives-market services, with the remainder distributed across investment banking, equities, bonds, foreign exchange, fund management, insurance, and central banking. This is not a directly comparable count to the New York series. Figure 1's London and New York growth comparison therefore illustrates activity in related financial sectors rather than a harmonized census of derivatives employment.

The distinction matters for assessing spillovers. A larger derivatives desk may support legal, accounting, information technology, and asset-management services, but simple employment totals do not establish the size of the causal multiplier. The paper cites outside work on employment spillovers and a stock-transfer-tax scenario; it does not estimate a new multiplier specific to derivatives relocation.

## Traditional markets: strong growth without broad U.S. displacement

For exchange-traded products, the report compares North American and European activity and describes continued U.S. strength, particularly the large interest-rate futures complex. Electronic trading and cross-border ownership make a simple exchange-headquarters allocation incomplete. For example, the share of Eurex traders located in the United Kingdom reportedly rose from 7% in 1997 to 46% in 2005. LIFFE had about 75% foreign customer clearing activity and the London Metal Exchange about 90%. These examples separate trading access and exchange ownership from local employment.

For traditional OTC foreign-exchange and interest-rate derivatives, BIS surveys across 54 jurisdictions show the U.S. share rising from 18.9% in 1998 to 23.8% in 2007. The United Kingdom's share also rose, from about 36% to 42.5%. U.S. daily volume increased from roughly \$90 billion to \$607 billion. Both centers can gain share as smaller centers lose share, and absolute expansion can coexist with a decline in some other product segment.

Credit derivatives provide another comparison. Table 1 gives the following BBA geographical shares:

| Year | North America | London | Other Europe | Rest of world |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 2002 | Not reported | 51% | Not reported | Not reported |
| 2003 | 41% | 45% | 5% | 9% |
| 2004 | 40% | 44% | 6% | 10% |
| 2006 | 41% | 37% | 11% | 12% |

The 2006 entries sum to 101%, consistent with rounded shares. North America is approximately stable in these observations, while London's loss is mainly accompanied by gains elsewhere in Europe and the rest of the world. One should not relabel the entire North American category “New York” or treat a London decline as a U.S. gain without further data.

OCC and BBA series on credit notional outstanding provide corroborating scale information, but their institutional coverage differs. The report notes that three U.S. banks account for about 91% of U.S. commercial-bank credit-derivatives volume. Concentration in these reporting institutions is not the same as concentration of all global trading or all U.S. market participants.

The equity-derivatives picture is less favorable to the U.S. proxy. Global OTC equity notional rose from about \$4.55 trillion in June 2005 to \$9.22 trillion in June 2007. U.S. commercial-bank equity derivatives rose from about \$1.26 trillion in the fourth quarter of 2005 to \$2.64 trillion in the second quarter of 2007. Those starting dates differ and should not be used as if they defined identical growth windows. The longer-run U.S.-bank share of the global total reportedly fell from about 52% to 29%, but the interpretation depends on whether omitted investment banks and other institutions offset that decline.

Commodity derivatives expanded globally from about \$2.94 trillion in June 2005 to \$7.6 trillion in June 2007. The reported U.S.-bank share rose from about 28.5% to 34% over December 2000–June 2007. The source paragraph accidentally calls the category “equity” in part of this commodity discussion; the product identity must be retained correctly when assembling the data.

## Volume is not revenue, and revenue is not profit

Pages 15–20 distinguish low-margin traditional contracts from more engineered products. A five-year vanilla interest-rate swap is described as having a bid–ask spread of roughly two or three basis points of swap rate. If a dealer captures one basis point and the swap has duration about four years, the approximate present-value margin is $4\times10^{-4}$ of notional, or 0.04%. This is a duration calculation, not one basis point of notional multiplied arbitrarily.

Margins on some highly structured equity or bespoke credit products are suggested to reach 0.5–1.0% of notional. These are rough examples from the report, not estimated universal net margins. They do not deduct all hedging, staffing, capital, funding, credit-loss, and operational costs. Innovation can also generate temporary advantages in related businesses or reputation before a product becomes commoditized.

The OCC-based evidence attempts to compare revenue intensity across products. In the second quarter of 2007, interest-rate derivatives account for about 81% of U.S. commercial-bank notional but only 49% of associated revenue; equity OTC contracts account for about 1.7% of notional but 16.6% of revenue. These proportions suggest that a loss of a small notional share could matter economically if it occurs in a high-revenue business.

Table 2 averages quarterly revenue over the last 12 quarters, except credit, where only two quarters are available. Its printed entries are:

| Category | Q2 2007 notional, trillions | Revenue numeral as printed | Printed ratio |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Interest rate | 122 | 1181 | 9.7 |
| Foreign exchange | 13.8 | 1336 | 96.8 |
| Equity | 2.6 | 333 | 128 |
| Credit | 7.7 | 853 | 72.3 |
| Commodity and other | 1 | 115 | 121 |

This table has material internal problems. Its revenue header says billions, but the magnitudes and ratios require reconciliation with the original OCC releases before assigning economic units. The simple arithmetic $853/7.7$ is about 110.8, not 72.3. Likewise, $115/1$ is 115, not 121, though rounding of an underlying denominator could explain the latter. These are visible in the PDF itself, not text-extraction artifacts. The note preserves the source numbers as reported and does not silently convert them into a clean estimated revenue yield.

The qualitative ranking claimed by the authors is that equity and commodity contracts produce well over ten times as much revenue per unit notional as interest-rate contracts, and credit roughly seven times. The latter depends especially on the inconsistent table and short revenue window. A careful reproduction must recover the underlying revenue series, harmonize units and reporting dates, and recompute the ratios before treating those multiples as quantitative estimates.

Even corrected revenue-to-outstanding-notional ratios are not dealer bid–ask margins. Revenues include inventory revaluation and proprietary exposures, and outstanding notional is a stock rather than turnover. A growing market can change the stock denominator without a corresponding change in current earned spread. The paper acknowledges noise and assumes that foreign banks have similar product-specific revenue intensity when discussing possible international shifts; that assumption is not estimated.

The authors also regard CDO issuance as informative about structured-credit activity, including synthetic products, while acknowledging classification debates. Figures 6 and 8 show the USD series exceeding Europe in high-grade structured-finance CDOs and total CLOs, respectively; Figure 7 instead shows Europe larger in middle-market CLOs. The USD legend should be reconciled with the underlying provider's currency/geography definition before using the figures as location shares. Figure 2 shows the credit-squeeze collapse, but its January 2008 observation is only one month compared with the preceding full quarters. These figures are graphical summaries from external providers, not a machine-readable issuance dataset with a complete deal-level classification protocol.

## A location mechanism, not an estimated causal model

Section IV.A argues that financial services cluster because proximity lowers communication costs, improves customer interaction, supports specialized labor markets, and creates knowledge spillovers. Banks benefit from proximity to other dealers, corporate and hedge-fund clients, lawyers, accountants, regulators, and information providers. Derivatives' technical complexity increases the value of personal networks and shared expertise.

London's time zone is a concrete example: a desk can interact with the Asian close and the U.S. open, as well as European clients. Electronic execution reduces the need to share a trading floor with every counterparty, but does not necessarily eliminate the need for nearby structuring, sales, risk management, or client meetings. Large fixed costs and relatively low marginal processing costs can reinforce existing concentrations rather than produce geographically dispersed activity.

The report cites agglomeration research and interviews with industry professionals. It does not specify a complete interview sampling frame, response rate, coding protocol, or statistical treatment of selection bias. The accounts are mechanism evidence and informed opinion, not random-sample estimates of the causal value of locating in London.

Endnote 34 proposes adapting an industry-migration model. A compact interpretation, rather than an estimated equation from this report, is

$$
E[\Delta Y_{t+1}\mid\mathcal I_t]\approx a\,E[\Delta F_{t+1}\mid\mathcal I_t]+b(F_t-Y_t),
$$

where $Y_t$ measures a city's concentration of OTC derivatives activity and $F_t$ its concentration of broader financial services. The first term represents common growth or migration; the second represents catch-up toward the broader financial center. The authors do not estimate $a$ or $b$, choose a normalization for concentration, or establish an instrument that distinguishes cause from joint location choice.

This mechanism motivates a dataset containing employment and skill composition, client locations, complementary financial services, infrastructure, costs, and regulation across centers and through time. Reverse causality is unavoidable: talented workers attract desks, and successful desks attract talented workers. A descriptive correlation or a city ranking cannot resolve it. The report explicitly recommends collecting the information needed for such work rather than claiming that the identification problem has already been solved.

## Regulation: fragmentation is a cost, but consolidation is not a complete answer

Sections IV.B and the endnotes describe the U.S. regulatory environment as it existed in early 2008. Institutions and products could face overlapping federal, state, and self-regulatory oversight. The Commodity Futures Modernization Act had reduced uncertainty for many negotiated OTC transactions among eligible participants, but exchange-traded innovation still encountered jurisdictional disputes.

The report uses concrete examples: rival SEC/CFTC approval routes for exchange-traded credit products; delays concerning options on gold ETFs; and CFTC/FERC disagreement over energy-market enforcement. Eurex launched an exchange-traded credit derivative on March 27, 2007, ahead of U.S. rivals while U.S. jurisdictional issues were being resolved. These cases illustrate possible delay mechanisms. They do not quantify the counterfactual welfare or trading volume had approvals occurred earlier.

A merger of agencies might reduce boundary disputes but could also import a more interventionist culture into activities previously regulated differently. The authors therefore do not offer a simple numerical conclusion that an SEC–CFTC merger would improve innovation. Coordination, consistent treatment of economically similar exposures, and cost–benefit analysis receive more measured support.

The comparison with the U.K. FSA is similarly qualified. “Principles based” and “rules based” are not clean dichotomies: U.S. antifraud provisions and parts of the CFTC framework already used broad principles, while the FSA handbook contained extensive detailed material. The U.K. could be more intrusive in particular areas, including disclosure of cash-settled equity positions during takeovers and hedge-fund controls.

The enforcement comparison is striking but not standardized. The report lists FSA fines of £5.3 million in 2007 and £13.3 million in 2006, versus SEC disgorgement and penalties of \$496.4 million and \$1.804 billion in the corresponding fiscal years. The CFTC imposed more than \$1.8 billion in sanctions over five years. These figures use different currencies, categories, periods, institutions, and market populations. They establish an apparent scale difference, not a clean estimate of relative deterrence or optimal enforcement.

The authors argue that shifting to broader principles without examining enforcement could reduce deterrence. They also give credit to cooperative prudential approaches to derivatives-confirmation backlogs. Their policy criterion includes investor protection, market integrity, efficiency, and stability alongside compliance burden and competitiveness. All legal and institutional descriptions here are historical claims from the supplied draft; the note makes no assertion that those rules or agencies remain unchanged.

## Human capital, taxes, currencies, and accounting

Derivatives engineering depends on scarce quantitative skills. The report cites U.S. declines from 1995 to 2004 in engineering doctorates of 3%, mathematics doctorates of 10%, and physical-science doctorates of 14%, alongside only 2% growth in computer-science doctorates. It contrasts broader science-and-engineering doctorate growth over 1993–2003 of about 1% in the United States, 44% in the United Kingdom, and 71% in Japan. These are overlapping but different fields and periods; they should not be combined into one internally consistent panel without returning to the underlying education statistics.

Foreign nationals accounted for reported shares of 46.1% of U.S. physical-science doctorates, 55.1% of mathematics doctorates, and 58.7% of computer-science doctorates in 2005. The mechanism is that immigration restrictions or low retention could constrain local financial-engineering capacity. However, the text's statement that only about 13% of foreign doctorate recipients stay in the United States is directly qualified by endnote 96, which cites a 72.7% stay figure from another survey. The populations and definitions are not reconciled. The 13% figure should not be accepted as an established retention estimate.

London's access to European mathematical training and worker mobility is offered as a location advantage. A bank may retain a U.S. headquarters while moving technical teams abroad. This again distinguishes firm nationality from the location of knowledge-intensive work. The report provides an example of such a modeling center, but no complete census or estimate of how many jobs visa policy causes to move.

Cost comparisons cut in different directions. Mercer's March 2007 index makes London 26.3% more expensive than New York, with London second and New York fifteenth among covered cities. A separate financial-services tax comparison gives combined firm tax burdens of 35% in London and 46% in New York, while employee effective burdens are 34% and 33%, respectively. These are index and model-tax comparisons, not a tax calculator for any specific firm or employee. They do not imply that one city dominates every location decision.

The rise of the euro expands the underlying debt markets that create demand for derivatives. The report gives euro-denominated debt shares and reserve-currency trends, and notes that euro contracts represented about 39% of surveyed OTC interest-rate volume in April 2007 versus 32% for dollar contracts. Deeper European bond markets, clearing infrastructure, credit indices, and dealer platforms provide complementary sources of growth. The argument is a market-development mechanism, not proof that all derivative activity must occur in the currency issuer's territory.

Appendix I explains why accounting treatment matters. A derivative hedge may be marked to market while the underlying commercial commitment is not. An airline hedging fuel can then report a derivative loss without recognizing the offsetting reduction in future fuel costs. Hedge accounting attempts to align reporting, but complex qualification rules can impose substantial costs or distort incentives. The report discusses historical SFAS 133, IAS 39, fair-value standards, and convergence efforts. It cites 57 hedge-accounting-related company restatements in 2005 and major Fannie Mae restatement costs as examples, not as an estimated effect on derivatives-market location.

Fair value also requires reliable valuation. Model-dependent instruments can have low initial market value but large economic exposures, and a precise reported value can conceal substantial parameter uncertainty. The authors support disclosure about valuation quality and more consistent treatment of underlying assets and hedges. They acknowledge that greater harmonization and flexibility must be weighed against investor protection and governance of standard setters.

## The appendices qualify the competitiveness evidence

Appendix II classifies RISK award winners by headquarters location for 2000–2007. The table caption ends at 2006, but the actual table includes 2007. U.S. institutions win five of eight overall derivatives-house awards and six of the seven exchange awards actually given. French institutions frequently win equity-derivatives awards, while European banks dominate currency awards. The sample is editorially selected; no independent validation of award reliability is available.

These awards measure perceived institutional capability, not where the winning business took place. A U.S. bank's award can reflect a French transaction or Asian expansion, and a French bank can earn a substantial share of revenue in the United States. The report's discussion of Merrill Lynch and Lehman Brothers is confined to the pre-crisis information cutoff and should not be interpreted as an evaluation incorporating later events.

Appendix III examines hedge-fund investment-manager location. HFN data put global assets at \$2.68 trillion in September 2007 versus \$1.17 trillion three years earlier. In Table 4, the U.S.-manager share falls from 74.5% in 2004 Q2 to 66.2% in 2007 Q3; Europe's rises from 22.7% to 30.1%. Asia rises from 1.4% to 1.8%. The table omits 2006 Q4, so it is not a complete uninterrupted quarterly panel. Assets can grow in both places while shares change.

Hedge-fund managers are presented as relatively mobile, with tax treatment and personal location important. This is qualified by strategies such as activism, where proximity to target companies may matter. Data-source coverage, self-reporting, performance effects on assets, fund launches and closures, and actual manager migration are not separately decomposed. A change in assets by manager location cannot be attributed entirely to tax-driven relocations.

## A reproducible extension and the limits of the conclusion

To reproduce the descriptive results, recover the contemporaneous BIS turnover and outstanding-position releases, OCC quarterly bank reports, BBA credit survey, issuance-provider series, labor statistics, education reports, and HFN location tables. Preserve vintage dates. Match numerators and denominators before calculating shares. Reconcile Table 2 directly against OCC data; do not propagate its unit and arithmetic inconsistencies. Keep firm domicile, sales desk, trading desk, and worker location in separate fields.

The next research step would require a longitudinal city-level dataset and an explicit identification design for location choices. The paper proposes this work but does not supply it. No reported regression establishes the effect of regulatory fragmentation after controlling for customer proximity, skill supply, product mix, time zone, and prior agglomeration. Interview evidence and comparative tables motivate hypotheses rather than settle them.

The supported historical conclusion is narrower and useful: the available data did not substantiate a wholesale U.S. exit from derivatives markets, although product mix and high-value activity deserved closer attention. The strongest recommendation is to improve the evidence on where economically valuable derivatives services are produced and why. Sound policy requires that measurement alongside the benefits and risks of innovation, rather than treating either notional-volume leadership or lighter regulation as an adequate objective by itself.
