# Bivariate GARCH Estimation of the Optimal Commodity Futures Hedge

Richard T. Baillie and Robert J. Myers, *Journal of Applied Econometrics* 6(2), April–June 1991, 109–124. [Source PDF](../../Finance/CommodityPrices_Baillie_1991.pdf). The supplied 17-page scan contains a JSTOR cover and the complete 16-page article, including seven tables, six figures, footnotes, and references. Printed page 109 is PDF page 2. All pages were read visually. There is no mathematical appendix in this article; several tests and a utility argument are referred to the authors' 1989 working paper, which is not included in the supplied file.

## Why this matters

The minimum-variance futures hedge depends on the covariance between the cash exposure and the futures contract, divided by futures variance. A constant regression coefficient estimates an average relationship. It need not describe the risk faced tomorrow when the two variances and their covariance change differently. Baillie and Myers estimate those three conditional moments jointly and use them to construct daily hedge ratios for six commodities.

The contribution is a conditional-covariance implementation of hedging, extending earlier applications that allowed changing variances while holding cash–futures correlation fixed. Its empirical evidence supports substantial variation in estimated hedge ratios. It does not establish that the resulting strategy is profitable after trading costs, that the ratios have a structural unit root, or that a live investor could have achieved the reported historical comparison. These limits follow from the experiment: the main fitted sample concerns 1986 contracts, the second sample concerns earlier 1982 contracts, and performance is measured using conditional variances generated by the fitted model.

The paper is particularly useful for separating three questions: whether a constant covariance matrix fits the observations; whether a fitted dynamic covariance changes the prescribed hedge; and whether that prescription improves a genuinely independent economic outcome. The first two are addressed directly. The third receives a model-based historical exercise with important timing and evaluation qualifications.

## Data and the exact economic exposure

Daily cash prices and closing futures settlement prices come from the Columbia Center for the Study of Futures Markets data tape. The commodities are beef, coffee, corn, cotton, gold, and soybeans. One delivery month is used per commodity: May for coffee, July for cotton, September for corn and soybeans, and December for beef and gold. Two contracts are examined for each commodity, one maturing in 1982 and one in 1986.

For each contract, observations cover the dates on which that particular contract traded. The samples therefore differ across commodities. This is not a continuously rolled front-month series or a fixed-maturity interpolation. The authors say the 1982 estimation results were similar and omit them to save space; the detailed estimation tables report 1986. Figures show histories extending into 1985, but the exact first dates, observation counts, missing-date rules, and cash-quote specifications are not listed. A reconstruction needs the original contract identifiers and cash-market locations, quality grades, and timing conventions. Different cash locations can materially change basis risk.

Let $p_t=\log P_t$ and $f_t=\log F_t$, where $P_t$ is cash price and $F_t$ is the settlement price of the chosen futures contract. Statistical estimation uses $1000\Delta p_t$ and $1000\Delta f_t$. Thus a conditional variance of one in a reported recursion corresponds to $10^{-6}$ squared log-return units. Scaling both series by 1000 leaves their covariance-to-variance ratio unchanged. It does not make the fitted variance a dollar P&L variance.

The hedge problem starts with a fixed long cash position and a short futures position. The source defines $b_{t-1}$ as futures sales notional divided by cash-position value. With normalized arithmetic price changes, portfolio return is

$$
R_t=R_t^c-b_{t-1}R_t^f.
$$

This futures return is a price-change normalization, not a return on the initial value of a futures contract, which is normally zero. If $Q_c$ cash units are hedged with $n_f$ contracts of size $q_f$, the notional convention gives

$$
n_f=b_{t-1}\frac{Q_cP_{t-1}}{q_fF_{t-1}}.
$$

Contract counts, rounding, cash financing, margin calls, and collateral returns are not analyzed. The source assumes margin can be met with interest-bearing government bonds and abstracts from the resulting implementation constraints.

## Deriving the hedge and distinguishing objectives

Write the conditional covariance matrix of the two normalized returns as

$$
H_t=\begin{pmatrix}h_{11,t}&h_{12,t}\\h_{12,t}&h_{22,t}\end{pmatrix}.
$$

At date $t-1$, the variance of the next-period hedged return is

$$
V_t(b)=h_{11,t}-2bh_{12,t}+b^2h_{22,t}.
$$

When $h_{22,t}>0$, differentiation gives the unconstrained optimum and residual variance:

$$
b_{t-1}^*=\frac{h_{12,t}}{h_{22,t}},\qquad
V_t(b^*)=h_{11,t}-\frac{h_{12,t}^2}{h_{22,t}}.
$$

The reduction relative to no hedge is the squared conditional correlation, $h_{12,t}^2/(h_{11,t}h_{22,t})$, provided $H_t$ is positive semidefinite and $h_{11,t}>0$. This identity is an algebraic consequence of the model, not an empirical performance estimate. It explains why a volatile futures market is not necessarily a useful hedge: the covariance must be large relative to both marginal variances.

A negative ratio prescribes buying futures alongside the long cash exposure. A ratio greater than one prescribes more futures notional than cash value. Both occur naturally in an unconstrained covariance problem; the source does not impose a zero-to-one hedge interval. For a constrained interval $[b_L,b_U]$, the one-period quadratic solution would be the projection of $b^*$ onto that interval. This is a possible implementation variant, not a restriction used in the paper.

Equations (7)–(8), printed page 117, move from return covariance to covariance of log-price changes by explicitly equating returns with log changes. For actual arithmetic cash and futures P&L, that step is an approximation. Exactly, the relevant normalized changes are $e^{\Delta p_t}-1$ and $e^{\Delta f_t}-1$. Their covariance ratio generally differs from the log-return ratio. The approximation is usually plausible for small daily changes, but the cash outliers reported here are precisely where its accuracy deserves checking.

The source also argues that zero expected futures returns make minimum-variance hedging broadly equivalent to expected-utility maximization. This statement needs distributional structure. Under the paper's conditional joint-normal *linear return* model, all hedges have the same conditional mean when the futures mean is zero, and reducing variance gives a mean-preserving contraction; an increasing concave utility then prefers the minimum-variance choice, assuming expected utility exists. Zero futures mean alone does not give this ordering for arbitrary joint distributions. For a mean–variance objective $E[R]-\gamma\operatorname{Var}(R)/2$, the more general unconstrained choice is

$$
b=\frac{h_{12}}{h_{22}}-\frac{E[R^f]}{\gamma h_{22}}.
$$

The second term is speculative demand, which the pure hedge criterion excludes. Moreover, zero expected *log* change does not imply zero expected arithmetic futures price change. Under a nondegenerate normal log change, Jensen's inequality gives a positive arithmetic mean when the log mean is zero. These distinctions prevent transferring the utility claim without its qualifications.

## Univariate distributional analysis

The initial model for each cash or futures series is

$$
1000\Delta y_t=\mu+\epsilon_t,\qquad
\epsilon_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}\sim t_\nu(0,\sigma_t^2),
$$

$$
\sigma_t^2=\omega+\alpha\epsilon_{t-1}^2+\beta\sigma_{t-1}^2.
$$

The Student distribution is parameterized so that $\sigma_t^2$ is its variance. It therefore requires $\nu>2$ and the appropriate scale normalization. A reproducible per-observation log likelihood is

$$
\ell_t=\log\Gamma\left(\frac{\nu+1}{2}\right)-\log\Gamma\left(\frac\nu2\right)
-\frac12\log[\pi(\nu-2)\sigma_t^2]
-\frac{\nu+1}{2}\log\left[1+\frac{\epsilon_t^2}{(\nu-2)\sigma_t^2}\right].
$$

This expression is reconstructed from the stated distribution. The paper says numerical maximization uses the Berndt–Hall–Hall–Hausman algorithm, but does not supply initial variance values, optimization starts, stopping tolerances, or all parameter bounds. A standard conditional-variance implementation imposes $\omega>0$ and $\alpha,\beta\ge0$; covariance stationarity additionally requires $\alpha+\beta<1$. Near-unit persistence is not the same as establishing exact integration.

Tables I–II report $\nu^{-1}$ rather than $\nu$. Futures estimates range from .146 for soybeans to .182 for gold, corresponding to about 6.85 to 5.49 degrees of freedom. Conditional Student kurtosis is $3(\nu-2)/(\nu-4)$ when $\nu>4$. This describes standardized innovations, not the unconditional kurtosis of a GARCH return, which also depends on variance dynamics and may fail to exist.

Reported asymptotic standard errors include .021 for cash-beef $\alpha=.088$, .025 for cash-coffee $\alpha=.199$, .051 for futures-beef $\alpha=.101$, and .021 for futures-gold $\beta=.942$. The printed coffee-futures persistence is $1.014$, above one. It should not be silently forced below one or described as a fitted finite-unconditional-variance process. Soybean futures have substantially lower persistence, .822. These are point estimates with uncertainty, not exact statements about the data-generating process.

Cash standardized-residual kurtoses are 7.900, 34.094, 3.857, 25.30, 7.227, and 3.260 in commodity order. Futures counterparts are 3.735, 4.407, 4.155, 5.003, 6.772, and 4.291. The cash coffee and cotton tails remain problematic. The diagnostics include tenth-order Box–Pierce statistics on residuals and squared normalized residuals; passing them does not validate every feature of a conditional density.

The authors' general statement that mean terms are insignificant has a visible exception. Corn futures have $\widehat\mu=-1.328$ with standard error .490 in Table II, approximately a conventional $t$ ratio of −2.71. The positive-definite bivariate fit also reports a negative corn-futures mean of −1.212 with standard error .499. The zero-mean motivation should therefore be treated as an approximation or maintained hedging restriction, not a uniform empirical finding across the tables.

## Cointegration and mean specification

The authors report that Phillips and Phillips–Perron tests do not reject a unit root in log cash or futures levels, while log changes show little serial correlation. They therefore use constant mean vectors. Before doing so, they test whether cash and futures are cointegrated by regressing cash log price on an intercept and futures log price, then testing the residual for a unit root.

Table III's level-regression slopes are .970, .926, 1.058, .624, 1.337, and .280. These are **not** the constant hedge ratios subsequently used, which come from regressions of changes. Residual unit-root statistics with trend are −2.11, −2.25, −.22, −2.51, −2.69, and −.77; the corresponding no-trend statistics are −2.13, −2.44, −.70, −1.84, −2.09, and −1.04. The Newey–West adjustment uses 20 lags. The source reports no rejection of no cointegration and consequently excludes an error-correction term.

Nonrejection in these short, contract-specific samples is weaker than proof of no economic long-run relation. A fixed delivery date also makes remaining maturity shrink through the sample. A carry relation that varies with maturity is not automatically a stationary relation with constant coefficients. The source motivates its test with $f_{t,k}-p_t=r_{t,k}+\delta$, appealing to integrated rates and a premium. That argument suppresses storage costs and convenience yields, and is not a general no-arbitrage theorem for all six physical commodities. The statistical specification can be reproduced without adopting that simplified economic argument as an identity.

## Bivariate covariance models and a reproducible filter

For $y_t=(p_t,f_t)'$, the common mean model is $1000\Delta y_t=\mu+\epsilon_t$. The bivariate estimation uses a **Gaussian** conditional density, unlike the univariate Student models. The first covariance specification is diagonal vech:

$$
\operatorname{vech}(H_t)=c+A\operatorname{vech}(\epsilon_{t-1}\epsilon_{t-1}')
+B\operatorname{vech}(H_{t-1}),
$$

where $\operatorname{vech}(H)=(h_{11},h_{21},h_{22})'$ and $A,B$ are diagonal three-by-three matrices. Each variance and the covariance respond to their own corresponding lagged shock product and lagged moment. The specification has nine covariance parameters, plus two means. It permits changing correlation; imposing only diagonal $A,B$ does not impose constant correlation.

Corresponding cash/futures mean pairs are (.034,.061), (−.014,.717), (−.398,−1.004), (.576,−.221), (.221,−.073), and (−.467,−.396). The table reports standard errors for every coefficient. For example, beef's covariance shock coefficient is −.019 (.014), and its covariance persistence coefficient is 1.004 (.017). Thus the prose assertion that all covariance coefficients are positive and significant is not literally supported by this row. The negative coefficient is retained here as printed.

Diagonal vech does not by itself guarantee that every recursively generated $H_t$ is positive definite. A reproduction must inspect both marginal variances and the determinant at every step; separate positivity of marginal recursions is insufficient. The article also estimates

$$
H_t=C'C+A'\epsilon_{t-1}\epsilon_{t-1}'A+B'H_{t-1}B,
$$

with symmetric two-by-two $C$ and unrestricted two-by-two $A,B$. The three free entries of symmetric $C$ plus four entries each of $A,B$ give eleven covariance parameters. This is the source's convention; replacing symmetric $C$ by an upper-triangular matrix changes parameter values even if both can represent the same constant covariance. Each contribution is positive semidefinite. Strict positivity follows, for example, when $C$ has full rank; the displayed algebra alone only guarantees semidefiniteness in singular cases.

For either model, compute residuals using the fixed estimated means, initialize $H$, recurse using observations through $t-1$, and maximize

$$
\ell(\theta)=-\frac12\sum_t\left[2\log(2\pi)+\log\det H_t+\epsilon_t'H_t^{-1}\epsilon_t\right].
$$

Use a stable factorization rather than explicitly forming $H_t^{-1}$. Reject or appropriately constrain parameter candidates producing inadmissible covariances. After estimation, the day-$t$ hedge uses $h_{12,t}/h_{22,t}$ before observing day-$t$ returns. No contemporaneous shock should enter that day's hedge. The paper describes FORTRAN estimation using the BHHH algorithm and credits Ken Kroner for the program; the actual code is not embedded in the PDF.

The diagonal-vech log likelihoods are −1881.72, −2270.84, −2180.05, −2092.68, −2983.60, and −1713.22. Positive-definite-model values are −1883.58, −2252.61, −2178.28, −2101.56, −2906.19, and −1707.46. The alternative is not uniformly better even though it has more parameters; these differently restricted specifications should not be compared by a naive nested-model parameter-count test.

Reported likelihood-ratio statistics against constant covariance range from 61.34 to 491.68 for diagonal vech and from 57.62 to 464.40 for the positive-definite form. They strongly motivate time variation descriptively. Boundary restrictions and nuisance-parameter identification under a zero-dynamics null complicate routine chi-square calibration; the article does not establish a modern uniform reference distribution for these cases. Residual multivariate Hosking $H(5)$ statistics and separate squared-residual tests provide additional specification checks, with some residual tail and dependence problems remaining, especially coffee cash.

These entries multiply matrices; they are not separate scalar ARCH persistence coefficients. In particular, a negative entry of $A$, $B$, or $C$ does not make its quadratic contribution to $H_t$ negative semidefinite. Conversely, checking that a particular diagonal entry is below one does not establish covariance stationarity. The relevant second-moment stability calculation uses the spectral radius of the linear covariance operator generated by $A$ and $B$. The soybean value $B_{11}=1.046$ therefore should not be interpreted in isolation as a univariate persistence estimate.

Some constant-matrix estimates are imprecise: the standard error for beef's $C_{11}=.532$ is 17.080, and gold's $C_{11}=-.978$ has standard error 51.210. Those large uncertainties reinforce the need to compare implied covariance paths and hedge behavior rather than attach economic meaning to each matrix entry. Matrix factorizations also admit sign-related parameter representations with the same quadratic contribution. The paper does not report confidence intervals for functions such as the hedge ratio derived jointly from these estimated entries. Its displayed performance comparison uses the diagonal-vech model, so this parameter table documents a specification check rather than a second reported strategy-performance table.

## Hedge paths, persistence, and performance

Figures 1–6 use the diagonal-vech estimates. The constant OLS hedge ratios, estimated from log changes, are .07 for beef, .25 for coffee, .61 for corn, .38 for cotton, .50 for gold, and .76 for soybeans. They differ materially from the level-regression slopes in the cointegration exercise.

The figures show time variation rather than a single common pattern. Beef's fitted hedge is near zero and sometimes negative. Coffee has a temporary spike toward .9. Corn moves from small positive values to values exceeding one. Cotton has large early fluctuations and falls substantially near the end of 1985; gold fluctuates around roughly .5, while soybeans generally have larger positive ratios. These are visual descriptions, not digitized daily estimates. No confidence bands for the estimated hedge paths are plotted.

The authors test the generated hedge-ratio series for unit roots and report nonrejection. Table VI fits changes scaled by 1000, with white-noise or MA(1) innovations. Gold's MA coefficient is −.28 (.038), and soybeans' is −.176 (.040). The prose singles out gold, but the table also contains the soybean MA term. Failing to reject a unit root in an estimated nonlinear ratio over one contract history does not establish that its population process is a random walk. Parameter-estimation error, persistent conditional moments, and the changing delivery horizon all deserve consideration. No inference adjustment for the generated hedge-ratio series is supplied.

The principal evaluation computes each period's conditional portfolio variance under the fitted bivariate GARCH model. It compares no hedge, the constant OLS ratio, and the dynamic covariance ratio. Percentage changes relative to no hedge are calculated date by date and then averaged. An average of these conditional percentage changes is not the percentage change in realized sample P&L variance or even necessarily the ratio of average conditional variances.

These are Table VII's signed average percentage changes; negative means risk reduction. The largest 1986 dynamic reduction is soybean risk, 56.8%, but its improvement over constant hedging is only two percentage points of unhedged variance. Cotton gains 14.8 percentage points relative to the constant hedge in 1986. In 1982, constant hedging increases the model-based risk for beef and coffee, whereas the dynamic rule reduces it.

The exercise called out of sample fits all parameters on **1986** and applies them to **1982**, updating covariance states from then-available 1982 price histories. It is a holdout across contract histories, but it runs backward in calendar time. An investor in 1982 could not have known the 1986-fitted parameters. This timing does not invalidate a stability experiment, but it rules out interpreting the comparison as a historical real-time trading result.

There is also a built-in feature of the evaluation criterion. For any alternative hedge $b_0$ and the same positive-definite $H_t$,

$$
V_t(b_0)-V_t(b_t^*)=h_{22,t}(b_0-b_t^*)^2\ge0.
$$

Consequently the fitted dynamic ratio must weakly dominate every other ratio under that model's own conditional variance, even when the filter is run on another price history. Table VII quantifies the model-implied cost of using a different hedge; it is not independent validation that the estimated covariance forecasts are correct. A stronger evaluation would use realized losses or an independent covariance target and preserve genuinely forward parameter timing. Those are proposed extensions, not analyses performed by the authors.

The article gives no standard errors or paired tests for Table VII differences. Its use of “significantly better” should therefore not be converted into a documented statistical rejection for the hedge-performance comparison. The model estimates have asymptotic standard errors, but uncertainty in parameters, covariance forecasts, and hedge ratios is not propagated into the performance table.

## Reproduction limits and investment interpretation

The core algorithm is fully specified at the model level: select a cash/futures pair, compute scaled log changes, estimate a joint conditional covariance recursion, forecast the next covariance matrix, and divide covariance by futures variance. The published diagonal-vech parameter table enables conditional-path reconstruction once the original observations and initialization are supplied. The article also supplies enough information to reproduce its notional hedge convention and signed performance metric.

Exact historical reproduction still needs contract calendars, cash-quote definitions, observations and missing-data handling, initial covariances, likelihood constraints and starts, the fitted 1982 results if those are to be compared, and the unpublished test details referred to the 1989 working paper. A change in price normalization or a modern futures roll rule would be a different experiment. A multivariate Student likelihood would likewise be an extension; the paper deliberately uses normal bivariate innovations.

For implementation, preserve separate records of estimated covariance quality and realized hedge performance. Daily changes in the ratio generate turnover, which is absent from the quadratic objective. Spread costs, commissions, liquidity limits, margin liquidity, contract indivisibility, and financing can make a smoothed or constrained hedge preferable to the instantaneous unconstrained ratio. The source does not estimate a threshold at which those costs outweigh its model-implied risk reductions.

The enduring result is a concrete way to make a hedge respond to changing conditional covariance. The evidence is strongest for rejecting the assumption that one unconditional slope captures every day's relationship. Claims about economic optimality, nonstationarity, and deployable performance require more than the fitted covariance ratios and the internally evaluated variance reductions reported here.
