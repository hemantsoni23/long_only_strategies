# Price Formation in Commodity Markets

**M. J. Lord, 1991.** *Journal of Applied Econometrics* 6(3), 239–254. [Source PDF](../../Finance/CommodityPrices_Lord_1991.pdf). The supplied file contains a JSTOR cover followed by the complete 16-page article. All 17 PDF pages were read, including the six tables, footnotes, data appendix, and references. Printed page 239 is PDF page 2. Detailed estimates repeatedly cited as Lord (1991), Annex C, belong to the separate book *Imperfect Competition and International Commodity Trade: Theory, Dynamics, and Policy Modelling* and are not present here.

## Why this matters

Lord asks whether an annual commodity-price model can retain economically interpretable supply, consumption, and inventory behavior while accommodating slow adjustment and persistent time series. The alternative he criticizes is to estimate an unrestricted reduced-form price equation whose coefficients cannot be reconciled with separately estimated behavioral relationships. His proposed model uses error correction in consumption and inventories, distributed price responses in production, an inventory accounting identity, and expected prices generated by a separate time-series model.

The contribution is this combination and its application to seven internationally traded commodities: coffee, cocoa, maize, soybeans, cotton, sugar, and copper. It is not a new general theory of competitive storage under uncertainty. There is no Bellman equation for storage, explicit nonnegative-inventory constraint, endogenous convenience yield, or equilibrium risk premium. The model is closer to a structural annual forecasting system. Its value for a quantitative investor is the discipline of connecting price forecasts to physical quantities and production lags, together with a useful example of how the apparent strength of an economic forecast depends on its information set.

The empirical comparison favors the behavioral model over its ARIMA benchmark for most commodities in 1988–1990, with copper the exception. Those results are conditional one-step forecasts, based on actual lagged endogenous variables and actual current and past exogenous variables. They do not constitute a futures trading strategy or a real-time forecast experiment with fully documented release lags. Neither transaction costs nor any risk-adjusted investment performance is calculated.

The closest methodological antecedents in the paper are Engle–Granger error correction, Granger–Lee multicointegration of stocks and flows, and Wallis's treatment of time-series expectations in a behavioral equation. Lord's novelty is narrower than the label “theory-consistent” might suggest: he seeks to preserve structural restrictions during dynamic specification and solution, while using commodity-specific equations rather than imposing one identical empirical process across markets.

## Variables, timing, and market clearing

Use $C_t$ for consumption, $Q_t$ for production, $K_t$ for physical stocks, $Y_t$ for aggregate economic activity, $P_t$ for the commodity price, and $D_t$ for a general price deflator. Lowercase letters denote logarithms. In particular, $r_t=p_t-d_t$ is the log real commodity price. The model operates at annual frequency. Production responses can begin several years after a price movement, whereas consumption generally adjusts much faster.

Inventory market clearing is written $K_t^d=K_t^s$. Supply of stocks is the quantity already on hand, with the accounting identity

$$
K_t^s=K_{t-1}^s+Q_t-C_t.
$$

This is an identity in physical levels. It is not $\Delta k_t=q_t-c_t$. Substituting logged behavioral equations into the clearing condition therefore produces a nonlinear system in levels. A replication that replaces the level identity with a log difference changes both the equilibrium and the size of inventory movements.

Inventory demand reflects transactions, precautionary holdings, and speculative demand. Production and consumption are primarily related to lagged conditions; speculative inventory demand depends on an expected future price. The article first presents the simplified log relationship

$$
k_t^d=\psi_0+\psi_1q_t+\psi_2p_t^e+v_{3t},
\qquad \psi_1,\psi_2>0.
$$

Consumption can replace production as the scale variable. Interest, storage, and depreciation costs are discussed as additional determinants but suppressed in the generic theoretical expression. The reported copper equation includes the current three-month US Treasury-bill rate. This heterogeneous empirical treatment matters: one cannot reproduce every commodity by estimating the simplified three-variable relationship alone.

For given beginning inventory, exogenous income, deflator, disturbances, and a price-expectations rule, the behavioral equations and the accounting identity determine the current market price jointly with quantities. A practical solver would evaluate consumption and production at a trial price, calculate end-of-year stock supply, compare it with desired stocks, and find a price that clears the inventory market. The paper describes this equilibrium logic but does not provide executable solution code, convergence tolerances, initial conditions, or the complete fitted equations needed to carry it out numerically.

## Consumption and error correction

The consumption equation is

$$
\Delta c_t=\alpha_0+\alpha_1\Delta y_t+
\alpha_2(c-y)_{t-1}+\alpha_3y_{t-1}
+\alpha_4\Delta r_t+\alpha_5r_{t-1}+v_{1t}.
$$

The expected restrictions are $\alpha_1>0$, $-1<\alpha_2<0$, $\alpha_3>\alpha_2$, and $\alpha_4,\alpha_5<0$. The negative adjustment coefficient makes excess consumption relative to the long-run relation reduce subsequent growth. The separate $\alpha_3y_{t-1}$ term allows a nonunit income elasticity. This term must be retained when mapping dynamic coefficients into the equilibrium relation; it is not another short-run income effect.

For constant real price and a stationary long-run level relation, algebra gives

$$
\log C=\text{constant}+
\left(1-\frac{\alpha_3}{\alpha_2}\right)\log Y
-\frac{\alpha_5}{\alpha_2}\log(P/D).
$$

Thus the long-run income elasticity is $1-\alpha_3/\alpha_2$ and the long-run price elasticity is $-\alpha_5/\alpha_2$. The latter is negative under the proposed sign restrictions. An income elasticity below one corresponds to negative $\alpha_3$ when $\alpha_2<0$; the sign interpretation is easy to reverse if the adjustment coefficient is ignored.

The article extends this expression to a steady growth path, writing

$$
C=k_1Y^{1-\alpha_3/\alpha_2}(P/D)^{-\alpha_5/\alpha_2},
\qquad
k_1=\exp\left[-\frac{\alpha_0}{\alpha_2}+\chi_1g_2\right],
\quad \chi_1=\frac{1-\alpha_1}{\alpha_2},
$$

where $g_2$ is income growth and relative price is held constant. The reported “income growth elasticity” is distinct from the long-run income-level elasticity. In the displayed exponential normalization, the coefficient multiplying a numerical growth rate acts as a semi-elasticity with respect to that growth-rate variable. Its numerical interpretation depends on whether growth is entered as a decimal, percentage, or transformed rate; that normalization is not fully documented in the article's underlying estimates.

There is also a consistency qualification to the stated growth-path formula. Direct substitution into the consumption equation gives an intercept term $(g_1-\alpha_1g_2-\alpha_0)/\alpha_2$, where $g_1=\Delta c$. Replacing this by the printed expression uses $g_1=g_2$. A nonunit long-run income elasticity with constant relative price normally implies a different relation between those growth rates. The static elasticities are transparent, but the growth-dependent intercept should not be treated as a universally valid balanced-growth solution without specifying the compatible path.

## Production dynamics and physical interpretation

The production equation nests differenced price terms within a level specification:

$$
\Delta q_t=\beta_0+\beta_1q_{t-1}
+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\beta_{2+k}\Delta r_{t-k}
+\gamma_3r_{t-n}+\gamma_4T+\gamma_5W_t+v_{2t}.
$$

Here $T$ represents secular technology or policy changes and $W_t$ major disturbances. Expected restrictions are $-1<\beta_1<0$, positive price effects, and positive trend and disturbance coefficients under the author's chosen coding. A weather or disease dummy can have a negative economic effect even when its coefficient convention is positive, so the definition of each disturbance is essential.

Holding the trend and disturbances at specified values, the steady-state supply relation is

$$
Q=k_2(P/D)^{-\gamma_3/\beta_1},
\qquad
k_2=\exp\left[-\frac{\beta_0+\gamma_4T+\gamma_5W}{\beta_1}\right].
$$

The long-run supply elasticity is $-\gamma_3/\beta_1>0$. This is a conditional stationary expression, not a constant level when $T$ itself keeps changing. The paper's use of differenced regressors aims to reduce collinearity without simply differencing away an underlying level relationship. Its warning about over-differencing is econometric: a difference-only model can introduce moving-average error structure and omit equilibrium information.

The coffee and cocoa delays are related to planting-to-bearing intervals. Copper's extremely long tail is related to investment and capacity adjustment. The long copper response is a model implication estimated from finite history, not forty years of independently observed experimental adjustment.

Trend terms are significant for all commodities except cotton and copper; all reported trend signs are positive. Maize and soybean productivity trends are especially large. EEC sugar production includes a quadratic trend to capture a dampening effect associated with institutional policy, and US sugar consumption includes a quadratic trend associated with high-fructose corn syrup substitution. These details make the system more than a uniform price-income regression, but they also create specification choices that need to be reproduced explicitly.

## Price expectations and stock demand

The article defines expected price using information available $m$ periods earlier. Its economic intention is

$$
P_t^e=E[P_t\mid\mathcal I_{t-m}].
$$

The empirical time-series representation is then expressed in log prices:

$$
\phi(L)p_t=\theta(L)u_t,
\qquad
\phi(L)=1-\phi_1L-\cdots-\phi_pL^p,
\quad
\theta(L)=1-\theta_1L-\cdots-\theta_qL^q.
$$

For an invertible process, an infinite autoregression can be written

$$
p_t=\omega(L)p_{t-1}+u_t,
\qquad
\omega(L)=\frac{\theta(L)-\phi(L)}{L\theta(L)}.
$$

The one-step conditional forecast replaces the innovation by its conditional mean, zero. This means the *conditional expected forecast error* is zero; it does not mean the realized error vanishes. The source's wording that the forecast error is zero should be interpreted in that conditional sense. Moreover, $E[\log P\mid\mathcal I]$ and $\log E[P\mid\mathcal I]$ are different unless an appropriate adjustment is made. The article moves between level and log expectations without presenting a retransformation correction.

The displayed generic $m$-period forecast uses the same $\omega(L)$ applied to a more distant lag. In an actual ARMA implementation, a horizon greater than one requires recursive conditional forecasting of intermediate values and innovations; simply shifting the one-step predictor is not generally correct. This matters because most stock-demand equations use expectations formed at $t-2$. Reproducing the intended rational predictor therefore requires the complete fitted ARIMA process and a precise definition of the forecast horizon.

The dynamic stock-demand equation ultimately used has the form

$$
\Delta k_t^d=\psi_0'+\psi_1\Delta q_t+
\xi_2(k^d-q)_{t-1}+\psi_2p_t^e+v_{3t}',
\qquad -1<\xi_2<0.
$$

Its long-run scale elasticity is restricted to one. At fixed expected price and a common growth path for stocks and production, the relationship is

$$
K^d=k_3Q(P^e)^{-\psi_2/\xi_2}.
$$

An additional lagged production-level term could test departure from proportionality. Lord reports that such tests support the restriction. The demand for stocks also depends on the growth rate of its scale variable through the intercept. The estimated price process is introduced first and then treated as an input in fitting inventory demand. Generated-regressor uncertainty is not reported in the article's elasticity summaries.

The formal manipulation preceding the ECM deserves care. Multiplying the static stock-demand equation by $\theta(L)$ should apply that operator to its intercept and disturbance. The printed definitions for transformed intercept and disturbance instead show inverse operators. This is an algebraic inconsistency in the exposition; the reduced ECM should not be implemented from those definitions without reconciling the actual estimation specification. Similarly, the construction is not a fully demonstrated rational-expectations equilibrium in which the structural model's implied price law necessarily equals the auxiliary ARIMA law. The author explicitly acknowledges tension between the two forecasting representations.

Table V gives expected-price elasticities of 1.1 for coffee, 1.2 for cocoa, 2.7 for soybeans, 0.3 for cotton, 0.1 for sugar, and 3.1 for copper. Maize's expected-price coefficient is not significant. All except sugar use information at $t-2$; sugar uses $t-1$. The production or consumption growth entries are respectively −3.0, 10.8, 1.6, 1.3, −0.5, −1.2, and −2.8 in the seven-commodity order. Stock-to-scale ratios evaluated at the reported average growth rates are 0.76 at 3.3%, 0.35 at 1.6%, 0.16 at 5.0%, 0.16 at 5.9%, 0.38 at 2.8%, 0.59 at 3.4%, and 0.16 at 2.9%. These ratios are conditional model evaluations, not universal desired inventory targets. Copper's inventory-demand interest-rate elasticity is −2.1.

## Integration tests, estimation, and uncertainty

The inventory argument distinguishes ordinary cointegration from multicointegration. If $Q$ and $C$ are integrated but $Q-C=\Delta K$ is stationary, production and consumption are cointegrated through accounting. If accumulated stocks are also related stationarily to one of those flows, a further stock-flow restriction exists. This second relationship is the reason for testing residuals from $K_t=a+bQ_t+u_{1t}$ and $K_t=a+bC_t+u_{2t}$.

Table III reports ADF tests for levels, stock changes, and those residuals. None of the quantity-level series rejects a unit root under the paper's stated critical values. Failure to reject is not proof of integration, especially with a short annual sample and commodity-specific breaks. The ADF statistics for stock changes are −3.77, −2.75, −5.81, −4.02, −4.75, −4.19, and −3.19. At the source's ordinary-series cutoff of −3.00, cocoa fails to reject. The author treats its proximity to the cutoff as suggestive; it remains a nonrejection at that threshold.

For stock-production residuals, statistics are −2.49, −1.90, −3.79, −2.84, −3.38, −2.96, and −2.75. For stock-consumption residuals, they are −2.54, −1.91, −3.89, −2.80, −3.51, −2.61, and −2.50. Using the stated residual-test critical value −3.29, only maize and cotton reject in both versions. Evidence for coffee, soybeans, sugar, and copper is weaker; cocoa provides little support. It would misrepresent the tables to say multicointegration is uniformly established.

The accompanying stock-flow regressions have stock-production slopes 0.36, 0.34, 0.16, 0.24, 0.29, 1.01, and 0.24. Stock-consumption slopes are 0.38, 0.25, 0.15, 0.24, 0.20, 1.00, and 0.22. High fit does not establish stationarity: sugar's two regressions have $R^2$ of 0.96 and 0.95 while residual ADF statistics remain above the rejection threshold. Durbin–Watson statistics generally give less support than the ADF tests. Coffee's stock-flow analysis starts in 1970 because the full-period relationship performed poorly, possibly reflecting changes between the 1962 and 1968 International Coffee Agreements. This subsample choice is a substantive specification decision.

The article's printed equation for testing $\Delta K$ places $K_{t-1}$ on the right side rather than $\Delta K_{t-1}$. A standard ADF regression for stationarity of $\Delta K$ would regress $\Delta^2K_t$ on lagged $\Delta K$ and lagged differences of $\Delta K$. The reported statistics can be recorded, but the printing should not be copied mechanically as an unambiguous test specification.

Equations were initially estimated by two-stage least squares. When quantity responses were noncontemporaneous and an IV-versus-OLS comparison did not find a significant difference, OLS was used for efficiency. The software was GIVE. A Chow test around the 1981 worldwide recession reportedly fails to reject parameter stability at 5% for every estimated relationship. The paper does not reproduce instrument lists, every diagnostic, all lag choices, or coefficient covariance matrices. Nonrejection of one break test does not establish invariance over arbitrary policy changes or validate forecasts decades later.

## Forecast experiment and numerical results

The evaluation consists of one-step post-sample forecasts for 1988, 1989, and 1990. Its benchmark is the same pure time-series price model used to construct expectations inside the behavioral model. Price series are generally first-difference stationary except sugar, which is modeled as stationary in levels. Coffee and cocoa have unusually long autoregressive orders, AR(11) and AR(12). Sugar and cotton include moving-average influence from innovations six periods earlier; other commodities have current and at most one-period innovation effects. Complete orders and estimated coefficients are not given for every series.

The source reports annual-average percentage changes and root-mean-square percentage errors:

| Market | Actual average change | Behavioral forecast | Time-series forecast | Behavioral RMSE | Time-series RMSE |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Coffee | −5.9 | 6.7 | 17.0 | 15.6 | 38.4 |
| Cocoa | −13.3 | −10.9 | −12.9 | 11.0 | 24.7 |
| Maize | 14.5 | 16.1 | 13.1 | 13.5 | 17.7 |
| Soybeans | 7.0 | 14.8 | 7.3 | 13.5 | 16.1 |
| Cotton | 23.4 | 23.3 | 19.2 | 15.9 | 23.7 |
| Sugar, world | 24.9 | 16.6 | 16.8 | 13.9 | 16.4 |
| Sugar, United States | 2.3 | 0.1 | −8.2 | 27.5 | 29.5 |
| Copper | 16.5 | 22.5 | 12.7 | 20.8 | 19.2 |

All values are percentages. A forecast can match the average change closely yet have substantial annual errors; cotton's average behavioral forecast of 23.3% versus 23.4% actual still accompanies RMSE of 15.9%. Conversely, the soybean ARIMA forecast is much closer to the average change but has a larger RMSE than the behavioral forecast. Average growth and squared annual prediction loss measure different things.

The behavioral-to-time-series RMSE ratio is about 0.41 for coffee and 0.45 for cocoa, versus 1.08 for copper. These ratios are simple calculations from the table, not separately reported statistical tests. Copper's loss is attributed to overpredicting the moderate 1989 price rise. The table provides neither confidence intervals nor a formal test of predictive-accuracy differences. Three annual observations are insufficient to support strong general claims about forecast superiority, especially after commodity-specific specification choices.

Crucially, actual past endogenous variables replace recursively simulated values, and actual current and past exogenous inputs are supplied. The exercise is therefore conditional forecasting with annual updating. It does not measure the error from forecasting GDP, deflators, or other contemporaneous inputs at an earlier decision date. There is no documented expanding-window parameter re-estimation rule, real-time data vintage, release calendar, or forecast-origin timestamp. Those omissions limit both a literal reproduction and an interpretation as achievable investment performance.

## Data provenance and what a replication needs

Production, consumption, and stock sources are commodity-specific: Gill and Duffus's *Cocoa Market Report*; USDA *World Coffee Situation*, *Grains*, and *Oilseeds and Products*; the World Bureau of Metal Statistics' *Metal Statistics*; International Cotton Advisory Committee *Cotton: World Statistics*; and International Sugar Organization *Sugar Yearbooks*. The appendix identifies SITC categories and price conventions but does not supply a machine-readable panel or complete estimation dates and sample counts for each equation.

Prices come from the World Bank International Trade Division. Cocoa uses an ICCO New York/London average across the nearest three future trading months. Coffee uses the ICO other-mild-arabicas indicator across New York and Bremen/Hamburg, switching from Guatemalan prime washed ex-dock New York before January 1984. Copper uses LME Grade A cathode settlement prices, preceded by high grade before July 1986. Cotton uses the A Index for middling cotton delivered to Europe, with a Mexican middling series before January 1984. Maize is US No. 2 yellow, FOB Gulf ports; soybeans are US origin, CIF Rotterdam; sugar uses the ISA Caribbean-port price, preceded before 1961 by the New York World Contract No. 4, FAS Cuba.

These are heterogeneous benchmark prices, with some futures-related components and some physical trade quotes. They are not a uniform set of tradable, continuously rolled futures excess-return series. Splicing conventions, units, calendar-year aggregation, stock measurement dates, GDP construction, deflator definition, disturbances, regional weights, and data revisions all matter. The original annual observations and the external Annex C estimates are necessary to reproduce numerical forecasts exactly.

A careful reconstruction would preserve levels for the inventory identity, estimate each reported dynamic relationship with its actual instruments and lag structure, generate price expectations at the specified horizons, solve the market-clearing condition, and evaluate only information available at each chosen origin. First reproduce the conditional three-year experiment; then separately test a genuinely real-time version with forecasted exogenous inputs and forward parameter estimation. Those latter steps are proposed validation work, not results established by this article.

For investment use, the paper offers a causal organization of commodity fundamentals and a warning against treating all supply responses as fast. It does not provide a calibrated futures return signal, position-sizing rule, hedge ratio, risk premium, or cost budget. The strongest empirical conclusion is that the specified behavioral systems achieve smaller reported conditional forecast errors than their auxiliary ARIMA models in seven of eight market rows over 1988–1990. Extending that conclusion to deployable alpha requires additional data, timing discipline, and an explicit mapping from price forecasts to traded contracts.
