# Estimating Probabilities of Default for Low Default Portfolios

Katja Pluto and Dirk Tasche, manuscript dated July 28, 2005, Deutsche Bundesbank affiliations. [Source PDF](../../Finance/LowRiskEstimation_Pluto2005.pdf). All 20 pages, including the numerical propositions in Appendix A and additional scaling tables in Appendix B, were read. Page references below are PDF pages, identical to the manuscript pagination. The Basel references describe the paper's historical motivation, not a statement of current regulatory requirements.

## Why this matters

A rating grade with no observed defaults does not have a known zero default probability. For a small portfolio, a zero count can be consistent with economically substantial risk. Conversely, estimating every grade separately discards information in the assumed ordering of credit quality. Pluto and Tasche combine this ordering with one-sided confidence bounds: estimate a grade using its own observations and those of every worse grade, under an equal-risk configuration that is favorable to observing few defaults.

The contribution is a simple, implementable calibration principle rather than a new default-prediction model. It requires obligor and default counts, an externally justified grade ordering, a confidence level, and, for correlated observations, a specified dependence model. It does not require a complete migration history or some defaults in the worst grade. It therefore addresses a case where migration-based estimation can still run out of information.

Three distinctions determine whether an implementation is defensible. A confidence bound is not a posterior mean or a best estimate under a stated loss function. Uncertainty about a probability differs from adverse realizations generated by that probability. Finally, scaling the bounds to a portfolio target changes their statistical meaning. The paper discusses these issues, but its abstract's monotonicity language and some later labels are stronger than the construction warrants once defaults and scaling are introduced.

## Statistical target and the ordered pooling construction

Number grades from best to worst, $j=1,\ldots,J$. Let $n_j$ be the number of obligors observed at the start of a common one-year horizon, $d_j$ their default count, and $p_j$ the unknown grade default probability. The maintained ordering is

$$
p_1\le p_2\le\cdots\le p_J.
$$

For grade $j$, form the suffix totals

$$
N_j=\sum_{\ell=j}^{J}n_\ell,\qquad D_j=\sum_{\ell=j}^{J}d_\ell.
$$

The paper's most prudent construction sets the suffix probabilities equal to the candidate value $p_j$ when evaluating the lower tail of the default count. Earlier, better grades are omitted: their probabilities may be smaller than $p_j$ and cannot be treated as additional observations with a probability bounded below by $p_j$.

There is a useful precise interpretation of the equal-probability step. Under independent defaults and the asserted ordering, every Bernoulli probability in the suffix is at least $p_j$. Couple the indicators using independent uniforms. Replacing all suffix probabilities by $p_j$ can only reduce their total count and increase the probability of observing at most $D_j$ defaults. Thus the homogeneous binomial calculation is favorable to accepting a large candidate $p_j$. It does not assert that the actual grades have equal risks, and it does not estimate their relative risk ratios.

For a chosen confidence level $\gamma$, retain candidate probabilities satisfying

$$
B_{N_j,D_j}(p_j)\equiv
\sum_{k=0}^{D_j}\binom{N_j}{k}p_j^k(1-p_j)^{N_j-k}\ge1-\gamma.
$$

The upper endpoint $U_j$ solves equality when $0\le D_j<N_j$. Values above it make the observed count unusually low under the candidate probability. This is inversion of a lower-tail test into a one-sided upper confidence bound. The discrete construction is conservative in its frequentist coverage; $\gamma$ is not the conditional probability that the realized interval contains the parameter.

The suffix coupling also explains why the bound remains conservative for the individual grade when suffix risks are heterogeneous but ordered and independent. The observed suffix count is stochastically larger than the equal-risk count, and the inverted endpoint increases with the observed count. This is a pointwise grade guarantee. The paper does not construct a simultaneous confidence region covering all grade probabilities with probability $\gamma$. The overlapping suffix samples make the estimates dependent, and separate coverage statements should not be relabeled joint coverage.

The method is not maximum likelihood over an ordered vector and not isotonic regression. Its objective is the largest candidate probability retained by a specified test and pooling rule. Choosing a confidence level is consequently a risk-policy decision. The authors suggest moderate levels but supply neither an optimal statistical loss function nor an empirical calibration of that choice.

## Independent defaults: formulas and reproducible numbers

With zero observed defaults throughout a suffix, the endpoint is explicit:

$$
U_j=1-(1-\gamma)^{1/N_j}.
$$

For large $N_j$ and a small endpoint, $U_j\approx-\log(1-\gamma)/N_j$. This is a local approximation, not an alternative formula to use at arbitrary probabilities. Numerically, evaluate the exact expression with `-expm1(log1p(-gamma)/N)` to avoid cancellation.

The paper uses three grades with $(n_A,n_B,n_C)=(100,400,300)$. The applicable suffix sizes are therefore 800, 700, and 300. Tables 1-3 give the following percentages:

| Confidence | A | B | C |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 50% | 0.09% | 0.10% | 0.23% |
| 75% | 0.17% | 0.20% | 0.46% |
| 90% | 0.29% | 0.33% | 0.76% |
| 95% | 0.37% | 0.43% | 0.99% |
| 99% | 0.57% | 0.66% | 1.52% |
| 99.9% | 0.86% | 0.98% | 2.28% |

These are probabilities, not percentage losses or credit spreads. The greater endpoint for a worse grade reflects the smaller usable suffix, even though every default count is zero. The data do not identify a numerical spacing of economic risk among the grades. The ordering and sample structure produce the displayed separation.

For nonzero counts, Appendix A gives the beta representation. If $D<N$,

$$
B_{N,D}(p)=1-I_p(D+1,N-D),\qquad
U=I^{-1}_{\gamma}(D+1,N-D),
$$

where $I_p(a,b)$ is the regularized beta CDF and the inverse is with respect to $p$. The source writes its beta identity for $D\le N$, but the ordinary beta distribution requires $D<N$; if all obligors default, the one-sided upper endpoint is simply one.

The uniqueness proof is directly implementable:

$$
\frac{dB_{N,D}(p)}{dp}
=-(N-D)\binom ND p^D(1-p)^{N-D-1}<0.
$$

The function goes from one to zero, so a unique root exists for $0<\gamma<1$. Appendix A's bracket, using $v=1-\gamma$, is

$$
1-v^{1/N}\le U\le(1-v)^{1/N}.
$$

Bisection on $[0,1]$ also works. A library binomial CDF or incomplete beta implementation is preferable to summing large binomial coefficients at extreme sample sizes.

The second illustration observes $(d_A,d_B,d_C)=(0,2,1)$. Its suffix counts are 3, 3, and 1. At 95% confidence, Tables 4-6 report endpoints of 0.97%, 1.10%, and 1.57%; direct inversion gives 0.9663309%, 1.1039092%, and 1.5714555%. At 50%, the corresponding exact values are 0.4588148%, 0.5243284%, and 0.5588170%. At 99.9%, the printed values are 1.62%, 1.85%, and 3.04%.

Defaults outside grade A affect its bound even though its own count is zero. At 75%, treating A alone would give 1.3767%, compared with 0.6378366% from the suffix construction. Table 4 prints 0.65% for the latter, rather than the 0.64% obtained by rounding the stated binomial equation. This small discrepancy was checked against the page image and a direct numerical root; it is not an official correction by the authors.

The absence of monotonicity is more consequential. With zero defaults in every grade, decreasing suffix sample sizes guarantee ordered endpoints. With some defaults, removing a better grade can remove enough defaults to lower the endpoint of the worse grade. For the paper's own footnote-5 variant, $(d_A,d_B,d_C)=(0,2,0)$, at 75% confidence the B suffix gives 0.5592895% while C alone gives 0.4610321%. Thus $U_B>U_C$ despite the maintained ordering of the true probabilities. The authors acknowledge this on pp. 6 and 16 and suggest, as a conservative workaround, adding hypothetical defaults to C. Such editing is a calibration intervention; it must be disclosed separately from observed data. A monotone display cannot be taken as evidence validating the ranking, and a nonmonotone display alone does not prove the ranking wrong.

## Correlated defaults: conditional binomial mixtures

The dependence extension uses a Gaussian asset-factor model. For obligor $i$,

$$
V_i=\sqrt\rho S+\sqrt{1-\rho}\,\xi_i,
\qquad S,\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_N\sim N(0,1),
$$

with independent idiosyncratic factors and default when $V_i\le\Phi^{-1}(p)$. Conditional on $S=s$, the default probability is

$$
G(p,\rho,s)=\Phi\left(\frac{\Phi^{-1}(p)-\sqrt\rho s}{\sqrt{1-\rho}}\right).
$$

The parameter $\rho$ is correlation of latent asset variables. It is not correlation of the observed default indicators. Given the factor, the suffix count is binomial; unconditionally it is a mixture. Replace the previous CDF by

$$
H_{N,D}(p;\rho)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
\phi(s)B_{N,D}\bigl(G(p,\rho,s)\bigr)\,ds,
$$

and solve $H_{N,D}(U;\rho)=1-\gamma$. Since $G$ increases with $p$ and the binomial lower tail decreases, the root is unique for $D<N$. The equal-risk suffix argument continues to work within a common factor/loading construction with ordered thresholds: conditionally couple the individual indicators before integrating over the factor.

For zero defaults, Appendix A proves that correlation raises the endpoint relative to independence. Write $Z=G(p,\rho,S)$, so $E[Z]=p$. Convexity gives

$$
H_{N,0}(p;\rho)=E[(1-Z)^N]\ge(1-p)^N.
$$

Because the left side decreases in $p$, it reaches the same tail probability at an equal or larger $p$. This proof concerns zero defaults. It does not establish every monotonicity assertion one might wish to make about arbitrary lower-tail counts or dependence specifications. The plus sign on the factor in Appendix A, compared with the minus sign in the main text, is harmless under the symmetric normal integration measure.

The illustration sets $\rho=0.12$, motivated by the historical Basel corporate correlation range. This is an assumed input, not an estimate from 800 obligors. Table 7's zero-default results are:

| Confidence | A | B | C |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 50% | 0.15% | 0.17% | 0.37% |
| 75% | 0.40% | 0.45% | 0.92% |
| 90% | 0.86% | 0.96% | 1.89% |
| 95% | 1.31% | 1.45% | 2.78% |
| 99% | 2.65% | 2.92% | 5.30% |
| 99.9% | 5.29% | 5.77% | 9.84% |

For A at 99.9%, the correlated endpoint is about six times the independent 0.86%. Intuitively, a good realization of the shared factor can generate no defaults across many names even when the unconditional PD is materially positive. More names do not provide the same independent information as they would without the common factor.

With counts $(0,2,1)$, Table 8 reports A/B/C values of 0.72%/0.81%/0.84% at 50%, 3.42%/3.77%/4.41% at 95%, and 10.08%/10.92%/13.14% at 99.9%. A separate numerical evaluation of the stated mixture, using 160-node Gauss-Hermite quadrature and bisection, gives 0.71055%/0.80057%/0.83517% at 50% and 3.41208%/3.76531%/4.40776% at 95%. Small differences from the printed tables are therefore present; the tables should be treated as approximate illustrations rather than exact regression-test targets. For A's 99.9% few-default endpoint, 80 and 160 quadrature nodes both give approximately 10.0753562%, agreeing to substantially better than the printed precision.

The paper used R for its harder numerical calculations but does not provide code or integration tolerances. A reproducible implementation should fix the quadrature scheme, tolerance, and inversion criterion. Increasing a confidence level under independence is not an established replacement for modeling correlation: the paper explicitly leaves the necessary mapping unresolved. Its de Finetti footnote also does not make the Gaussian probit specification assumption-free. Exchangeability or a mixture representation alone does not identify a normal factor, common loadings, or the value 0.12.

## Scaling: useful calibration, changed coverage meaning

Let $\bar U=\sum_jn_jU_j/\sum_jn_j$ and choose a portfolio target $p_*$. The scaling proposal is

$$
K=\frac{p_*}{\bar U},\qquad \widetilde p_j=KU_j.
$$

This guarantees the obligor-count-weighted average $\sum_jn_j\widetilde p_j/\sum_jn_j=p_*$. These are name weights, not exposure or loss-given-default weights. Positive multiplication preserves an ordering already present; it does not repair a nonmonotone unscaled vector.

The first target is the observed overall default rate, $3/800=0.375\%$. Table 9 reports, at 95% confidence in the independent case, $K=0.30$ and scaled A/B/C probabilities 0.29%/0.33%/0.47%. At 99.9%, they are 0.27%/0.31%/0.50% with $K=0.17$. Raising confidence can lower the scaled best-grade PD because the target average is fixed while the shape changes. In the correlated case, Table 10 at 95% gives $K=0.09$ and 0.32%/0.36%/0.42%. The confidence level now helps allocate a fixed portfolio target rather than preserving its original test-inversion meaning.

The paper acknowledges that scaling to the observed rate removes the overall conservatism. It also fails usefully in a zero-default portfolio: a zero target would drive every estimate to zero. The preferred variant uses the upper endpoint for the entire homogeneous pooled portfolio as $p_*$. Under the same assumptions and confidence level used for the suffix calculations, this numerical target equals $U_A$.

For the few-default independent example, Table 11 gives a 95% portfolio target of 0.97%, $K=0.77$, and scaled probabilities 0.74%/0.84%/1.22%. Table 12's correlated example gives a 3.42% target, $K=0.86$, and 2.95%/3.25%/3.80%. Table 12 prints 0.71% for the 50% portfolio target whereas Table 8 prints 0.72%; the direct mixture calculation above is closer to 0.71%. This is another reason to preserve formulas and full-precision intermediate values rather than copy rounded tables into code.

The scaled grade values are not automatically upper confidence bounds with the original coverage, despite that label in several captions. Multiplying a valid endpoint by a factor below one can exclude parameters that the original confidence set includes. Nor is the homogeneous pooled calculation a complete proof of confidence coverage for an arbitrary heterogeneous portfolio average. The method offers a calibration shape and a target, not a theorem restoring all grade-level guarantees after scaling.

Appendix B applies this second variant to zero defaults. Table 16's correlated 95% example reports a 1.31% portfolio target, $K=0.68$, and 0.89%/0.98%/1.89%. Table 15's independent 75% column is inconsistent with the stated equations in a way that exceeds normal display rounding: it prints $K=0.66$ and 0.11%/0.13%/0.24%. Using the exact zero-count endpoints and the stated weights gives

$$
K=0.58999987,
\qquad
(\widetilde p_A,\widetilde p_B,\widetilde p_C)
=(0.10215065\%,0.11672916\%,0.27200887\%).
$$

Their weighted average is the exact pooled endpoint 0.17313674%. These are independently derived values, checked against Eq. (5.1), not an official amended table. Compute the whole scaling stage from unrounded values; do not mix the manuscript's rounded $K$ and PD rows.

## Multi-year cohorts and temporal dependence

Section 6 follows the names present at the beginning of a fixed $T$-year interval. Later entrants are excluded, original grades define the suffixes, and each name can default only once. The latent process is

$$
V_{i,t}=\sqrt\rho S_t+\sqrt{1-\rho}\,\xi_{i,t},
\qquad \operatorname{Corr}(S_s,S_t)=\vartheta^{|s-t|}.
$$

The systematic vector is jointly Gaussian; idiosyncratic variables are independent across names and years and independent of that vector. The default time is the first threshold crossing, $\tau_i=\min\{t:V_{i,t}\le\Phi^{-1}(p)\}$. This is a cohort model with absorbing default, not a binomial sample of $NT$ independently replaceable names.

Given a systematic path, the probability that a name defaults at least once is

$$
\pi(p;S_1,\ldots,S_T)
=1-\prod_{t=1}^{T}[1-G(p,\rho,S_t)].
$$

The cohort lower-tail probability is therefore

$$
H_{N,D,T}(p)=E\left[B_{N,D}\bigl(\pi(p;S_1,\ldots,S_T)\bigr)\right].
$$

Invert $H_{N,D,T}(U)=1-\gamma$ separately for each original-grade suffix. The paper evaluates the $T$-dimensional expectation by Monte Carlo but does not give simulation counts, seeds, or error bars. One implementable reconstruction generates a stationary Gaussian AR(1), $S_1\sim N(0,1)$ and $S_t=\vartheta S_{t-1}+\sqrt{1-\vartheta^2}\epsilon_t$. Use the same simulated paths at every trial probability in the root search; then the estimated lower tail remains monotone, avoiding artificial root noise from resampling. Increase path count and report integration uncertainty before claiming reproduction to a hundredth of a percentage point.

For small conditional probabilities, compute the survival product through the sum of `log1p(-G)` and recover $\pi$ with `-expm1`. A conditional binomial CDF integrates out idiosyncratic defaults and is more efficient than simulating every obligor. The independent benchmark is useful: with $\rho=0$, $\pi=1-(1-p)^T$, so the cohort bound is $p=1-(1-U_{N,D})^{1/T}$. For zero defaults this becomes $1-(1-\gamma)^{1/(NT)}$; with defaults, simply replacing $N$ by $NT$ in a binomial count is a different experiment.

The paper chooses $T=5$, $\rho=0.12$, and $\vartheta=0.3$. The factor correlation four years apart is $0.3^4=0.0081$, or 0.81%. These choices are illustrative, not fitted. For distinct years, the latent asset correlation is $\rho\vartheta^{|s-t|}$, whereas contemporaneous asset correlation between different names is $\rho$. Neither quantity should be confused with a binary-default correlation.

For A with no defaults at 95%, the five-year 0.16% contrasts with one-year 1.31% under the same cross-sectional correlation. The comparison holds total defaults fixed while increasing time at risk; it is not a claim that pooling arbitrary historical snapshots produces the same improvement.

Two source qualifications matter for this extension. First, Eq. (6.7)'s intermediate line compares a normal idiosyncratic variable with $G$, which is a probability. Its threshold should instead be $(\Phi^{-1}(p)-\sqrt\rho S_t)/\sqrt{1-\rho}$. The final survival-product expression is correct. This is a dimensional and algebraic correction verified on p. 13.

Second, Eq. (6.3) defines $p=P(V_{i,t}\le c)$ unconditionally, while its accompanying prose describes a probability among survivors. Under positive temporal dependence these differ. For two years, if $J=P(V_{i,1}\le c,V_{i,2}\le c)$, then

$$
P(\tau_i=2\mid\tau_i>1)=\frac{p-J}{1-p}.
$$

Positive Gaussian dependence makes $J>p^2$, so the survivor hazard is below $p$. With $p=1\%$ and asset correlation $0.12\times0.3=0.036$, independent Gaussian quadrature gives $J\approx0.0001281723$ and a second-year survivor hazard of 0.9971543%. The discrepancy is small in this illustration but conceptually real. The estimated parameter is the stationary latent one-period marginal threshold probability; it is not a constant conditional hazard. The footnote about implicit rating migration likewise does not supply observed migration states or an estimable transition matrix.

## What an actual deployment still requires

Every numerical exercise in this paper uses the stylized 800-name portfolio. There is no issuer dataset, out-of-sample validation, measured rating discrimination, realized loss distribution, trading cost model, or portfolio-return experiment. The essential inputs in practice are a consistent default definition, starting cohorts, exposure windows, treatment of withdrawals and missing status, grade histories, and evidence that the asserted ordering is meaningful. Unequal exposure sizes affect capital and expected loss but do not enter these name-count PD formulas.

Dependence parameters, time homogeneity, and the confidence level cannot be estimated reliably merely by having observed few defaults. Their uncertainty remains outside the displayed bounds. A common-factor model with known correlation can produce a valid conditional-on-model confidence construction while still materially understating uncertainty about model choice. Stressing the factor distribution and raising the statistical confidence level address different sources of risk.

The authors discourage applying this method only to isolated low-default grades within an otherwise data-rich system, because combining upper endpoints with average-rate estimates creates a break in interpretation. They also do not propose these high endpoints as powerful backtesting thresholds: a plausible central PD would often lie comfortably below them. The unresolved transition from low-default to ordinary estimation, selection of confidence level, and treatment of apparent rank violations remain open decisions on pp. 15-16.

A useful implementation therefore retains three objects separately: the unscaled pointwise endpoints, the calibrated PD curve after any scaling or monotonicity adjustment, and the portfolio target with its own provenance. Record all assumptions and transformations with each curve. That preserves the paper's contribution, quantifying what sparse observations can exclude under stated assumptions, without converting a calibration convention into stronger evidence than the data provide.
