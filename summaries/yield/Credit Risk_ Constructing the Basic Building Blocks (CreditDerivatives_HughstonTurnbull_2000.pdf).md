# Credit Risk: Constructing the Basic Building Blocks

**Lane P. Hughston and Stuart M. Turnbull (2001).** *Economic Notes* 30(2), 281–292. Source: [CreditDerivatives_HughstonTurnbull_2000.pdf](../../Finance/CreditDerivatives_HughstonTurnbull_2000.pdf). The filename's year is misleading: this is the 2001 article, not the authors' separately cited 2000 *Risk* article, “Credit Derivatives Made Simple.” All 12 PDF pages, including references, are covered. The source contains theoretical valuation formulas, without an empirical dataset, numerical calibration, or appendix.

## Why this matters

The article provides a compact way to price three economically different cash flows: a payment conditional on survival, a stream that stops at default, and a payment triggered by default. Separating these components prevents common implementation mistakes, especially treating a default recovery as if it were a promised maturity payment or charging a credit spread without accounting for how utilization changes near default.

The central pricing device is an effective discount factor containing the short rate plus the risk-neutral default intensity. A default payment also carries an intensity multiplier outside the discount factor. The first adjustment accounts for survival until the payment date; the second converts a payment conditional on default into an unconditional expected cash-flow rate. Omitting either changes the contract being valued.

The contribution is principally application and exposition. The authors credit Lando's earlier work for the building blocks and explicitly identify their coupon-bond result as equivalent to a Jarrow–Turnbull expression. Their useful extensions here are compact accrued-interest accounting, a funded revolver cash-flow decomposition, and several collateral or guarantee examples. The paper does not estimate a default model, solve the borrower's utilization decision, or demonstrate profitable trading. Its value is as a transparent specification for a pricing implementation.

## Probability model, information, and units

Use $\tau$ for default time and $J_t=\mathbf1\{\tau>t\}$ for survival. The market filtration is $\mathcal F_t=\mathcal G_t\vee\mathcal H_t$, where $\mathcal G_t$ is generated by a multidimensional Brownian motion and $\mathcal H_t$ records default history. Let $r_t$ be the short rate and $h_t\ge0$ the default intensity. The money-market account is $B_t=\exp(\int_0^t r_s\,ds)$, initialized at one. All valuation expectations below are under a specified risk-neutral measure $Q$.

Rates and intensities have units of inverse time. If time is measured in years, a 2% annual intensity is $h=.02$, not 2. A payment rate $Y_s$ has units of currency per year; a recovery amount $Z_s$ has units of currency. This distinction explains why the default integrand contains $Z_sh_s$ whereas the continuous-payment integrand contains $Y_s$.

The source presents pathwise survival probabilities, implicitly conditional on the Brownian environment. With random intensity, the unconditional survival probability is not simply the exponential of a random integral. A sufficient precise construction for the formulas is the Cox model

$$
\tau=\inf\left\{t:\int_0^t h_s\,ds\ge E\right\},
\qquad E\sim\operatorname{Exp}(1),
$$

where $E$ is independent of the Brownian environment and $h$ is a nonnegative adapted, locally integrable process. Conditional on the environment path, survival from $t$ to $s$ is $\exp(-\int_t^s h_u\,du)$, given survival at $t$. The conditional default density at $s$ is that survival factor times $h_s$. Averaging over future environment paths gives the remaining conditional expectation in the pricing formulas.

This explicit construction is a sufficient mathematical specification supplied here, rather than a separately estimated mechanism in the article. Merely stating that a default time has an intensity and that the intensity is Brownian adapted does not, without the relevant conditional-survival and information assumptions, justify every exponential-survival identity for arbitrary enlarged filtrations. In particular, knowing $\mathcal G_t$ reveals past and current intensity, not its future path. The path-conditioning step and the subsequent expectation over future paths must remain distinct.

Use $\widetilde D(t,s)=\exp[-\int_t^s(r_u+h_u)\,du]$. This is a pathwise effective discount factor, not generally a traded default-free bond price. Stochastic interest rates, intensities, recovery, and collateral can be dependent. Nothing in the single-name formulas permits replacing an expectation of their product by a product of separate expectations.

## Three pricing operators and their derivation

For a claim paying one at $T$ only if the borrower survives, risk-neutral pricing gives $B_tE^Q[J_T/B_T\mid\mathcal F_t]$. Under the conditional-survival construction, the value becomes

$$
P^0(t,T)=J_tE^Q[\widetilde D(t,T)\mid\mathcal F_t].
$$

This is source equation (8). At maturity its value is $J_T$; after default its remaining ex-dividend value is zero. With deterministic constant $r,h$, it is $J_te^{-(r+h)(T-t)}$. The spread equals $h$ in this zero-recovery case, not loss given default times intensity because loss given default is one.

For a continuous cash-flow rate $Y_s$ paid until default or $T$, whichever comes first,

$$
C_t=J_tE^Q\left[\int_t^T Y_s\widetilde D(t,s)\,ds\mid\mathcal F_t\right].
$$

This is source equation (10). It follows by writing the discounted payment as $Y_sJ_sB_t/B_s\,ds$, conditioning on the environment, and interchanging the time integral and expectation. Nonnegative payments permit Tonelli's theorem; signed payments require an appropriate integrability condition. The formula includes the final infinitesimal payment only while the borrower is alive and contains no recovery after default.

For a payment $Z_\tau$ at default before $T$,

$$
D_t=J_tE^Q\left[\int_t^T Z_sh_s\widetilde D(t,s)\,ds\mid\mathcal F_t\right].
$$

This is the operational content of source equation (12). Conditional default probability over $[s,s+ds]$ supplies $h_s\exp(-\int_t^sh_u\,du)ds$, while money-market discounting supplies $\exp(-\int_t^sr_u\,du)$. Their product produces the effective factor.

For direct implementation, $Y_s$ and $Z_s$ should be specified as suitable pre-default environment processes, or as the appropriate conditional projections of event-time cash flows. The source describes $Z_s$ as $\mathcal F_s$-measurable, but measurability alone does not specify a default-specific jump or an additional random recovery mark. If recovery is known only after a default shock, its conditional marked-default expectation must be supplied. Likewise, the raw event-time notation in the source is understood as a future unpaid cash flow, $\mathbf1\{t<\tau\le T\}Z_\tau$, rather than a payment that has already occurred. These qualifications preserve the intended ex-dividend valuation.

Together, the operators price a terminal survival payment $X_T$, a survival stream, and a default payoff by summing their corresponding expectations. They do not impose the same recovery rule on all three components. This is why contract decomposition should precede any attempt to read a spread from a yield curve.

## Coupon bonds with recovery of principal and accrued interest

Let $N$ be principal, $T_1<\cdots<T_n$ remaining coupon dates, and $cN$ the cash coupon on each date. Here $c$ is the coupon per payment period, not automatically an annual rate. The terminal survival payment includes both the final coupon and principal. Let $\delta_s$ be the fraction of the current coupon period accrued at time $s$, with contract-specific day-count conventions.

At default, the claim recognized by the contract is $N(1+c\delta_\tau)$. If effective recovery is $R_\tau$, the value received at default is $N(1+c\delta_\tau)R_\tau$. The source's example is a semiannual coupon with default one month after the previous coupon, giving $\delta_\tau=1/6$. Using $1/12$ without changing the meaning of $c$ would halve the accrued coupon erroneously.

The complete pre-default dirty value is

$$
V_t=N J_tE^Q\left[
\widetilde D(t,T_n)+c\sum_{i:T_i>t}\widetilde D(t,T_i)
+\int_t^{T_n}(1+c\delta_s)R_sh_s\widetilde D(t,s)\,ds
\mid\mathcal F_t\right].
$$

This combines source equations (13)–(17). At a coupon date, use a clearly specified before-payment or after-payment convention; the displayed remaining-date sum is after any time-$t$ payment. A quoted clean price also requires the applicable accrued-interest subtraction. The paper does not prescribe a settlement calendar or market quotation convention.

The recovery fraction is effective value at default. It may represent the present value, at that time, of later bankruptcy distributions or restructured debt. Therefore delayed recovery is not ignored economically, but compressed into $R_\tau$. Discounting the same delay again would double count it. Conversely, entering an eventual undiscounted recovery fraction when the model expects present value would overstate the default payoff.

The recovery integral is additive because recovery is of a contractually specified claim. It is not generally equivalent to pricing all promised coupons at a single discount rate $r+h(1-R)$. That latter simplification belongs to a different recovery structure, such as fractional recovery of market value under the appropriate assumptions. Recovery of par plus accrued interest has a different maturity and coupon dependence.

For deterministic coefficients, ordinary quadrature over each coupon interval gives an exact numerical implementation up to integration error. Split the integral at coupon dates because $\delta_s$ resets. With stochastic coefficients, retain the joint paths and average pathwise integrals; substituting average intensity or recovery before exponentiating loses dependence and convexity effects.

## Revolver economics and the funding cancellation

A revolver authorizes lending up to $N$ until $T$. The borrower controls utilization $\phi_t\in[0,1]$, so outstanding principal is $N\phi_t$. It pays interest continuously at $r_t+g_t$, where $g_t$ is the contractual spread. At default the bank recovers $N\phi_\tau R_\tau$. Define loss given default $L_t=1-R_t$.

The bank's discounted inflows consist of interest $(r_s+g_s)N\phi_s$ while alive and recovery $N\phi_sR_s$ at default. Its economic outflows consist of funding interest $r_sN\phi_s$ while alive and the outstanding amount $N\phi_s$ lost from the funded principal at default. Subtracting these four terms cancels the risk-free funding-interest component and yields source equation (25):

$$
V_t=N J_tE^Q\left[
\int_t^T\phi_s\bigl(g_s-h_sL_s\bigr)
\widetilde D(t,s)\,ds\mid\mathcal F_t\right].
$$

This is the facility's net value relative to funding, not the gross market value of a loan asset with its outstanding principal included again. The daily renewal interpretation in the source explains why one should not separately subtract every drawdown and then also retain the entire principal-at-default cost without carefully reconciling cash flows.

If $g_s\ge h_sL_s$ state by state, the value is nonnegative for nonnegative utilization. A strictly positive margin on a set with positive expected utilized duration gives positive value. The pointwise break-even floating rule is $g_s=h_sL_s$. It is a sufficient way to make every integrand vanish; it is not necessary for an initially fair fixed-spread contract.

For a constant contractual spread $g$, the corresponding fair level is the derived ratio

$$
g^*_t=
\frac{E^Q[\int_t^T\phi_sh_sL_s\widetilde D(t,s)\,ds\mid\mathcal F_t]}
{E^Q[\int_t^T\phi_s\widetilde D(t,s)\,ds\mid\mathcal F_t]},
$$

provided the denominator is positive and utilization is specified independently of the choice being solved for. It is an exposure- and discount-weighted expected loss rate. If the utilization rule changes with the quoted spread, both numerator and denominator depend on $g$, and this becomes an implicit fixed-point pricing problem. The paper does not solve that behavioral problem.

An unconditional average intensity times an unconditional average loss fraction generally misses wrong-way exposure. Borrowers may draw more precisely when intensity and loss given default are high. The source explicitly allows utilization to depend on intensity and warns against independently specifying utilization and credit quality. Its statement that maximum utilization maximizes bank value presumes a positive net margin and fixed other inputs; with negative margin, more utilization increases the loss.

The simplified bank funds at the money-market rate, pays no commitment liquidity cost on undrawn capacity, and absorbs relevant fees into the spread on drawn balances. Adding a commitment fee on $N(1-\phi_s)$ or a funding spread requires an additional cash-flow term. A zero-utilization path generates zero value in the displayed model even though a real commitment can consume liquidity and capital. That is a scope restriction, not evidence that unused commitments are costless.

## Collateral is a contingent default payoff

Suppose the bank receives an asset with effective realizable value $S_\tau$ if the obligor defaults before $T$. The collateral component alone is

$$
K_t=J_tE^Q\left[\int_t^TS_sh_s\widetilde D(t,s)\,ds\mid\mathcal F_t\right].
$$

This is source equation (27). Effective value should include delivery delays, legal costs, and realization costs already valued at default. If the pledged asset is the obligor's own equity, its value at obligor default may be negligible. A pre-default equity mark cannot simply be substituted for its value after the default event.

The formula pays the full stated $S_\tau$; it does not automatically cap recovery at outstanding debt or net it against another recovery claim. If the contract gives the lender only $\min(S_\tau,\text{amount owed})$, or applies collateral before an unsecured recovery waterfall, that actual payoff must replace $S_\tau$. The paper labels collateral an option, but does not formulate an optimal exercise problem: payment is triggered by default under the specified contract.

A risk-free guarantee of principal is the special case $S_s=N$:

$$
K_t^{\rm safe}=NJ_tE^Q\left[\int_t^Th_s\widetilde D(t,s)\,ds\mid\mathcal F_t\right].
$$

It pays principal at default, not at the original maturity, and is therefore not generally the difference between a default-free maturity bond and a zero-recovery risky maturity bond. That difference has a different payment date unless the recovery cash is carried appropriately to maturity.

The source suggests that deteriorating conditions can combine higher intensity, lower survival, and lower collateral value into an adverse effect. The economically relevant wrong-way exposure is real, but monotonicity of the collateral option's present value does not follow from those signs alone. Higher intensity also increases the chance that the collateral payoff is triggered. Even with constant positive collateral, zero interest, and no guarantor risk, its value is $S(1-e^{-h(T-t)})$, increasing in $h$. The effect on the entire loan and on loss risk must be distinguished from the value of the default-contingent collateral component.

## A defaultable parent guarantee

Let $\tau_P$ and $\tau_S$ be parent and subsidiary default times, with intensities $\eta_t$ and $h_t$. Define survival indicators $J_t^P$ and $J_t^S$. The guarantee pays $N$ on subsidiary default only if the parent remains alive. The source assumes conditional independence of the default clocks given the Brownian environment. Their intensities can still be correlated through that environment, so this is weaker than unconditional independence of default times.

Conditional on the common environment, subsidiary default at $s$ has density $h_s\exp(-\int_t^sh_u\,du)$, while the parent survives to $s$ with probability $\exp(-\int_t^s\eta_u\,du)$. Hence

$$
K_t^{\rm parent}=NJ_t^SJ_t^P E^Q\left[
\int_t^T h_s\exp\left(-\int_t^s(r_u+h_u+\eta_u)\,du\right)ds
\mid\mathcal F_t\right].
$$

This is source equation (31), written with consistent subsidiary notation. The article switches between its earlier single-obligor symbol and its subsidiary symbol in the intervening calculation, and refers to an equation (32) that is absent. Those are notation/cross-reference slips; the surviving-parent density derivation identifies the intended result.

The hazard sum prices the event “subsidiary defaults first and before maturity.” It is not a complete model of joint-default losses. Independent Cox clocks with continuous integrated intensities exclude simultaneous default conditional on the environment. A common-shock default mechanism, parent contagion, partial payment after parent default, or guarantee recovery would require additional states and cash flows. The article's case where both entities default and no payment is made does not justify ignoring the order of the two events when the parent initially survives.

## Reproduction checks and missing calibration inputs

A practical implementation should first use deterministic constant coefficients. Put $A=r+h$ and $\Delta=T-t$. The zero-recovery unit bond is $e^{-A\Delta}$, a constant survival payment rate $Y$ is worth $Y(1-e^{-A\Delta})/A$, and a constant default payment $Z$ is worth $Zh(1-e^{-A\Delta})/A$. Use the continuous limit $\Delta$ for the quotient when $A=0$.

The revolver then has value

$$
V_t=N\phi(g-hL)\frac{1-e^{-(r+h)\Delta}}{r+h},
$$

conditional on current survival. The defaultable guarantee has value

$$
K_t^{\rm parent}=Nh\frac{1-e^{-(r+h+\eta)\Delta}}{r+h+\eta},
$$

conditional on both entities being alive. These are derived numerical checks, not calibrated findings reported by the paper. They verify discounting, the location of the hazard multiplier, fee cancellation, and the additional parent-survival factor without requiring simulation.

Limiting checks are informative. Setting $h=0$ removes all default-triggered payments and leaves default-free promised cash flows. Setting $\eta=0$ recovers the safe-parent guarantee. As the maturity tends to zero, each integrated payment value tends to zero at its instantaneous payment rate. Setting $g=hL$ makes the constant-coefficient revolver exactly fair. Setting recovery to zero removes the bond's recovery integral; setting recovery to one does not generally turn a coupon bond into its default-free counterpart, because default accelerates the recognized principal and accrued-interest claim and extinguishes future coupons.

For a stochastic implementation, generate jointly specified paths of rates, intensities, recovery, utilization, and collateral. Accumulate effective discount factors on each time grid, sum survival coupon payments, and integrate default or continuous cash-flow terms. The intensity representation can avoid simulating a separate default time when pricing expectations; simulating Cox clocks is an independent verification route and is needed for a full realized-loss distribution. Refine the time grid around coupon resets and high-intensity periods, compare the two valuation routes, and report Monte Carlo uncertainty separately from model uncertainty.

The source supplies no risk-neutral parameter estimates, dependence matrix, utilization function, collateral process, recovery distribution, term structures, market quotes, or numerical examples. It also leaves funding spreads, liquidity reserves, capital charges, settlement rules, guarantee enforceability, and implementation discretization unspecified. Exact formulas are therefore available, but a numerical market valuation cannot be reproduced from the paper alone. Historical default frequencies cannot fill the risk-neutral intensity input without a pricing-measure specification.

These limitations locate the useful result precisely. The article provides a consistent cash-flow language for credit contracts and identifies how utilization and collateral dependence enter value. It does not make recovery conventions interchangeable, eliminate the need to model borrower behavior, or convert risk-neutral valuation directly into physical expected loss, VaR, or economic capital.
