# A Hidden Markov Model of Momentum
**Authors:** Kent Daniel, Ravi Jagannathan, Soohun Kim
**Year:** 2019
**Journal/Venue:** Working paper (draft of February 17, 2019)
**Source file:** Finance/MomentumMarkovModel_DanielJagannathan.pdf

## Question

Momentum (US, value-weighted 12-2 winner-minus-loser deciles with NYSE breakpoints, 1927:01-2017:12, 1,092 months) earns 1.17%/month (SD 7.80%, annual SR 0.52; CAPM alpha 1.53%/month, t = 7.43; FF3 alpha 1.75%, t = 8.63) but has skewness -2.34 and kurtosis 20.43: eight months below -30%, none above +30%, worst month -77.02%. The paper asks whether this tail is explained by a time-varying *written call on the market* embedded in the short (loser) leg, and whether a latent-state model of that optionality forecasts crashes better than volatility or past-return signals.

## Mechanism and motivating evidence

- **Merton (1974) leverage story.** After prolonged market declines loser stocks are highly levered (financially and operationally); their equity becomes an at/out-of-the-money call on firm assets. A market rebound produces outsized loser gains, and WML, being short losers, crashes.
- **Crash anatomy (Table 2, 12 months with MOM < -20%).** Losers earned +49.33% on average vs winners +8.79%; market excess return +18.12%. The market had fallen more than 34% over the prior 36 months, realized vol was about 3x normal, and the loser breakpoint averaged -64.88% (about 2.7x its average). In the five post-1964 crashes, loser book debt/market equity averaged 15.39 vs an unconditional 1.07.
- **Henriksson-Merton regression**, sorted by prior 36-month market return (top/middle/bottom 20/60/20%):
$$R_{p,t} = \alpha_p + \beta_{0p} R^e_{MKT,t} + \beta^+_p \max(R^e_{MKT,t},0) + \varepsilon_{p,t}.$$
$\beta^+$ is significant only in the "Low" (depressed) group: loser-leg $\beta^+ = 0.66$ ($t = 3.34$); MOM adj. $R^2$ is 49% there vs 6% and 5% in the other groups; 10 of the 12 crashes fall in this group; controlling for the option term cuts MOM skewness from -2.26 to -0.91. Similar results when sorting on 12-month realized vol or the loser breakpoint. Note that $\alpha_p$ here is not an abnormal return, because the option payoff is not an excess return.
- **Leverage within losers (Table 4, post-1964).** High-BD/ME losers have $\beta^+ = 0.40$ vs 0.03 for low-leverage losers (difference $t = 1.95$). Financial leverage is an incomplete proxy: the 2001:01 (-42.03%) and 2002:12 (-20.12%) dot-com crashes happened with loser leverage of only 0.70 and 0.97, but high operating leverage.
- **Structural simulation.** 1,000 firms with Merton equity, a common plus idiosyncratic GBM asset shock, and partial leverage adjustment toward a target $(L/E)_0$ (AR(1) speed $\rho$ calibrated to 751 S&P 500 firms, 1964-2018). This reproduces the pattern: MOM $\beta^+$ is -0.34 in "depressed" months vs -0.17 otherwise. An HMM fitted to the simulated data separates the states more sharply ($\beta^+$ -0.51 vs -0.15), and 86% of the simulated worst-1% months fall in the top 30% of the filtered turbulent probability. Crashes arise with rational pricing and no frictions.

## Model

A two-state (Calm/Turbulent) HMM, a dynamic Henriksson-Merton / downside-risk CAPM (Lettau et al., 2014):
$$R_{MOM,t} = \alpha(S_t) + \beta_0(S_t) R^e_{MKT,t} + \beta^+(S_t)\max(R^e_{MKT,t},0) + \sigma_{MOM}(S_t)\,\varepsilon_{MOM,t}$$
$$R^e_{MKT,t} = \mu(S_t) + \sigma_{MKT}(S_t)\,\varepsilon_{MKT,t}$$
The state follows a first-order Markov chain; there are 14 parameters. Estimation is by exact ML with the Hamilton (1989) filter, initialized at the stationary distribution. The output is the one-step-ahead probability $\Pr(S_t = T \mid J_{t-1})$.

**Distributional choice matters.** Appendix A shows that QML can be *inconsistent* for HMMs under a misspecified likelihood, so the residual distributions are chosen by matching simulated unconditional moments. Only $\varepsilon_{MOM} \sim N(0,1)$ with $\varepsilon_{MKT} \sim t_5$ puts all four sample moments of MOM inside the 95% bands of 10,000 simulated 1,092-month paths. So the huge MOM skew and kurtosis are generated by the state-dependent option exposure, not by fat-tailed MOM shocks.

**Full-sample estimates (Table 9, monthly %):**

| | Calm | Turbulent |
|---|---|---|
| $\alpha$ | 1.86 (t 7.17) | 3.62 (t 3.52) |
| $\beta_0$ | 0.32 | -0.40 |
| $\beta^+$ | -0.41 | -1.07 (t -4.48) |
| $\sigma_{MOM}$ | 4.42 | 10.68 |
| $\mu_{MKT}$ | 1.13 | -0.50 (n.s.) |
| $\sigma_{MKT}$ | 4.01 | 8.33 |
| Persistence | 0.99 | 0.95 |

In the turbulent state the up-market beta is $-0.40 - 1.07 = -1.47$ vs a down-market beta of -0.40. The calm-state market has 13.6%/yr mean and 13.9%/yr vol (SR 0.98); the turbulent state has -6.0% and 28.9%.

## Crash forecasting

- **Coverage (Table 12).** $\Pr(T \mid J_{t-1}) > 80\%$ in only 156 of 1,092 months, yet all 12 losses worse than -20% occur there; MOM averages -0.86%/month in those months. Large *gains* are less concentrated: 8 of 12 gains above +20% fall there, and 2 occur with probability below 30%. The regime is high-variance with negative skew, not uniformly bad.
- **Out of sample.** Parameters are re-estimated with data only through $t-1$ and starting in 1937 (so 1929-32 is excluded), with forecasts from 1984:09. The large losses still line up with high filtered probabilities. The $t_5$ market distribution was chosen on the full sample, so this is not fully out of sample.
- **False positives (Table 13).** Threshold = the lowest signal value observed across crash months; count non-crash months above it. For -20% crashes: HMM 124; HMM with $\beta^+ \equiv 0$ 163 (about 30% more, so the convexity term is what adds information); best momentum-RV signal (6m) 210; market GARCH 183; market RV(3m) 164; past market returns much worse. Conditioning on the HMM probability, these alternative signals add nothing (Figure 3).
- **Leverage vs HMM (Table 11, 1964-2017).** All 5 crashes are in the top-20% turbulent-probability bucket; 2 of them (dot-com) are not in the top-20% financial-leverage bucket.

## Implementation results

- **Simple exit rule (Table 14, full sample).** Exit when $\Pr(T)$ exceeds the level that captures all -20% crashes: mean 1.31%, SD 5.09%, SR 0.89 (vs 0.52), CAPM appraisal ratio 0.92. This rule is **beaten** by plain vol scaling: $k/\sigma$ gives SR 0.96 (AR 1.05) and $k/\sigma^2$ gives SR 1.07 (AR 1.10). The authors state this explicitly. The threshold is also chosen with hindsight.
- **Option-adjusted alpha (Section 4).** Under an SDF that prices the riskless asset, the market and a one-month ATM S&P call, and is orthogonal to MOM's residual (Assumption 1), project MOM on $(1, R^e_{MKT}, \max(R^e_{MKT},0))$ using the state-mixture moments to get
$$\alpha^*_{t-1} = \alpha_{t-1} + (1+R_{f,t})\,\beta^+_{t-1} V_{c,t-1},$$
where $V_c$ is the call price. Out of sample over 1996:01-2016:04 (244 months), the average $\alpha^*$ is 1.37%/month; it is significantly positive in 209 months and negative in 7, significantly so only in 2008:12 and 2009:03. It is also negative in 1998:10 and late 2008 to early 2009. No loss worse than -20% occurs in a significant-alpha month (0 of 5; $\chi^2 = 29.5$).
- **Timing on $\alpha^*$ (Table 16, 1996-2016).** Hold MOM only when $\alpha^*$ is significant, else T-bills: 16.49%/yr, SD 22.60%, SR 0.73, skew 0.17. Switching into the market instead gives SR 0.75. Always-on MOM: 9.23%/yr, SD 31.72%, SR 0.29, skew -1.31.
- **Residual check (Table 17).** $\epsilon_{MOM}$ is unrelated to most factors (MKT, SMB, RMW, CMA, I/A, liquidity, BAB, spreads, VIX, jump measures) but loads on HML ($t = -4.40$) and ROE ($t = 3.34$, $R^2$ 13.8%). Over 1996-2016 the Hou-Xue-Zhang q-factor alpha of MOM is only 0.15%/month (n.s.), vs an FF3 alpha of 1.28% ($t = 2.22$).

## Assessment

- **Convincing:** The crash mechanism is well documented (loser-leg convexity, leverage and breakpoint evidence, structural simulation). The ablation with $\beta^+ \equiv 0$ is a clean demonstration that the option term, not just the volatility regime, carries the crash signal. It is notable that Gaussian MOM residuals suffice once the state-dependent convexity is modeled.
- **Fragile:** Only 12 (full-sample) or 5 (post-1964) crash events drive the forecasting evidence. The false-positive metric is set by the single worst-scoring crash month and the exit threshold is chosen ex post. The turbulent regime also contains most of the large gains, so the exit rule gives up upside. The option-adjusted alpha rests on Assumption 1 and a partial-equilibrium SDF, and the residual's HML/ROE loadings show that the assumption is not fully satisfied. There are minor internal inconsistencies (the conclusion quotes 224 months and 1.36% vs 244 months and 1.37% in the text).
- **Practical:** For a PM the headline is sobering: a simple continuous vol-scaling overlay (Barroso and Santa-Clara; Daniel and Moskowitz) beats the HMM binary exit on Sharpe. The HMM's value is as a *tail-specific* diagnostic with fewer false alarms, plus an option-priced view of when the momentum premium turns negative. A natural use is to combine the turbulent probability (or $\beta^+$-implied convexity) with vol scaling, or to hedge the embedded short call with index calls when $\Pr(T)$ is high, rather than switching the strategy on and off.
